## Case 21: Patanjali Ayurved / Baba Ramdev — Misleading Advertising Contempt Case

**Company:** Patanjali Ayurved Ltd.
**Industry:** FMCG / Ayurvedic Pharma & Consumer Goods
**Year:** 2022–2024 (litigation), peak crisis Nov 2023–Aug 2024
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory non-compliance, misleading advertising, court contempt

**Trigger Event:** The Indian Medical Association (IMA) filed a 2022 writ petition accusing Patanjali of running a systematic disinformation campaign against modern medicine, including ads claiming Ayurvedic products could "permanently cure" diseases like diabetes, asthma, and high blood pressure. Patanjali gave the Supreme Court a written undertaking on 21 November 2023 to stop these ads — then continued running similar ones anyway.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to Delayed/Reactive under court pressure

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
Patanjali did not stop the ads after its own court undertaking. Baba Ramdev held a press conference on 8 May 2024 — a day *after* being pulled up by the Supreme Court — reasserting that Patanjali's medical claims were true and blaming allopathy practitioners, rather than de-escalating. The company only filed newspaper apologies after the Court explicitly rejected earlier "unqualified apologies" as insufficient in April 2024.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5
Apologies were tendered but the Court found them non-credible on more than one occasion, rejecting affidavits and raising the possibility of perjury proceedings over allegedly forged air-ticket documents submitted to justify non-appearance. The company continued the challenged conduct after promising to stop, which is the core transparency failure.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Contempt of Court proceedings (Supreme Court of India); Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954, Sections 3 and 4; Uttarakhand State Licensing Authority suspended licenses for 14 Patanjali products; Ministry of AYUSH scrutiny; Consumer Protection Act advertising provisions referenced by the Court.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
Contempt charges against Baba Ramdev and Acharya Balkrishna were dropped in August 2024 after the Supreme Court accepted their apology as sincere and noted corrective steps taken. However, the underlying advertising-practices scrutiny was widened by the Court to cover the FMCG sector generally, and the reputational association between Patanjali and "misleading health claims" persists in public discourse.

**What Went Right:**
- Once the Supreme Court rejected the first apology and signalled contempt/perjury risk, Patanjali did publish newspaper apologies and complied with license suspensions.
- Balkrishna and Ramdev personally appeared in court rather than sending representatives, which the Court noted favourably later in proceedings.
- The eventual, more contrite compliance posture (post-May 2024) is what led to contempt charges being dropped by August 2024.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Breached its own written undertaking to the Supreme Court within weeks — publishing ads in *The Hindu* on 4 December 2023, less than two weeks after the 21 November 2023 undertaking.
- Baba Ramdev's 8 May 2024 press conference doubled down on the disputed claims one day after a Court rebuke — a textbook example of a spokesperson message clashing with the legal position, undermining the apology strategy simultaneously being pursued.
- First-round apology was widely seen (and the Court itself questioned) as performative — asking pointedly whether the apology ad was "as big and expensive" as Patanjali's usual front-page product ads.
- Allegations of forged travel documents submitted with affidavits compounded the credibility problem — moving the story from "misleading ads" to "misleading the Court."

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Once a written undertaking is given to a court, every subsequent public communication — press conferences, ad placements, social posts — must be pre-cleared against that undertaking before publication. A crisis-comms team should have flagged the 8 May 2024 press conference language against the pending contempt notice and prevented the founder from re-asserting the very claims under litigation. A single, sincere, cost-equivalent apology in the same media placements as the original ads (not buried, smaller insertions) at the first compliance deadline would have resolved this in one cycle instead of six court hearings over nine months.

**Estimated Impact:**
- *Financial:* 14 product licenses suspended by Uttarakhand FDA; recurring legal costs across a nine-month multi-hearing Supreme Court process.
- *Reputational:* Sustained negative national press coverage from November 2023 to August 2024; Supreme Court's characterization of the conduct as a "blatant violation" of undertakings became a widely quoted media narrative; expanded judicial scrutiny to the wider FMCG advertising sector, keeping Patanjali in the news cycle as the origin case.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown / ORM
Directly relevant to advising clients on the risk of spokesperson messaging contradicting a legal position mid-crisis, and on how court-mandated apologies must be executed with matching prominence/placement to the original offending content to be seen as credible (both by the court and by the public/search narrative).

**Key Sources:** Supreme Court Observer; SCC Online (Bar and Bench affiliate); Supreme Court of India judgment 2024 INSC 605 (Suo Motu Contempt Petition (Civil) No. 4 of 2024); Gulf News; Deccan Herald / News on Air coverage of hearing dates.

## Case 22: Paytm Payments Bank — RBI Regulatory Restrictions

**Company:** Paytm Payments Bank Ltd. (PPBL), subsidiary of One97 Communications Ltd. (Paytm)
**Industry:** Fintech / Digital Banking
**Year:** 2024 (with regulatory history from 2021–2022)
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory shutdown, compliance failure, investor confidence collapse

**Trigger Event:** On 31 January 2024, the Reserve Bank of India ordered Paytm Payments Bank to stop accepting new deposits, top-ups, and most banking services after March 15, 2024, citing "persistent non-compliances and continued material supervisory concerns" found by an external auditor — this after RBI had already barred PPBL from onboarding new customers back in March 2022 and fined it ₹5.39 crore for KYC violations in October 2023.

**Response Type:** Reactive
Paytm did not preempt the shutdown despite two years of escalating RBI warnings (2022 onboarding ban → 2023 penalty → 2024 full restriction). The company's public response came only after the RBI notification was already issued.

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
Founder/CEO Vijay Shekhar Sharma issued a reassurance statement fairly promptly after the 31 January order, saying the company would continue operations and was in discussions with RBI about a bank partnership model. However, this was reactive damage control rather than a pre-empted disclosure, and it arrived alongside a stock that had already gone into consecutive lower-circuit declines.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5
Paytm did not conceal the RBI order — parent-company disclosures and CEO statements followed standard listed-company norms — but the company did not proactively disclose the severity of the underlying compliance gaps flagged by the external auditor before the RBI acted, so the market was blindsided. This is a "compliant but not forthcoming" pattern rather than active concealment.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (RBI's supervisory-action power); RBI KYC/AML Master Directions; SEBI listed-company disclosure norms (as One97 Communications is publicly traded).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
PPBL effectively ceased core banking operations by mid-March 2024, and its nodal accounts were shut down. Paytm restructured its business model around third-party bank partnerships (e.g., for UPI and wallet services) to keep its consumer-facing app functioning, but Paytm Payments Bank as a licensed banking entity did not return to its prior scope.

**What Went Right:**
- Paytm complied with RBI's deadlines (customer fund access preserved, nodal accounts wound down by 15 March 2024) rather than contesting the order publicly, which avoided a harder regulatory confrontation.
- CEO Sharma made a direct, named public statement rather than routing all communication through PR statements, which gave the market a face and a voice during the crash.
- The company moved relatively quickly to explore bank-partnership alternatives to preserve the wallet and payments business, limiting total platform shutdown.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Two years of prior RBI warnings (March 2022 onboarding ban, October 2023 penalty) were treated as manageable regulatory friction rather than existential risk signals — no visible remediation communicated to investors in that window.
- The market found out the scale of the compliance failure at the same time as the punishment, not before — One97 Communications' stock fell nearly 20% in back-to-back sessions and lost roughly ₹20,500 crore in market value within days, and continued sliding roughly 50–55% over the following weeks.
- Denial/minimization of associated news — reports of a potential Jio Financial Services acquisition of the wallet business were publicly refuted by both parties, adding a layer of confusing, contradictory signal to an already volatile news cycle.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Given a documented two-year regulatory escalation ladder (2022 → 2023 → 2024), the company should have run investor and public messaging in parallel with each escalation step, not only after the final restriction. A materiality disclosure explaining concrete remediation milestones after the October 2023 penalty would have primed the market and softened the shock of the January 2024 order. Once the order hit, a same-day (not next-day) leadership statement with a specific operational continuity plan — not just a reassurance of intent — would have reduced the multi-day cascading stock decline.

**Estimated Impact:**
- *Financial:* One97 Communications' market capitalization fell by more than ₹20,500 crore within the first days of the order; shares hit 20% lower circuits on multiple consecutive sessions; stock declined roughly 50–55% from pre-announcement levels within weeks.
- *Reputational:* Sustained negative fintech-sector coverage; PPBL's core banking license effectively became non-functional; the episode became a reference case in Indian fintech regulatory-compliance discourse.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms
A strong reference case for advising fintech and regulated-sector clients: shows the reputational cost of treating sequential regulator warnings as isolated incidents rather than a pattern requiring proactive public disclosure, and how founder-led reactive statements — while useful — cannot substitute for pre-crisis investor communication.

**Key Sources:** Reserve Bank of India press release (Ref. 2023-2024/1895, 16 February 2024); Deccan Herald; Business Standard; Outlook Business; AMLEGALS regulatory analysis.

## Case 23: Go First (GoAir) — Insolvency Filing and Collapse

**Company:** Go Airlines (India) Ltd. ("Go First"), Wadia Group
**Industry:** Aviation
**Year:** 2023 (filing), liquidation ordered January 2025
**Crisis Type:** Operational collapse, insolvency, mass passenger disruption

**Trigger Event:** On 2 May 2023, Go First abruptly cancelled all flights for 3–5 May, then filed for voluntary insolvency resolution under Section 10 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, blaming Pratt & Whitney for failing to supply serviceable replacement engines, which had grounded roughly 50% of its Airbus A320neo fleet.

**Response Type:** Reactive, bordering on Delayed
The engine-supply problem had been building since 2019 (7% of fleet grounded) to 50% grounded by late 2022/early 2023, but the airline's public-facing cancellation notices were same-day or next-day, giving ticketed passengers almost no advance warning.

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
The DGCA issued a show-cause notice to Go First within 24 hours of the flight suspensions and demanded a response in the same window, indicating the regulator itself judged the airline's own advance communication to passengers as inadequate. Go First did do next-day-style cancellation notices ("we regret to inform...") but repeatedly extended the cancellation window in short increments (3–5 May, then to May 9, then May 12, eventually into August 2023), creating a rolling disruption rather than a single clear communication of the true scale of the problem.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5
Go First was relatively open about blaming Pratt & Whitney publicly and detailed its arbitration claims (an emergency arbitrator had ordered P&W to deliver 10 spare engines by 27 April 2023, which the airline said didn't happen). This specific, named accountability was more transparent than most airline PR crises. However, the rolling, short-window cancellation extensions functioned as a drip-feed of bad news rather than one clear disclosure of the actual timeline, which eroded passenger and market trust incrementally.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, Section 10 (voluntary corporate insolvency); DGCA show-cause notice and refund directives; Delhi High Court rulings on aircraft deregistration and lessor repossession rights; NCLT Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) and eventual liquidation order under Section 33(2)/34 IBC.

**Resolution Status:** Fatal
After nearly 20 months in CIRP with no viable resolution plan or buyer, the Committee of Creditors voted unanimously (100%) for liquidation in July 2024, and the NCLT formally ordered liquidation on 21 January 2025, ending the airline's 17-year operating history. Debts totalled roughly ₹11,000+ crore across lenders, lessors, vendors, and unpaid customer refunds (~₹500 crore owed to customers).

**What Went Right:**
- Go First named its cause of crisis specifically and publicly (Pratt & Whitney engine failures, backed by an emergency arbitration ruling) rather than issuing vague "operational reasons" language alone — this gave media and regulators a concrete, checkable narrative.
- The company pursued international arbitration in parallel with domestic insolvency proceedings, showing an active (not passive) legal strategy.
- DGCA-mandated refund process was acknowledged and the airline committed publicly to processing refunds to original payment methods.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The airline let fleet availability decline from 96% (FY2015) to 54% (FY2023) without any comparably scaled public disclosure of the risk to operations — passengers had no advance signal before the abrupt May 2023 shutdown.
- Repeated short-window cancellation extensions (three days, then to May 9, then May 12, then into August) meant thousands of customers rebooked into a service that kept collapsing again, compounding reputational damage with each extension rather than absorbing it once.
- The "regret to inform... operational reasons" boilerplate language used repeatedly across cancellation notices under-communicated the true severity (a company in formal insolvency proceedings) to the flying public.
- Legal disputes with lessors over aircraft repossession dragged on for nearly two years, during which the brand remained visibly unresolved in the news cycle rather than fading.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
When fleet grounding crosses a threshold that materially threatens schedule reliability (well before the 50% mark), airlines should issue proactive, specific public disclosure — not wait for a same-day shutdown. Cancellation communications should state a realistic outside-date range up front rather than rolling short extensions, which is what actually damaged trust here. Once insolvency was filed, faster, more specific customer communication (e.g., "flights suspended for X weeks minimum while resolution process proceeds" rather than repeated 3–5 day increments) would have reduced the repeated rebooking-and-cancellation cycle that generated the worst passenger sentiment.

**Estimated Impact:**
- *Financial:* Total creditor claims of roughly ₹11,463 crore at filing; ~₹65.22 billion (₹6,522 crore) owed to primary lenders at liquidation; ~₹500 crore in unrefunded customer dues; DGCA estimated ~₹350 crore in refund liability from the initial cancellation wave alone.
- *Reputational:* Complete loss of brand — airline ceased to exist as a going concern; 17-year operating history ended in liquidation; became a reference case in Indian aviation for engine-supply-chain risk and insolvency mishandling.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM
Useful for advising any client in a supply-chain-dependent operational business: shows the reputational cost of rolling, short-window bad-news disclosures versus one clear, longer-horizon disclosure, and how public regulator show-cause notices become part of the permanent search/media record even years later.

**Key Sources:** BBC News; Business Today; Deccan Herald; India TV News; ch-aviation; SCC Online (NCLT liquidation order coverage); Tribune India.

## Case 24: SpiceJet — Repeated Safety, Financial, and Regulatory Crises

**Company:** SpiceJet Ltd.
**Industry:** Aviation
**Year:** Recurring, 2022–2025 (illustrative peak: July 2022 DGCA capacity restriction)
**Crisis Type:** Recurring operational/safety incidents compounded by financial distress and regulatory sanction

**Trigger Event:** Following at least eight technical malfunction incidents in 18 days (19 June–6 July 2022) — including engine and system failures that forced aircraft to turn back or land with degraded safety margins — the DGCA issued a show-cause notice on 6 July 2022 and then, on 27 July 2022, ordered SpiceJet to cut scheduled departures to 50% of its approved summer-schedule capacity for eight weeks.

**Response Type:** Reactive, recurring pattern (Delayed on underlying fixes)
CMD Ajay Singh responded publicly within roughly a day of the show-cause notice, but the same underlying pattern — technical snags tied to deferred maintenance and cash-flow stress — resurfaced repeatedly in subsequent years (pilot training lapses in April 2022, the July 2022 capacity cut, and continued grounded-aircraft/salary-delay reporting into 2025), indicating the core financial and maintenance issues were never durably fixed.

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
Public statements followed regulatory action within about a day each time, which looks fast on paper — but because the same category of problem (financial strain → deferred maintenance/spares shortage → safety incidents) recurred across multiple years, the underlying corrective response was not fast or durable. Repeated recurrence lowers the effective score even where individual PR responses were prompt.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5
CMD Ajay Singh's public framing after the July 2022 notice — that "a lot of these incidents being reported are relatively minor in nature and happen to every airline" — minimized a regulator's explicit written finding of "poor internal safety oversight and inadequate maintenance actions." The DGCA's own notice separately disclosed that a September 2021 financial assessment had already found the airline operating on a "cash and carry" basis with vendors unpaid and spare parts in shortage — a structural financial-safety linkage the company had not proactively disclosed to the public itself.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** DGCA show-cause notices (April 2022 pilot-training suspension of 90 B737 MAX pilots; July 2022 technical-incident notice); DGCA capacity restriction order (50% of approved summer-schedule departures for 8 weeks, effective 27 July 2022); ongoing DGCA supervisory audits; company-law and labour-law exposure from later reported salary delays.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
SpiceJet resumed full scheduled capacity after the 8-week restriction period in 2022 and has continued operating, but underlying financial fragility persisted: reporting from 2025 showed only 18 of 53 aircraft active, continued salary delays for senior staff, and DGCA maintaining close oversight, alongside disclosure of an interest-free advance to the CMD that drew criticism given concurrent staff pay delays.

**What Went Right:**
- CMD personally and promptly addressed the media after the show-cause notice rather than remaining silent, which kept the story from reading as evasive.
- The airline avoided a full grounding or license revocation by complying with the DGCA's capacity-restriction order rather than contesting it.
- More recent reporting notes SpiceJet has avoided Level 1 DGCA audit findings for over a year, suggesting some genuine compliance improvement on the audit-paperwork side.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Minimizing language ("relatively minor... happens to every airline") directly contradicted the regulator's own written finding of "poor internal safety oversight," creating a credibility gap between company messaging and the public regulatory record.
- The financial root cause (unpaid vendors, spares shortages) had been flagged by DGCA's own assessment nearly a year before the 2022 capacity cut, yet no visible structural fix (recapitalization, vendor payment plan, fleet reduction to match maintainable capacity) was communicated at the time.
- The same category of issue — financial strain producing operational/safety-adjacent problems — resurfaced in later years (2025 reporting on salary delays and grounded aircraft), suggesting reactive per-incident PR without a durable structural narrative of recovery.
- The disclosed CMD advance amid staff salary delays created an easily-quotable "optics" story that compounded reputational damage independent of the original safety issue.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
When a regulator's own notice explicitly links safety incidents to a documented financial root cause, the company's public response should acknowledge and address that root cause directly (e.g., a funding/recapitalization plan, vendor payment timeline) rather than characterizing individual incidents as "minor." A recurring-incident airline should publish a visible, dated remediation roadmap after the first serious regulatory notice, so that subsequent incidents can be measured against stated progress instead of each one restarting the reputational damage cycle from zero. Executive compensation actions (such as advances to leadership) should be timed and communicated with visible awareness of concurrent employee financial hardship to avoid unforced optics damage.

**Estimated Impact:**
- *Financial:* Share price fell roughly 42–43% over 2022 amid the crisis sequence; reported net losses in preceding years (₹316 crore FY2018-19, ₹934 crore FY2019-20, ₹998 crore FY2020-21) underline the structural financial fragility behind the safety issues; continued fleet under-utilization (only ~34% of fleet active per 2025 reporting).
- *Reputational:* Recurring negative headlines across multiple years rather than a single contained episode; DGCA oversight explicitly framed as "close" and ongoing; became a recurring reference point in Indian aviation-safety and airline-financial-distress commentary.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM
A clear case for advising clients on the compounding reputational risk of recurring incidents: a single well-handled crisis response does not prevent damage if the same category of problem resurfaces, because each recurrence is read by media and search audiences as evidence the previous "fix" wasn't real. Also illustrates the risk of spokesperson language that visibly contradicts a regulator's own written record.

**Key Sources:** Deccan Herald; Tribune India; Gulf News; Outlook Business; Aerospace Global News (2025 reporting); DGCA show-cause notice text (July 2022) as reported by Deccan Herald and Daily Pioneer.
