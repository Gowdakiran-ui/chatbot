## Case 005

**Company:** Kingfisher Airlines / Vijay Mallya (United Breweries Group)
**Industry:** Aviation
**Year:** 2012 (collapse) – 2016 (fugitive status) – ongoing
**Crisis Type:** Loan Default / Fraud / Fugitive Economic Offence

**Trigger Event:** Kingfisher Airlines, which had not turned a profit in its 8 years of operation, defaulted on employee salaries and lender repayments through 2011–12; SBI declared the airline's account a non-performing asset in January 2012, and the airline was fully grounded by October 2012 after mounting losses and staff strikes over unpaid wages, with its flying licence cancelled that December.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to Delayed-Reactive once legal proceedings began

**Response Speed Score:** 1
*Mallya's public posture through 2012–2015 was largely one of downplaying the airline's troubles — attributing non-payment of salaries to "reported losses" while continuing a visible lifestyle (Formula 1, IPL ownership) and, in 2013, seeing Diageo pay roughly ₹6,500 crore for a stake in United Spirits with no proceeds reaching Kingfisher's lenders or unpaid staff. There was no proactive restructuring plan, no timely public accounting of where borrowed funds went, and no engagement with creditors that produced results — banks were left declaring the accounts NPA and, eventually, Mallya a "wilful defaulter" (2013) unilaterally.*

**Transparency Score:** 1
*ED investigations later found ₹3,432 crore was allegedly diverted through over-invoicing of aircraft lease rentals between 2008–2012, including ₹45.42 crore used to pay for Mallya's personal corporate jet lease — funds diverted while the airline was defaulting on employee salaries and lender repayments. Mallya left India on 2 March 2016, using a diplomatic passport (as a sitting Rajya Sabha MP at the time) just as the Supreme Court was hearing bank petitions against him, which was widely interpreted as flight to avoid accountability. He has since publicly disputed the "fled" framing, but the timing — departure coinciding almost exactly with escalating legal exposure — undermines that framing.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** CBI case (2015) over a ₹900–950 crore IDBI Bank loan, charging fraud and criminal conspiracy; non-bailable warrant issued; Enforcement Directorate case (2016) under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), including asset attachment in India and abroad; India–UK Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty invoked; passport revoked (2016); declared a Fugitive Economic Offender in 2019 under the newly enacted Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 (a law substantially motivated by this and the PNB case); ongoing extradition proceedings in the UK, where a court approved extradition in 2018 but Mallya has remained in the UK on unresolved legal grounds since.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved
*Kingfisher Airlines itself is defunct and delisted. Banks have recovered a portion of dues through asset sales (including from Mallya's attached UK and Indian assets) but Mallya himself remains in the UK, has not been extradited despite a 2018 UK court approval, and criminal trials in India remain pending — a special court issued a fresh non-bailable warrant against him as recently as July 2024.*

**What Went Right:**
- Once fraud allegations solidified, the CBI and ED moved with reasonable coordination — CBI raided offices and homes in October 2015, ED filed PMLA charges in 2016 — showing agencies acting on hard evidence rather than only political pressure.
- India secured a UK court ruling approving Mallya's extradition (December 2018), demonstrating that cross-border legal pursuit of the case was substantively successful even though physical extradition has not followed.
- The case became the direct legislative trigger for the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018, closing a real gap (no prior law allowed asset confiscation from someone who fled before conviction).

**What Went Wrong:**
- Banks continued extending credit and rolling over exposure to Kingfisher for years after clear signs of financial distress (negative net worth, missed salary payments as early as 2009–2011) without demanding transparency on fund usage.
- There was no equivalent of a corporate crisis-communications response at any point — no restructuring plan shared with employees, creditors, or the public; the silence itself became the story and let speculation and anger fill the vacuum, especially around unpaid staff wages while Mallya's personal lifestyle remained highly visible.
- Mallya was allowed to leave the country on a diplomatic passport while a Supreme Court matter involving him was active, exposing a coordination failure between agencies (aviation/immigration, judiciary, investigative bodies) that should have prevented departure once red flags were clear.
- Personal guarantees given by Mallya were not converted into effective recovery action until years after default, by which time asset values and enforceability had eroded.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Lenders should have demanded an independent forensic audit of fund usage the moment the airline began missing statutory payments (service tax, salaries) rather than continuing to extend and roll over credit for years. A travel/passport flag tied to active bank NPA classification and pending investigations — not just a formal criminal charge — would have prevented departure; this gap was itself a lesson later reflected in tighter look-out-circular practices in subsequent high-value default cases. For any company facing public wage or repayment defaults, a crisis-comms team should treat silence as the worst option — even an unfavorable but honest restructuring update is more reputation-preserving than none at all, as the vacuum here fed years of "flamboyant defaulter" media framing that outlasted the company itself.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Kingfisher's consortium debt exceeded ₹9,000 crore (~$1.2–1.4 billion including accrued interest); banks have recovered only a fraction through asset sales over the following decade. Reputational: Mallya became the most recognizable face of India's "wilful defaulter" and "fled tycoon" narrative, directly shaping public and political pressure that led to new fugitive-offender legislation; the case remains a reference point in Indian banking-sector NPA discourse.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown (a core cautionary case for what happens to personal and corporate reputation when a company response is pure silence for years, followed by outright departure)

## Case 006

**Company:** Coffee Day Enterprises Ltd. (Café Coffee Day) / V.G. Siddhartha
**Industry:** Food & Beverage / Retail / Diversified Holding Group
**Year:** 2019
**Crisis Type:** Founder Death / Debt Crisis / Governance Disclosure Failure

**Trigger Event:** On 29 July 2019, CCD founder-chairman V.G. Siddhartha went missing from a bridge over the Netravati river near Mangaluru; his body was found roughly 36 hours later. A letter purportedly signed by him and addressed to the company board, which surfaced on social media before the company's own confirmation, described a "serious liquidity crunch" and pressure from lenders and an unnamed private equity investor.

**Response Type:** Reactive, but relatively fast for a founder-death crisis

**Response Speed Score:** 3
*The company confirmed Siddhartha's disappearance to stock exchanges on 30 July, the day after he went missing, and confirmed the authenticity of the leaked letter via a BSE filing shortly after it began circulating — both within roughly 24–48 hours, meeting the disclosure-speed bar even though the news itself (a leaked personal letter) initially got ahead of the company. However, a clear, board-verified account of total group debt was not provided until 17 August 2019 — nearly three weeks after the crisis broke — when CDEL first gave lenders a consolidated debt figure (₹4,970 crore).*

**Transparency Score:** 3
*The company did confirm the letter's authenticity rather than deny or suppress it, which is a genuinely transparent move in a highly sensitive situation. But the underlying financial picture was murky for weeks: reported group debt figures varied significantly across sources (₹6,547 crore for listed CDEL alone as of March 2019, versus later estimates of total group exposure, including personal guarantees and pledges, reaching ₹11,000 crore) — suggesting the full debt structure across the promoter's private holding companies had not been transparently disclosed even internally before the crisis.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** SEBI disclosure norms (listed company obligations under LODR Regulations) for material event disclosure; subsequent debt-default disclosures to exchanges; eventual insolvency-related proceedings — lenders considered referring the group's flagship entity to the NCLT after further loan defaults were disclosed in 2020–2021.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
*Siddhartha's wife, Malavika Hegde, took over as CEO and undertook an asset-sale-led deleveraging plan (including a September 2019 deal with Blackstone and Salarpuria Sattva for the Global Village Tech Park, which cut group debt from ₹4,970 crore toward roughly ₹2,400 crore). Despite this, CDEL continued to report loan defaults in subsequent quarters (including a ₹489 crore default disclosed in January 2020 and further defaults into 2021), and lenders considered NCLT referral. The core retail café business itself survived and was later sold/restructured, but full debt resolution took years.*

**What Went Right:**
- The company did not deny or attempt to suppress the leaked letter — it confirmed authenticity via a formal stock exchange filing, which under the circumstances built more credibility than a denial-then-forced-admission sequence would have.
- New leadership (Malavika Hegde) communicated directly and promptly with the 25,000-person employee base, committing to a clear deleveraging plan rather than remaining silent during a period of maximum uncertainty.
- The subsequent asset-sale strategy (Global Village Tech Park divestment) was disclosed to markets with specific, verifiable numbers, which helped rebuild some creditor and investor confidence over the following months.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The true scale and structure of group debt — spread across the listed entity, four private promoter-group holding companies, and personal guarantees — was not transparent to markets before the crisis, meaning investors and even some lenders were working from incomplete information for years.
- It took nearly three weeks post-crisis for the company to provide lenders and markets a single, consolidated debt figure, during which speculation about the "real" debt number (ranging from ~₹7,000 crore to ~₹18,000 crore across different reports) was allowed to run unchecked.
- Despite the 2019 stabilization narrative, the company continued to default on debt in subsequent quarters through 2020–2021, suggesting the initial post-crisis debt reduction plan was not sufficient to address the underlying structural over-leverage.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
A diversified group with material promoter-level pledging and personal guarantees should maintain (and be prepared to disclose on short notice) a single consolidated debt picture across listed and private entities — the absence of this here meant the crisis response had to start with basic fact-finding rather than immediate reassurance. Once the crisis broke, an independent, board-commissioned review of total group exposure released within days (not weeks) would have reduced the multi-week window of conflicting debt estimates that dominated coverage. For any founder-led group, succession and crisis-communication protocols should be pre-established, not built reactively — the fact that Hegde's employee letter and the company's market disclosures were credible and fairly fast reflects well, but the underlying financial opacity that preceded the crisis is the core lesson.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Total group debt (including personal guarantees and pledges) estimated as high as ₹11,000 crore at peak; CDEL's market capitalization fell by roughly ₹3,541 crore in the period around Siddhartha's death alone; the company continued reporting debt defaults into 2021. Reputational: The case became a widely cited example of hidden leverage risk in founder-controlled Indian conglomerates and entrepreneurial mental-health strain tied to debt pressure; Hegde's turnaround leadership was separately covered as a positive counter-narrative.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (illustrates both the reputational value of prompt, honest confirmation during a sensitive personal-crisis event, and the cost of pre-existing financial opacity that a crisis then exposes)

## Case 007

**Company:** Yes Bank Ltd.
**Industry:** Banking / Financial Services
**Year:** 2020 (acute crisis); governance issues traced to 2018–2019
**Crisis Type:** Bank Failure Risk / Corporate Governance & NPA Crisis

**Trigger Event:** After years of aggressive lending to stressed corporate borrowers under founder-CEO Rana Kapoor and a steady deterioration in asset quality and capital position, the RBI imposed a 30-day moratorium on Yes Bank on 5 March 2020, capped deposit withdrawals at ₹50,000 per depositor, superseded the bank's board, and appointed an administrator — a near-unprecedented step for a large private-sector bank.

**Response Type:** Proactive (by RBI/Government, once intervention began) — the preceding years of drift by the bank itself were Delayed/Denial

**Response Speed Score:** 4
*Once RBI decided to act, the sequence moved fast and stayed accurate: moratorium and board supersession on 5 March 2020, administrator (Prashant Kumar, ex-SBI) appointed 6 March, the Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme 2020 notified by government on 13 March, and the moratorium lifted on schedule by 18 March — roughly a two-week window from crisis declaration to a credible, funded resolution plan with SBI and other banks (ICICI, HDFC, Axis, Kotak Mahindra) committing capital. This is close to best-practice regulatory response speed for a systemic banking crisis. The years before intervention, however, saw the bank's own management delay confronting its NPA and governance problems — RBI had already declined to extend Rana Kapoor's tenure in 2018, and an independent director publicly resigned over governance concerns in January 2019, more than a year before the moratorium.*

**Transparency Score:** 4
*RBI's public rationale for the moratorium explicitly cited a steady decline in financial position, inability to raise capital, and "serious lapses in corporate governance" — a relatively direct public accounting rather than euphemism. The Reconstruction Scheme, investor lock-ins, and administrator appointment were all publicly gazetted and specific. Separately, the ED's parallel criminal investigation into Rana Kapoor (arrested 8 March 2020 on fraud and money-laundering allegations, later including asset freezes) added an independent layer of accountability distinct from the bank's own disclosures.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Section 45 of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (RBI's moratorium power to protect depositors); RBI-superseded board and administrator appointment; Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme 2020 (government-notified, with SBI taking up to 49% stake and a 3-year lock-in on major investors); Enforcement Directorate action under PMLA against Rana Kapoor; CBI investigation; later, the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) Act was amended in August 2021 — partly informed by this case — to guarantee depositor payouts within 90 days of a moratorium, versus the 8–10 years typical for stressed cooperative banks previously.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved
*The bank was recapitalized (₹10,000+ crore led by SBI, with other banks and later a July 2020 follow-on public offering raising a further ₹14,870 crore) and continued normal operations under new management. Rana Kapoor's criminal cases remain in process years later (he was granted bail on money-laundering charges in late 2023 but remained in custody on other charges), but the bank itself was stabilized and did not collapse or wipe out depositors.*

**What Went Right:**
- The RBI's intervention sequence — moratorium, board supersession, administrator appointment, reconstruction scheme — was executed within roughly two weeks, protecting depositors and avoiding a bank run or systemic contagion.
- A public-private consortium (SBI plus ICICI, HDFC, Axis, Kotak, and later high-profile individual and institutional investors) shared the recapitalization burden, spreading risk rather than relying on a single bailout entity.
- RBI's public statements named the actual causes (governance lapses, capital-raising failure) rather than issuing vague reassurances, which helped establish credibility for the intervention.
- The case directly produced a durable regulatory improvement (faster depositor payout guarantees) — the crisis response left the system better prepared for future cases, not just this one.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Warning signs were visible for well over a year before intervention — RBI declined to extend Rana Kapoor's CEO term in 2018, ratings agencies downgraded the bank through 2019, and an independent director resigned publicly in January 2019 citing governance issues — yet no earlier corrective action prevented the slide to a moratorium.
- The bank's own management under Kapoor's successor (Ravneet Gill, from March 2019) was unable to raise adequate capital or arrest the NPA slide despite roughly a year in charge, suggesting internal remediation attempts were insufficient or too late relative to the scale of the problem.
- Aggressive lending practices — including to companies with alleged personal ties to Kapoor — were only fully surfaced publicly after the crisis, not flagged and addressed proactively by the bank's own governance structures.
- The moratorium itself, while short, still caused real depositor disruption (₹50,000 withdrawal cap) that eroded public trust in mid-sized private banks generally, beyond Yes Bank alone.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Governance red flags — a regulator declining to extend a CEO's term, an independent director resigning over governance concerns, and successive ratings downgrades — should trigger a mandatory, time-bound independent forensic review well before the point of systemic risk, not be treated as isolated data points. Yes Bank's own board and later management (2018–2019) had roughly 18 months of visible warning signal before the March 2020 moratorium and did not use that window to force a credible recapitalization or governance overhaul on their own terms. For financial institutions specifically, an ORM/crisis-comms team should recognize that the credibility of a regulator-led rescue depends on speed and specificity — RBI's two-week execution here is the benchmark other financial-sector interventions should be measured against.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: RBI-led recapitalization mobilized over ₹10,000 crore initially, with a further ₹14,870 crore raised via FPO in July 2020; Kapoor's personal assets worth roughly ₹1,400 crore were frozen by the ED. Reputational: Yes Bank became the reference case for private-bank governance failure in India and directly shaped subsequent depositor-protection law; the bank itself has since rebuilt its public standing under new ownership and management.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (a rare "regulator-as-crisis-manager" case — useful for benchmarking what fast, specific, credible intervention looks like once a private company's own governance response has failed)

## Case 008

**Company:** Byju's (Think & Learn Pvt. Ltd.)
**Industry:** Edtech
**Year:** 2022–ongoing
**Crisis Type:** Financial Governance Collapse / Auditor & Board Exodus / Insolvency

**Trigger Event:** After India's Ministry of Corporate Affairs asked Byju's in August 2022 to explain non-filing of its audited financials for the year ending March 2021, delays compounded — culminating in June 2023 when statutory auditor Deloitte Haskins & Sells resigned, citing an inability to complete the FY22 audit due to prolonged non-receipt of financial records, and three board members linked to major investors (Sequoia/Peak XV, Prosus, Chan Zuckerberg Initiative) resigned the same week.

**Response Type:** Denial-Delayed, escalating over multiple stages

**Response Speed Score:** 1
*Byju's response pattern across this multi-year crisis was consistently reactive and slow: it took over a year from the MCA's initial query (August 2022) to Deloitte's resignation (June 2023) to actually produce FY22 financials, and even then a second auditor (BDO/MSKA, appointed after Deloitte) also resigned roughly a year later (September 2024) citing the same core problem — inadequate management support and documentation. When Deloitte resigned, Byju's initially termed it a "planned transition" and disputed that board members had actually resigned, even as those investors (Prosus, Peak XV, CZI) publicly confirmed their resignations within a day — a direct contradiction that undercut the company's own statements at the moment they were made.*

**Transparency Score:** 1
*The company's disclosed FY22 results, when finally filed in August 2023, showed a consolidated loss of ₹8,245 crore against operating revenue of ₹5,014 crore — a dramatically different picture from the growth narrative the company had projected during its $22 billion valuation era. Two consecutive auditors resigned citing essentially the same complaint (lack of management cooperation and documentation), a strong signal of a structural transparency failure rather than a one-off dispute. Byju's also denied allegations of fund diversion through its US subsidiary (Alpha) while simultaneously facing lender lawsuits alleging exactly that.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Ministry of Corporate Affairs scrutiny over non-filing of audited financials; US lender lawsuits alleging loan-agreement breaches and delayed disclosures (including a $1.2 billion term loan dispute with Glas Trust and related creditors); Companies Act provisions on statutory audit and board governance; Indian insolvency proceedings under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (initiated July 2024, triggered by an unpaid ₹158.9 crore BCCI sponsorship debt); NCLAT and Supreme Court proceedings (Supreme Court stayed NCLAT's approval of a BCCI settlement and later allowed the case to proceed); separate US Chapter 11-related bankruptcy filing by a Byju's US subsidiary (February 2024).

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved
*As of the most recent developments, Byju's remains in active insolvency proceedings in India (with a court-appointed Insolvency Resolution Professional and a suspended board), continues to fight a roughly $1 billion claim from US-based creditor Glas Trust, and has lost two successive statutory auditors. Founder Byju Raveendran's authority was briefly restored via an NCLAT ruling and a BCCI debt settlement, but the Supreme Court reopened the matter, leaving the company's ultimate fate undetermined years after the crisis became public.*

**What Went Right:**
- When board members' resignations were confirmed independently by the investors themselves (Prosus, Peak XV, CZI), the underlying facts still became public relatively quickly (within a day of Byju's own denial), preventing a prolonged cover-up.
- The company did eventually file FY22 financials (August 2023) and committed to a public timeline for FY23 filings, showing at least partial movement toward compliance after sustained regulatory and creditor pressure.
- Genuinely independent checks — two different Big-Four-adjacent audit firms, US courts, Indian insolvency courts, and India's Supreme Court — all engaged on the facts rather than accepting company narratives, which meant the true financial picture eventually surfaced despite company resistance.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The company repeatedly disputed facts (board resignations, characterization of auditor exits) that were independently confirmed within a day or two, which damaged credibility every time it happened — a pattern of denial followed by forced correction rather than getting ahead of the story.
- Two consecutive statutory auditors resigned citing the same root complaint — inadequate documentation and management cooperation — indicating the company did not fix the underlying governance/reporting problem even after the first, very public auditor exit and boardroom crisis in mid-2023.
- Financial statement delays stretched for years (FY21 accounts flagged by MCA in August 2022; FY22 accounts not filed until August 2023; FY23 accounts still delayed as of the second auditor's September 2024 resignation), compounding rather than resolving investor and lender distrust over time.
- The company's response to lender litigation (countersuing for "harassment" while simultaneously undergoing mass layoffs) read as combative rather than remediation-focused, at a point when credibility with creditors was already severely damaged.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
The moment the MCA flagged non-filing in August 2022, Byju's should have treated it as a governance-remediation priority — commissioning outside forensic support to accelerate the audit rather than allowing delays to stretch across multiple fiscal years and multiple auditors. When board members resign over stated governance concerns, a company should not publicly dispute the resignation itself; disputing an easily verifiable fact (whether someone resigned) destroys credibility faster than any bad financial number would. Once one major auditor resigns citing lack of documentation, the fix is to overhaul internal financial reporting infrastructure immediately — the fact that a second auditor resigned roughly a year later citing an almost identical complaint indicates the root cause was never addressed. For any high-growth, high-valuation private company, an ORM/crisis team should flag "delayed statutory filings" as an early-warning signal requiring proactive stakeholder communication well before a regulator has to ask publicly.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: FY22 consolidated loss of ₹8,245 crore against ₹5,014 crore revenue; company valuation reportedly written down by several major investors from a peak of $22 billion to a small fraction of that; disputed $1.2 billion term loan and related litigation with US lenders; thousands of employees laid off across multiple rounds. Reputational: Byju's became the definitive case study for Indian startup governance failure post-pandemic funding boom, and is widely cited in investor and media commentary as a cautionary tale about growth-at-all-costs edtech financing.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown (an active, unresolved case — highly relevant for any client evaluating startup/high-growth-company reputational risk; illustrates the compounding cost of disputing verifiable facts during a live crisis)
