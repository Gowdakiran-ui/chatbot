## Case 29

**Company:** Srei Infrastructure Finance Ltd (SIFL) & Srei Equipment Finance Ltd (SEFL)
**Industry:** NBFC — Infrastructure & Equipment Finance
**Year:** 2021 (crisis peak); fallout ongoing through 2025
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory takeover, alleged financial fraud, insolvency

**Trigger Event:** On October 4, 2021, RBI superseded the boards of both companies citing governance concerns and repeated payment defaults. Four days later, NCLT Kolkata admitted RBI's insolvency application against both firms under the IBC.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence
**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

Srei's public reaction was to say it was "shocked" by the RBI move and that banks had been "regularly appropriating funds from the escrow account" they controlled — a statement that shifted blame outward rather than addressing the governance findings. There was no proactive disclosure of the underlying financial problems before RBI acted; trouble had reportedly been building for years (since the 2018 IL&FS-triggered NBFC liquidity crisis).

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** RBI supersession under the RBI Act; IBC 2016 (Section 227, applications by RBI as regulator); subsequent fraud classifications by lender banks (Punjab & Sind Bank, Bank of Baroda, Union Bank of India, and — four years later, in December 2025 — Punjab National Bank, which flagged ₹2,434 crore as fraud based on a KPMG forensic audit).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved. National Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd (NARCL) won the resolution bid in February 2023, NCLT approved the plan in August 2023, and the takeover was finalized by January 2024 — but promoter-level fraud allegations and litigation (challenged by founder Hemant Kanoria) remain unresolved as of late 2025.

**What Went Right:**
- The RBI-appointed administrator (Rajneesh Sharma) moved quickly to stabilize operations and publicly assured employees of job security, limiting immediate panic.
- A structured, transparent insolvency process (open bidding, CoC voting, NCLT oversight) replaced the discretionary corporate response — this was regulator-driven, not company-driven.
- Multiple independent forensic auditors (BDO India, KPMG) were brought in over time, creating a documented evidentiary trail.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The company's first public reaction was defensive ("shocked," blaming banks) rather than acknowledging the governance issues RBI cited.
- Fraudulent transactions were disclosed in stages, not upfront — first ~₹5,100 crore, then ₹13,110 crore (November 2022), then a separate ₹2,434 crore flagged by PNB three years after that, in December 2025. Each new disclosure re-opened the negative news cycle instead of drawing a line under it.
- The forensic audit itself was contested by promoters for years (gaps, limited data access) — a fight the company could have short-circuited by cooperating fully and fast in 2021.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** When governance concerns are already known internally, the company (not the regulator) should commission and publish an independent forensic audit before supersession happens, and lead with acknowledgment rather than counter-blame. Staggered fraud disclosures over years are the single biggest driver of prolonged reputational damage here — a full, one-time disclosure in 2021 would have limited the crisis to one news cycle instead of five (2021, 2022, 2023, 2025, and counting).

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: ~₹32,750 crore owed to lenders; banks expected to recover only ~45% (~₹15,000 crore) through the NARCL resolution.
- Reputational: Ongoing — new fraud disclosures against the founders were still surfacing in December 2025, four years after the original crisis, keeping the Srei name in fraud-related search results indefinitely.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (long-tail negative search results spanning 2021–2025+), Crisis Comms (case study in denial-first response), Legal Takedown (promoter disputes over forensic audit findings)

## Case 30

**Company:** Cox & Kings Ltd (CKL)
**Industry:** Travel & Tourism
**Year:** 2019 (crisis onset); liquidated 2020–2021
**Crisis Type:** Financial fraud, falsified accounts, corporate collapse

**Trigger Event:** Starting June 2019, Cox & Kings defaulted on commercial paper repeatedly — three defaults totaling ₹325 crore within a single fortnight by mid-July 2019 — triggering lower-circuit trading halts for 12 straight days.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** IBC insolvency proceedings; SFIO corporate fraud investigation; ED money laundering (PMLA) cases; CBI FIRs; RBI fraud account classification (₹946.44 crore reported Feb 2020 on the Ezeego sister-company account alone); roughly 10 separate cases filed across agencies.

**Resolution Status:** Fatal. Declared bankrupt in November 2020; NCLT ordered liquidation. Promoter Peter (Ajay Ajit Peter) Kerkar and CFO Anil Khandelwal were both jailed. The 264-year-old company ceased to exist.

**What Went Right:**
- Lenders acted relatively fast on the financial side: within a month of the defaults, they had appointed PwC (August 2019) to run a forensic audit — this wasn't a company-led move, but it did create an independent record quickly.
- Nothing on the company's own communications side stands out as effective; this case is close to a pure cautionary tale.

**What Went Wrong:**
- PwC's forensic findings (April 2020) found the company had overstated sales/debtors, understated debt, recorded fictitious transactions, and siphoned roughly ₹4,367 crore into related-party entities with no proper approvals — later investigations pushed the related-party diversion figure toward ₹21,000 crore.
- When the interim PwC report became public, Kerkar's response was to call it incomplete and say the company "cannot comment" until further investigation — a deflection that read as stalling rather than accountability.
- A company GM, Sagar Deshpande, went missing shortly after being served an ED summons in October 2020 and was later found dead near railway tracks — an event that compounded reputational damage regardless of its ultimate cause, because it happened inside an active fraud investigation with no company statement addressing it directly.
- Total silence/legal-only responses over 2020–2021 let investigative journalism and agency leaks control the narrative entirely.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** At the first commercial paper default (June 2019), the board should have proactively disclosed the true state of related-party transactions and requested its own independent audit rather than waiting for lenders to impose one. Once the PwC interim findings leaked, a specific, fact-based response (which transactions are disputed, why, with what evidence) would have been far better than a generic "raises more questions than it answers" line that reads as evasive.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: ~₹5,500 crore in fraud/defaults across ~10 agency cases; total company liquidation.
- Reputational: Complete — a 264-year-old brand ended in liquidation with its promoter incarcerated; the case remains a standard reference point for Indian corporate fraud investigations.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (textbook failure case), Legal Takedown, ORM (permanent negative association between the Cox & Kings name and "fraud")

## Case 31

**Company:** Sterling Biotech Ltd (Sandesara Group — also Sterling Oil and Sterling SEZ)
**Industry:** Pharmaceuticals / Biotech (with linked oil & gas operations)
**Year:** 2017 (fraud exposed, promoters fled); settlement in 2025
**Crisis Type:** Bank fraud, money laundering, absconding promoters

**Trigger Event:** In October 2017, CBI filed an FIR alleging ₹5,383 crore in bank fraud by Sterling Biotech and the Sandesara brothers (Nitin and Chetan), based on falsified balance sheets and hundreds of shell companies. The brothers — along with Chetan's wife Dipti and associate Hitesh Patel — had already left India in 2017, reportedly fleeing to Nigeria on Albanian passports before the case fully broke.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

There was effectively no corporate crisis response — the promoters left the country and never engaged publicly with the allegations in India. Non-bailable warrants were issued October 25, 2018; Nitin Sandesara was briefly detained in Dubai in August 2018 but evaded custody.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** PMLA (ED money laundering case, registered August 2017); Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 (all four family members declared fugitive economic offenders in September 2020 — one of the first major uses of this law, alongside Vijay Mallya and Nirav Modi); SFIO corporate fraud probe; Income Tax Black Money Act cases; IBC insolvency of group companies.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved. On November 19, 2025 — eight years after the fraud surfaced — the Supreme Court conditionally quashed all criminal cases against the Sandesaras in exchange for a ₹5,100 crore final payment (part of a total ₹6,761 crore one-time settlement, after accounting for ₹3,507.63 crore already deposited and ₹1,192 crore recovered via IBC), with a payment deadline of December 17, 2025.

**What Went Right:**
- Enforcement agencies moved decisively on the asset side even though the individuals were unreachable: ED attached assets worth over ₹9,778 crore (later reported as high as ₹14,500 crore) across domestic and offshore holdings, including London properties and Nigerian oil assets.
- The Fugitive Economic Offenders Act was used as designed — to freeze and confiscate assets when the accused refuse to return and face trial.

**What Went Wrong:**
- There was no company-side crisis response at all — the defining failure of this case is total absence of communication, which let allegations, agency statements, and speculation fill the vacuum for nearly a decade.
- The promoters' choice to flee (using an allegedly fraudulent Albanian passport) rather than contest the allegations in India meant reputational damage was never contained or countered — it simply compounded year after year.
- The final resolution — criminal cases quashed for a payment equal to roughly a third of the ₹16,000 crore originally alleged — was widely criticized as a soft landing that undercuts deterrence, which itself became a fresh reputational and public-trust story in November–December 2025.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** This case has almost no "what the company did right" to point to on the communications side — it is primarily useful as a lesson in what total non-engagement costs. Promoters facing serious fraud allegations who leave the jurisdiction guarantee the worst possible reputational outcome: years of one-sided negative coverage, FEO designation, and a final settlement that still reads publicly as "escaped justice" regardless of the money paid.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: ED claimed the fraud was larger than the Nirav Modi PNB scam — over ₹14,500 crore versus ₹11,400 crore. Final settlement recovered roughly ₹6,761 crore, a fraction of the alleged total.
- Reputational: Severe and prolonged — nearly a decade of "fugitive" status, Interpol notices, and recurring news cycles; the November 2025 settlement itself generated fresh negative coverage questioning whether justice was actually served.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (an 8-year negative search footprint that never had a company-side counter-narrative), Crisis Comms (an extreme case of the cost of silence), Legal Takedown

## Case 32

**Company:** National Spot Exchange Limited (NSEL), promoted by Financial Technologies India Ltd (FTIL, later renamed 63 Moons Technologies), founder/chairman Jignesh Shah
**Industry:** Commodity & Financial Exchange
**Year:** 2013
**Crisis Type:** Payment default / systemic structural fraud

**Trigger Event:** On July 30, 2013, the Forward Markets Commission (FMC) ordered NSEL to stop launching new paired contracts over rule violations. NSEL suspended trading the next day, July 31, 2013, and defaulted on roughly ₹5,600 crore owed to about 13,000 investors — a scheme built on "paired contracts" that disguised speculative trading as physical-delivery commodity trading without sufficient underlying stock.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, later Reactive under regulatory pressure
**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

NSEL suspended trading within a day of the FMC order (fast, forced), but the framing that followed blamed the 24 defaulting members rather than acknowledging the exchange's own structural and governance failures. State trading company MMTC publicly told the Attorney General that NSEL's suspension of its management team was "eyewash" and that the board itself was complicit.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FMC regulatory orders (later merged into SEBI); Companies Act Section 396 (government-ordered forced merger of FTIL and NSEL, February 2016); Maharashtra Protection of Interest of Depositors (MPID) Act; SEBI insider-trading order (2017, ₹125 crore in "averted losses" by 13 persons including Shah's relatives); criminal cases via Mumbai EOW and CBI.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved / Partially Resolved. Jignesh Shah and former MCX CEO Shreekant Javalgekar were arrested in May 2014. FMC declared Shah and FTIL "not fit and proper" to run any exchange in December 2013, forcing FTIL to cut its MCX stake from 26% to 2%. But the government's central remedy — forcibly merging FTIL with NSEL to make FTIL liable for NSEL's debts — was struck down by the Supreme Court in April 2019 as "ultra vires" and not genuinely in the public interest, after five years in the courts. Many of the 13,000 investors have still not recovered their money in full, more than a decade on.

**What Went Right:**
- The regulator, not the company, acted with real speed: FMC issued a show-cause notice and reached a "not fit and proper" finding against Shah within about five months of the default (July to December 2013) — a genuinely fast regulatory turnaround for India at the time.
- Multiple agencies (FMC, SEBI, EOW, CBI) ran investigations in parallel rather than waiting on each other, which kept pressure on and produced a well-documented public record.

**What Went Wrong:**
- NSEL's own narrative from day one shifted blame to the 24 defaulting borrowing members instead of addressing the paired-contract structure and an FTIL subsidiary (IBMA) that traded on the exchange with margin exemptions — a direct conflict of interest that fed the crisis.
- The government's chosen legal remedy (forced merger under Section 396) was overreach — it took five years to be struck down by the Supreme Court, wasting enforcement resources and giving FTIL/63 Moons a genuine legal win to point to, which muddied the public narrative about accountability.
- Jignesh Shah's public strategy was pure, sustained denial ("I stand tall, no agency has found anything against me") for over a decade, without ever mounting a substantive fact-based rebuttal of the forensic findings — instead he emphasized process wins (the 2019 Supreme Court merger ruling) as vindication, even though the underlying default and investor losses were never disputed by any court.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The company should have gotten ahead of the structural conflict-of-interest story (margin exemptions for an affiliated trading entity) before the FMC's forensic audit made it public. On the regulatory side, using a narrower, more legally sound enforcement mechanism from the start — rather than a sweeping "public interest" merger order that courts eventually rejected — would have produced a cleaner, faster resolution and avoided handing the accused a legitimate legal victory years later.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: ~₹5,600 crore default affecting ~13,000 investors; MCX and FTIL market capitalization fell by hundreds of crores in the weeks after the crisis; commodity futures turnover across the industry fell roughly 30% in 2013 (from ₹175 lakh crore to an estimated ₹125 lakh crore).
- Reputational: Very long-tail — Jignesh Shah's name has remained tied to "NSEL scam" in search results and news coverage for over a decade, despite no direct criminal conviction against him personally as of the most recent reporting.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (a decade-plus negative search footprint for both the company and its founder), Legal Takedown (extensive, still-active litigation history), Crisis Comms (a case study in narrative control failure even where the regulator acted fast)
