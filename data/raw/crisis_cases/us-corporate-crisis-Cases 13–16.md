Case ID: 13
Company: Peloton
Industry: Consumer fitness technology / connected hardware
Year: 2021
Crisis Type: Product safety failure / recall crisis

Trigger Event: A 6-year-old child died after being pulled underneath a Peloton Tread+ treadmill in March 2021, part of a pattern of 70+ prior injury reports the CPSC had already flagged to the company.

Response Type: Denial-Silence, then forced Reactive
Response Speed Score: 2
Transparency Score: 2

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) — Consumer Product Safety Act; voluntary recall process

Resolution Status: Partially Resolved

What Went Right:

Once it reversed course, CEO John Foley issued an unambiguous on-record admission of fault: "Peloton made a mistake in our initial response to the CPSC's request. We should have engaged more productively with them from the outset."
Agreed to a full voluntary recall of 125,000 Tread+ units and separately recalled the Tread over a detaching-touchscreen hazard.
Pushed a software update on May 19, 2021 requiring a 4-digit passcode to unlock the machine, reducing unsupervised child access immediately (a fix that didn't require a hardware wait).
Extended the full-refund deadline an extra year (to November 6, 2023) when the rear-guard hardware fix still wasn't ready.

What Went Wrong:

On April 17, 2021, the CPSC issued an urgent public warning that children and pets could be pulled under the Tread+. Peloton's spokesperson response was to publicly contest the regulator: "We are disappointed that the CPSC is mischaracterizing the situation" — a denial issued after a child had already died.
That public fight with the CPSC lasted roughly three weeks (April 17 to May 5) before Peloton capitulated to a full recall — three weeks in which the product stayed on the market and in homes.
No proactive recall or public safety guidance was issued after the death itself in March; it took the regulator's public warning to force a response at all.
The final incident count kept climbing after the recall (72 entrapment reports, then 335 total incident reports and 87 injuries by 2023), suggesting the original April messaging understated the scope.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: The moment a company is notified a death is linked to its product, silence isn't neutral — it accumulates as concealment in the public record. Peloton had the CPSC's data on 70+ prior incidents before the April warning; it should have gone to the CPSC with a voluntary corrective action plan before the regulator had to force the issue publicly. Never contest a safety regulator's warning in the media when a child's death is on the record — the recall almost always happens anyway, at a much higher reputational price for the delay.

Estimated Impact: ~125,000 Tread+ units recalled; hundreds of millions in refund/inventory costs; multi-year CPSC oversight (rear-guard repair wasn't approved until 2023, two years after the recall); stock price and brand trust took a lasting hit during a period Peloton was already under pressure post-pandemic.

Onlyne Relevance: Crisis Comms / ORM — the "we are disappointed the CPSC is mischaracterizing" quote became the defining, most-searched soundbite of the crisis and is a textbook example of a denial statement outliving the crisis itself in search results.

Key Sources: CPSC.gov recall notices (May 5, 2021; Oct 2022; May 2023 rear-guard release); CNN Business (May 5, 2021); ConsumerNotice.org; Feldman Shepherd law firm case summary.

Case ID: 14
Company: Target Corporation
Industry: Retail
Year: 2013

Crisis Type: Data breach / cybersecurity failure

Trigger Event: Hackers used stolen network credentials from Target's HVAC vendor, Fazio Mechanical, to install RAM-scraping malware on point-of-sale systems, stealing card data from every swipe at ~1,800 US stores between November 27 and December 15, 2013 — the peak Black Friday shopping window.

Response Type: Reactive
Response Speed Score: 3
Transparency Score: 3

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: State data breach notification laws; FTC oversight; PCI-DSS; multistate Attorneys General investigation

Resolution Status: Resolved (at high financial cost)

What Went Right:

Once security journalist Brian Krebs broke the story on December 18, Target confirmed the breach publicly the very next day, December 19 — a genuinely fast public acknowledgment once it went public.
CEO Gregg Steinhafel apologized directly to customers and offered a 10% discount weekend within 24 hours of confirmation.
Briefed the Senate Judiciary Committee (Feb 4) and testified before a House subcommittee (Feb 5) — direct, public regulatory engagement rather than avoidance.
Created its first Chief Information Security Officer role and invested heavily afterward in EMV chip-and-PIN and a Cyber Fusion Center.

What Went Wrong:

Target's own security tools (FireEye malware detection) had flagged suspicious activity during the breach window, but internal teams didn't escalate or act on those alerts before the breach became public via outside reporting.
Disclosure came in installments, which reads as concealment even when unintentional: 40 million card records on Dec 19, then an additional 70 million customers' PII (names, addresses, phone numbers, emails) revealed on January 10 — three weeks later. Each new disclosure re-triggered the news cycle and eroded trust further.
CIO Beth Jacob resigned March 5, 2014; CEO Steinhafel resigned in May 2014 — leadership departures that signaled the company itself viewed the internal failure as severe.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: When internal detection tools generate alerts during an active intrusion, there needs to be a hard escalation trigger — not a queue. And when scope is still being investigated, say so explicitly upfront ("we believe X million records were affected and are still investigating whether more were exposed") rather than issuing a number that later has to be revised upward — the revision is what did the reputational damage, not the original number.

Estimated Impact: $292 million in total pre-tax breach costs ($202 million net of insurance); $18.5 million multistate AG settlement (largest of its kind at the time); $10 million consumer class-action settlement; comparable sales guidance cut following the breach; CEO and CIO both resigned.

Onlyne Relevance: ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — a foundational case in third-party vendor risk and incremental-disclosure reputational harm.

Key Sources: Star Tribune breach timeline; EveryCRSReport.com (Congressional Research Service); Huntress threat library; Forbes (Jan 10, 2014).

Case ID: 15
Company: Yahoo
Industry: Technology / internet services
Year: Breaches occurred 2013–2014; disclosed 2016–2017

Crisis Type: Data breach with multi-year concealment / nondisclosure

Trigger Event: State-sponsored hackers breached Yahoo's servers in August 2013, eventually compromising all 3 billion user accounts, and again in late 2014 (500 million accounts via forged cookies) — but Yahoo did not disclose either breach until 2016, while in the middle of acquisition talks with Verizon.

Response Type: Denial-Silence
Response Speed Score: 1
Transparency Score: 1

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: SEC disclosure rules (material event non-disclosure); state breach notification laws; shareholder securities litigation; FBI/DOJ criminal investigation

Resolution Status: Fatal (to Yahoo as an independent company)

What Went Right:

Once disclosed, Yahoo forced password resets and invalidated unencrypted security questions/answers across affected accounts.
Cooperated with the FBI, which led to a March 2017 indictment of four men, including two Russian FSB officers, for the 2014 breach.

What Went Wrong:

Yahoo's own senior executives and legal team were told about the 2013 intrusion at the time but did not launch a full investigation or disclose it — this is not a detection failure, it's a documented internal awareness failure that the SEC later penalized directly.
Disclosure only happened during Verizon acquisition due diligence in 2016 — three years after the 2013 breach and roughly two years after the 2014 breach — and looked to regulators and the public like it was forced out by the deal, not chosen voluntarily.
The scope of the 2013 breach was revised twice after initial disclosure: 1 billion accounts (Dec 2016) → all 3 billion accounts (Oct 2017), a full year later. Each revision re-opened the story and destroyed the credibility of the original disclosure.
General Counsel Ronald Bell resigned; CEO Marissa Mayer forfeited $12 million in equity compensation as an accountability measure — both signals of an internal failure at the top.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: A breach discovered during — or shortly before — an M&A process is exactly the moment materiality and disclosure obligations are sharpest, not the moment to go quiet. Legal and security teams should treat "we might be acquired soon" as an accelerant for disclosure, not a reason to delay. And final scope needs to be nailed down with an independent forensic review before the first public number is given — Yahoo's credibility problem was less about "you got breached" and more about "you told us the wrong number three times."

Estimated Impact: Verizon cut its acquisition offer by $350 million (final deal: $4.48 billion); $117.5 million class-action settlement; $35 million SEC fine — the first-ever SEC penalty issued specifically for failure to disclose a data breach; roughly $152.5 million in combined fines and settlements.

Onlyne Relevance: ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — the reference case for what happens when concealment, not the breach itself, becomes the story.

Key Sources: CBS News ($117.5M settlement, SEC $35M fine); SEC enforcement announcement (April 2018); Wikipedia "Yahoo data breaches" (chronology, exec accountability); Huntress and Panorays threat-library summaries.

Case ID: 16
Company: Capital One
Industry: Financial services / banking
Year: 2019

Crisis Type: Data breach (cloud infrastructure misconfiguration)

Trigger Event: Former AWS engineer Paige Thompson exploited a misconfigured web application firewall via server-side request forgery (SSRF) to access Capital One's AWS storage buckets between March 22–23, 2019, eventually pulling records on ~106 million US and Canadian customers.

Response Type: Reactive (but fast once alerted)
Response Speed Score: 4
Transparency Score: 4

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC); Federal Reserve cease-and-desist order; Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act data safeguards; state breach notification laws

Resolution Status: Resolved

What Went Right:

Capital One had no internal detection of the intrusion at all — it was a GitHub user who spotted Thompson's posts about the theft and tipped off the company on July 17, 2019. Once tipped, Capital One moved fast: confirmed the intrusion July 19, contacted the FBI, and publicly disclosed July 29 — 10 days from tip to public disclosure.
Customers were notified within 10 days of the intrusion's discovery — a detail the OCC explicitly credited in its enforcement order as a mitigating factor.
Public disclosure included a detailed statement, CEO Richard Fairbank's on-record comments, and an offer of free credit monitoring and identity protection.
Cooperation with the FBI led to Thompson's arrest the same day the breach was publicly disclosed — a rare instance of enforcement moving in lockstep with disclosure.
Immediately fixed the SSRF/WAF vulnerability upon discovery and later restructured its cloud security program, becoming a public advocate for cloud security standards.

What Went Wrong:

The underlying failure was basic: a misconfigured firewall paired with an IAM role that had far broader S3 access permissions than it needed. The OCC found Capital One's board had "failed to take effective actions to hold management accountable" for risk management gaps that had been flagged before the breach.
The intrusion went undetected internally for nearly four months (March 22 to July 17) — Capital One's own monitoring never caught it; an outside GitHub user did.
Nearly two years after the original disclosure, Capital One revealed that more Social Security numbers were part of the stolen data than originally stated — a smaller-scale repeat of the "revised scope" credibility problem seen in Target and Yahoo.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: Cloud permissions need continuous, automated configuration auditing — not periodic manual review — because a single overly broad IAM role is enough to turn one misconfigured firewall into a 106-million-record breach. And a bank's internal detection capability should be at least equal to what a random outside developer can spot by reading a public GitHub post; relying on an external tip for a breach of this scale is itself a finding, which is exactly how the OCC treated it.

Estimated Impact: $80 million OCC civil penalty — the first major cybersecurity penalty of its kind against a bank; $190 million class-action settlement (2022); Federal Reserve cease-and-desist order requiring board-level remediation; ~106 million US and 6 million Canadian customers affected.

Onlyne Relevance: ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — a strong contrast case to Target and Yahoo: shows that fast, transparent post-discovery response can materially limit narrative damage even when the underlying security failure was severe.

Key Sources: DOJ press release, United States v. Paige Thompson (Western District of Washington); CBS News ($80M OCC fine); Banking Dive (OCC consent order details); Security.org breach summary; Huntress threat library.
