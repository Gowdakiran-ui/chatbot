## Case 001

**Company:** Satyam Computer Services
**Industry:** IT Services
**Year:** 2009
**Crisis Type:** Accounting Fraud / Corporate Governance Collapse

**Trigger Event:** On 7 January 2009, founder-chairman B. Ramalinga Raju faxed a five-page confession letter to the Satyam board, SEBI, and stock exchanges admitting he had inflated profits, cash balances, and revenue for years — overstating assets by roughly ₹7,800 crore. This followed a botched attempt in December 2008 to have Satyam buy Maytas Infra and Maytas Properties (companies owned by his sons), which four independent directors had objected to before quitting within days.

**Response Type:** Reactive (by Raju — self-confession, immediate resignation) / Proactive (by Government of India, post-confession)

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*Raju's own "response" was a confession, not a crisis-management response — he resigned the same day and offered no remediation plan. But the Government of India acted fast once the fraud became public: within days it moved to dissolve the board, and by 10 January 2009 (3 days later) had appointed a new government-nominated board including Deepak Parekh, Kiran Karnik, and C. Achuthan to stabilize the company and prevent client and employee flight.*

**Transparency Score:** 2
*Raju's confession was itself an act of disclosure, which is unusual — but it came after nearly 7 years of concealment (fraud reportedly began 2002–03) and only when the gap became "unmanageable." There was no proactive investigation ahead of the confession; the truth surfaced only because Raju chose to reveal it (reportedly after a whistleblower email to independent director Krishna Palepu had already started circulating internally). The company itself never ran an independent probe — SEBI, SFIO and CBI did that externally.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** SEBI investigation and penalties; CBI criminal prosecution (forgery, criminal conspiracy, breach of trust); Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) probe; US SEC and PCAOB action (Satyam had ADRs on NYSE) — Satyam paid a $10 million SEC penalty; ICAI action against auditors; government invoked Companies Act provisions to supersede the board. Directly led to the Companies Act, 2013 (criminalizing corporate fraud, mandating auditor rotation) and tighter SEBI LODR norms.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (company survived via acquisition; criminal cases concluded with convictions, though appeals and auditor-liability litigation dragged on for a decade)

**What Went Right:**
- The Indian government intervened within days (not weeks) to prevent total collapse — dissolving the compromised board and installing credible, independent directors (Deepak Parekh, ex-NASSCOM chief Kiran Karnik) to reassure clients and employees.
- A transparent, government-supervised competitive bidding process was run for the sale of Satyam, which is credited with preserving enterprise value and jobs — Tech Mahindra won and completed the acquisition by April 2009, just over 3 months after the confession.
- New leadership fully cooperated with SEC and Indian regulators rather than contesting jurisdiction, which sped up settlement and reduced prolonged uncertainty for clients.
- Regulatory bodies (SEBI, CBI, SFIO) ran parallel, independent investigations rather than relying on internal company disclosures.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The fraud went undetected for roughly 7 years despite statutory audits by PwC, exposing a total breakdown of external audit assurance — PwC's Indian affiliate was later fined by the SEC/PCAOB ($6–7.5 million cited across sources) and SEBI issued a 2-year ban (later quashed on appeal by SAT on jurisdictional grounds).
- Independent directors who objected to the Maytas deal in December 2008 resigned instead of publicly escalating concerns or demanding an audit — their silence deprived the market of an early warning.
- No internal whistleblower mechanism functioned effectively; the fraud only became undeniable after Raju himself chose to stop it, not because internal controls caught it.
- There was zero pre-confession crisis communication — investors and 53,000+ employees learned of a company-threatening fraud with no warning, causing an immediate ~₹14,000 crore erosion in investor wealth in the hours after the letter became public.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Independent directors should have insisted on an independent forensic audit the moment the Maytas deal raised governance red flags in December 2008, rather than resigning quietly. Auditors should have been rotated periodically (this became mandatory only after 2013) and cash-balance confirmations should have been verified directly with banks rather than relying on company-provided documents — this single control gap allowed ₹5,040 crore of fictitious cash to go undetected for years. Any ORM/crisis team advising a company in this situation should push for parallel, board-commissioned independent investigation the day fraud is suspected — not wait for a confession — and prepare stakeholder (client, employee, investor) communication before any public disclosure, not after.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: ₹7,800 crore fraud; investors lost an estimated ₹14,000 crore (~$2.2 billion) in market value within hours of the confession; Satyam sold to Tech Mahindra for a fraction of pre-scandal valuation. Reputational: Became the reference case for corporate fraud in India ("India's Enron"); triggered a nationwide overhaul of audit and governance regulation (Companies Act 2013).

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown (retrospective — this is the foundational case study for why proactive disclosure and independent audit trails matter for reputation defense)

## Case 002

**Company:** Nestlé India (Maggi Noodles)
**Industry:** FMCG / Food & Beverage
**Year:** 2015
**Crisis Type:** Product Safety Scare / Regulatory Ban

**Trigger Event:** In April 2015, the Uttar Pradesh Food Safety and Drug Administration flagged elevated lead levels and undeclared MSG in a batch of Maggi noodles manufactured in February 2014. On 5 June 2015, following state-by-state lab tests, India's Food Safety and Standards Authority (FSSAI) issued a nationwide order declaring Maggi "unsafe and hazardous for human consumption" and banned its manufacture, sale, and distribution.

**Response Type:** Delayed initially, then Proactive

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*Nestlé India's first public statement came only after market hours on 1 June 2015 — more than five weeks after the UP FSDA's initial finding became public knowledge and after several states had already begun testing/banning the product regionally. Once the nationwide ban hit on 5 June, Nestlé did move fast on operational compliance: it voluntarily recalled all Maggi stock (37,000+ tonnes) within a day, and global CEO Paul Bulcke flew to India and met the FSSAI chairman within 2 days (4 June) — but the initial multi-week silence during the UP-to-nationwide escalation cost it the "under 48 hours" threshold.*

**Transparency Score:** 3
*Nestlé consistently maintained Maggi was safe, citing its own and independent lab tests, and this stance was later vindicated in court and by six foreign food regulators (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore) who all found Indian-made Maggi safe. However, the company's early defensiveness (challenging the FSSAI's lab accreditation rather than immediately commissioning a fully public, jointly-verified retest) and its initial insistence on keeping the "No Added MSG" claim (which it later agreed to drop) blurred the line between legitimate legal defense and reputational stonewalling. It did not proactively publish its own test data before the ban — it surfaced mainly through litigation filings.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FSSAI order under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (product recall, halt of manufacture/sale); Nestlé's writ petition to the Bombay High Court for judicial review; National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) class-action-style suit by the central government seeking ₹640 crore (~$99 million) in damages on behalf of consumers; state-level food safety prosecutions.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved
*Bombay High Court quashed the FSSAI ban on 13 August 2015 (about 10 weeks after imposition) citing a violation of natural justice (no hearing given) and un-accredited testing labs, and ordered a fresh, court-supervised retest. Maggi cleared the new tests and relaunched nationally on 9 November 2015 — roughly 5 months after the ban.*

**What Went Right:**
- Once the nationwide ban was issued, Nestlé complied fully and immediately — destroying over 35,000 tonnes of product rather than contesting the recall itself, which limited allegations of defiance.
- Global CEO personally engaged with the regulator within 48 hours of the escalation, signaling the issue was being taken seriously at the highest level.
- Nestlé pursued the correct legal route (writ petition for judicial review) rather than only fighting in the media, and ultimately won on the substantive safety question in court.
- The relaunch campaign (real-mother testimonials, revised packaging language, Diwali/Dhanteras timing) was well-sequenced and rebuilt market share relatively quickly.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Silence in the first several weeks (April to early June) let state-by-state bans and media coverage snowball into a full-blown national panic — consumers filled the information vacuum with fear.
- Continuing to defend the "No Added MSG" label as "factually correct" after regulators flagged it made the company look legally technical rather than consumer-focused, even though it was scientifically defensible (natural glutamates vs. added MSG).
- Nestlé chose an adversarial posture (suing to challenge lab accreditation) at the same moment as trying to reassure the public — legal strategy and public trust-building worked against each other in the first month.
- Bollywood celebrity endorsers were dragged into public backlash (effigies, boycotts) because Nestlé's slow initial response left endorsers exposed without company guidance.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
The moment the UP state finding became public in late April/early May, Nestlé should have issued an immediate holding statement acknowledging the report, announcing an independent parallel test, and giving a fixed date for results — before the story could spread state by state. A single, consolidated national test protocol agreed jointly with FSSAI at the outset (rather than a patchwork of state labs with disputed accreditation) would have prevented the "our lab says X, your lab says Y" credibility fight that dominated headlines for months. Any ORM crisis team should treat the first 72 hours after any single-state regulatory flag as the critical window to get ahead of a nationwide narrative, especially for a market-leading household brand with 80%+ category share.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Nestlé destroyed ~₹320 crore (~$50 million) worth of product; overall crisis cost widely reported at roughly $500 million (Fortune) including lost sales, destroyed inventory, and market share rebuilding; Nestlé India's stock and quarterly profit both fell sharply through mid-2015; a ₹640 crore government damages claim remained in litigation. Reputational: Maggi's market share, which had been ~80% of India's instant noodle category, dropped sharply during the ban and took over a year of marketing investment to substantially recover.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (textbook case for the cost of delayed first response and the value of a single, credible, jointly-verified fact source in a food-safety scare)

## Case 003

**Company:** Punjab National Bank (Nirav Modi / Mehul Choksi fraud)
**Industry:** Banking / Financial Services
**Year:** 2018
**Crisis Type:** Banking Fraud / Internal Control Failure

**Trigger Event:** In January 2018, jeweller Nirav Modi's firms approached PNB's Brady House branch (Mumbai) for fresh Letters of Undertaking (LoUs) without the collateral margin normally required. When a newly posted bank employee asked for security, the request exposed a scheme running since 2011 in which two branch officials had been issuing fraudulent LoUs — via unauthorized SWIFT messaging outside the bank's core system — that were never recorded in PNB's books.

**Response Type:** Reactive

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*PNB says it detected the fraud on 25 January 2018 and reported it to the CBI on 29 January — a reasonably fast 4-day internal-to-regulator escalation. However, the bank did not disclose the fraud publicly until it filed a stock exchange disclosure on 14 February 2018, roughly 3 weeks after internal detection — well outside the 24–48 hour window — during which Nirav Modi, his family, and Mehul Choksi had already left India (reportedly in the first days of January, before the branch employee's request even surfaced the fraud, though some accounts place their departure closer to detection).*

**Transparency Score:** 2
*PNB's initial disclosed liability figure (~₹280 crore in the first FIR) grew rapidly and repeatedly — to ₹11,400 crore within days, then ₹12,600–14,357 crore by May 2018 — creating an impression that the bank did not have (or did not share) a full picture at the time of first disclosure. The bank did cooperate with CBI/ED/SFIO investigations and wrote to 30 other banks flagging the modus operandi so they could check their own exposure, which was a genuinely transparent and system-protective move. But there was no independent, publicly released forensic report explaining how the fraud evaded detection for 7 years across 150+ LoUs before the RBI and government pressed for structural SWIFT-to-core-banking integration reforms.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** CBI criminal investigation and chargesheet (forgery, criminal conspiracy, cheating, abuse of official position); Enforcement Directorate action under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), including asset attachment and use of the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 (enacted partly in response to this case) to declare Modi and Choksi fugitive economic offenders; RBI directive mandating integration of SWIFT messaging with core banking systems across all Indian banks; extradition proceedings against Nirav Modi in the UK and against Choksi in Belgium/Antigua.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
*PNB absorbed and provisioned for the liability (RBI permitted phased provisioning) and continued operating without systemic collapse. Nirav Modi was arrested in London in March 2019 and, as of the most recent proceedings, remained in the UK fighting extradition; Mehul Choksi was arrested in Belgium in 2025 amid extradition proceedings after earlier obtaining Antiguan citizenship. Full recovery of funds and final criminal verdicts against the principal accused remain unresolved years after the fraud surfaced.*

**What Went Right:**
- PNB moved quickly from internal detection (25 January) to formal CBI referral (29 January) — a 4-day escalation internally, even though public disclosure lagged.
- The bank proactively alerted 30 other banks about the fraud's modus operandi so they could check their own SWIFT/LoU exposure — a systemically responsible move that likely limited contagion to other lenders.
- PNB's leadership publicly committed to honoring all legitimate LoU obligations to the other banks involved (rather than disputing liability), which prevented a broader interbank confidence crisis.
- The case directly drove a national regulatory fix: RBI mandated that all Indian banks integrate SWIFT messaging with their core banking systems, closing the exact loophole exploited here.

**What Went Wrong:**
- A single branch was able to issue 150+ fraudulent LoUs over roughly 7 years without detection because SWIFT transactions were not linked to PNB's core banking system — a basic reconciliation control failure.
- Public disclosure (14 February) came about 3 weeks after internal detection and only via mandatory stock exchange filing, not proactive company communication — the market and depositors learned the scale from escalating headlines, not from PNB.
- The disclosed fraud amount changed materially and repeatedly over the following months (from ₹280 crore to over ₹14,000 crore), which read publicly as either concealment or a lack of internal control over the bank's own exposure data.
- Accused individuals had time to leave India before enforcement action froze their movement — raising questions about the lag between initial suspicion and any lookout/travel-restriction measures.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Core banking and SWIFT messaging integration should have been standard practice well before 2018 — this was a known, addressable systems gap, not a novel fraud technique. Once fraud was internally confirmed (25 January), PNB should have made a same-week public disclosure with a preliminary (clearly labeled as preliminary) exposure estimate, rather than waiting for a mandatory filing three weeks later — early, honest "figure may change as investigation proceeds" framing is more credible than a low initial number that balloons 50x over subsequent months. For any client bank or NBFC, an ORM/crisis team should push for immediate account freezes and lookout notices the same day fraud is confirmed internally, before any public disclosure risks tipping off the accused.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Total liability reached ₹14,356.84 crore (~$2 billion); PNB stock fell roughly 20% over two trading days after disclosure; the bank posted its largest-ever quarterly loss in the following results. Reputational: Became shorthand in India for PSU bank governance failure and a direct trigger for the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018; both accused remain internationally infamous figures years later.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (illustrates the reputational cost of escalating, inconsistent public figures during an unfolding financial fraud, and the value of early proactive disclosure over compliance-minimum disclosure)

## Case 004

**Company:** IL&FS (Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services)
**Industry:** Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) / Infrastructure Finance
**Year:** 2018
**Crisis Type:** Systemic Financial Default / Governance & Liquidity Crisis

**Trigger Event:** In June 2018, IL&FS defaulted on repayment of commercial paper and inter-corporate deposits worth ₹450 crore — its first visible default. Despite a leadership change in July (non-executive chairman Ravi Parthasarathy resigned after 30+ years), the group defaulted again in September 2018 (including its subsidiary IFIN defaulting twice within two weeks on ~$1.05 billion of commercial paper), triggering a full-blown confidence collapse across India's NBFC and mutual fund sectors.

**Response Type:** Delayed (by IL&FS itself) / Proactive (by Government of India once systemic risk was clear)

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*IL&FS's own response between the June default and the September escalation amounted to a single leadership reshuffle with no public remediation plan or transparent explanation of the liquidity gap — nearly 3 months of drift while repeated defaults continued. The Government of India, by contrast, acted decisively once the systemic threat was undeniable: it moved the NCLT for change of management within days of the September defaults, secured board supersession by 1 October 2018, and had a new board (chaired by Uday Kotak) presenting a staged resolution framework by 31 October — about one month from intervention to a public plan.*

**Transparency Score:** 2
*The original IL&FS management gave no clear public account of why a company rated AAA as recently as months earlier was suddenly defaulting — the scale of the problem only became apparent to the public through repeated, escalating default disclosures rather than a single, complete accounting. Real transparency arrived only after the new Kotak-led board commissioned a forensic audit (Grant Thornton, 2019–2020) that revealed financial engineering, mis-categorized bad loans, and inflated credit ratings — this came a year-plus after the crisis broke, not during it.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Companies Act, 2013 (Sections 241/242/246 r/w 339) — government application to NCLT for board supersession on grounds of mismanagement prejudicial to public interest; NCLT order of 1 October 2018 suspending the board; NCLAT interim moratorium (15 October 2018) freezing creditor recovery actions across the group; subsequent Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code-linked asset resolution process; SEBI introduced "side-pocketing" (segregated portfolios) for mutual funds on 28 December 2018 to ring-fence IL&FS-linked exposure; RBI and SEBI tightened NBFC liquidity and disclosure norms sector-wide.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
*The government-appointed board avoided an uncontrolled collapse and has resolved debt on an asset-by-asset basis over several years — reports cite over ₹55,000 crore of the roughly ₹94,000 crore–₹99,000 crore group debt resolved by the early 2020s — but the resolution process stretched years beyond initial expectations, and full creditor recovery remains incomplete.*

**What Went Right:**
- The government intervened before the crisis triggered a full NBFC-sector or mutual-fund-industry collapse, recognizing the systemic risk explicitly in its NCLT filing (warning of possible knock-on effects on mutual funds and pension funds).
- Installing a credible, market-respected new chairman (Uday Kotak) alongside experienced regulators and bureaucrats on the new board rebuilt some institutional confidence quickly.
- SEBI's side-pocketing mechanism, introduced within three months of the crisis, gave mutual fund investors a structural tool to isolate bad debt exposure rather than face indiscriminate NAV panic.
- The new board commissioned an independent forensic audit rather than relying on prior management's figures, which eventually surfaced the true scale of governance failure.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The original board and management gave no adequate public explanation between the June and September 2018 defaults, allowing uncertainty to compound and contagion fears to spread into adjacent NBFCs and housing finance companies with no IL&FS-specific exposure.
- Credit rating agencies (ICRA, CARE) were criticized as reactive rather than proactive — downgrades came only after defaults were already public, not as an early warning, undermining confidence in the entire NBFC rating ecosystem.
- The complexity of the group (300+ subsidiaries/associates/JVs) meant even the new board and regulators took over a year to get a full, audited picture of group liabilities — a structural transparency failure baked into how the conglomerate was organized.
- The resolution process, while structurally sound, moved far slower than markets or creditors expected, prolonging the sector-wide liquidity squeeze for NBFCs and housing financiers into 2019 and 2020.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Once the first default occurred in June 2018, IL&FS should have commissioned an independent, board-level liquidity and asset-quality review immediately and disclosed a clear remediation timeline — rather than a quiet leadership change with no accompanying public explanation. Credit rating agencies should have flagged asset-liability mismatches based on ordinary surveillance well before actual default, not after. For any large, systemically important financial group, an ORM/crisis-comms team should insist that the first default (however small) triggers a proactive, detailed disclosure to markets and regulators — silence between the first and second default is precisely the window in which speculation, rating downgrades, and creditor panic compound fastest.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Group debt burden exceeded ₹90,000 crore at time of intervention; the crisis triggered a broader NBFC/housing-finance liquidity crunch that persisted for roughly two years, tightening credit availability across India's shadow banking sector (~$370 billion sector by some estimates); mutual funds with IL&FS exposure faced redemption pressure and NAV markdowns. Reputational: Became the reference case for NBFC/shadow-banking governance failure in India, prompting scale-based regulation for NBFCs and permanently changing how rating agencies and mutual funds treat concentrated NBFC/group exposure.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (systemic-risk case study — demonstrates how withheld or delayed disclosure at one company can cascade reputational and financial damage across an entire sector)
