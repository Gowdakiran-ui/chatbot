## Case 32

**Company:** TripAdvisor (Italian version of the review site)
**Industry:** Online travel reviews platform
**Year:** 2014 (Italian Competition Authority decision of 19 December 2014)
**Crisis Type:** Regulator finding that "authentic reviews" claims were misleading

**Trigger Event:** The Italian Competition Authority (AGCM) investigated TripAdvisor over the claims it made to consumers about its reviews. It found that statements presenting the reviews as "true and authentic reviews, which you can trust" and "most sincere opinions" were misleading. Hoteliers could easily introduce fake reviews, and some remained despite TripAdvisor's filtering system; the authority found those that remained were enough to influence the site's ranking of hotels and restaurants, which can significantly affect consumer choice.

**Response Type:** Regulated (the sources describe the decision, not a company response)

**Response Speed Score:** 3/5 - The authority closed the investigation on 19 December 2014 and gave TripAdvisor 90 days to comply; the sources reviewed do not document how quickly the company changed its practices.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 - The authority found the platform advertised its reviews as trustworthy while acknowledging that its filtering system "cannot eliminate all fake reviews".

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Articles 5, 6 and 7 of EU Directive 2005/29 on unfair commercial practices, transposed into articles 20, 21 and 22 of the Italian Consumer Code; the fine was imposed under Article 27 of the Italian Consumer Code.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (for the authority's decision) - fine of EUR 500,000 and 90 days to comply with the ruling. The sources reviewed do not cover any appeal.

**What Went Right:**
- The platform did operate a filtering system to check the truthfulness of reviews
- The regulator's reasoning gave the industry a clear standard: claims of authenticity must match what the verification can actually deliver

**What Went Wrong:**
- Marketing language promised "true and authentic" reviews that "you can trust" while fake reviews could still get through
- The remaining fake reviews were enough to influence the ranking of hotels and restaurants
- The platform's own trust claims became the basis of the regulator's finding

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Describe review quality in terms the verification can actually support (for example, how reviews are screened and that some may slip through) rather than absolute claims of authenticity. For businesses that depend on a review platform, the case shows regulators treat fake reviews as a consumer-protection issue that platforms can be held to account for, which supports reporting suspected fake reviews with evidence.

**Estimated Impact:** EUR 500,000 fine; 90-day compliance deadline; a regulator decision that judged a review platform on the trust claims in its own marketing.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (regulator precedent for challenging fake reviews and for how review-site claims are judged)

**Key Sources:**
- ICPEN, "Italy - Tripadvisor fined 500,000 EUR by the Italian Authority for misleading practice" (20 January 2015) - https://icpen.org/news/590

## Case 33

**Company:** The Shed at Dulwich (hoax restaurant on TripAdvisor)
**Industry:** Online travel reviews platform (manipulation of rankings)
**Year:** 2017 (idea April 2017; ranked first on 1 November 2017; one-night event 17 November 2017)
**Crisis Type:** Fake reviews gaming a platform's ranking system

**Trigger Event:** In April 2017 a journalist, Oobah Butler, decided to get a non-existent restaurant listed on TripAdvisor: his garden shed in Dulwich, London. He made a website, bought a phone, and for six months asked friends to post fake reviews, aiming to climb the ranking of London's 18,149 listed restaurants. The photographs showed fake food made from household items such as shaving foam and dishwasher tablets. On 1 November 2017 the Shed was the highest-rated restaurant in London. It opened for one night on 17 November 2017 and served ten guests microwaveable meals; the listing received one one-star review.

**Response Type:** Not documented (the sources reviewed describe the hoax and publicity, not a platform statement)

**Response Speed Score:** Not rated - The sources reviewed do not describe how TripAdvisor responded or when the listing was taken down, only that it was taken down afterwards.

**Transparency Score:** Not rated - The platform's response is not documented in the sources reviewed.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** None identified in the sources. Similar conduct (posting fake reviews, buying reviews) is now covered by consumer-protection rules in the UK (DMCCA 2024) and the US (FTC rule, 2024).

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (hoax disclosed; listing removed) - the story became a viral article and documentary; Vice claimed 100 million views worldwide, and the author published a book in 2019. Singapore's parliament reportedly used the story to inform fake-news laws.

**What Went Right:**
- The listing was eventually taken down
- The hoax made the weakness of an unverified ranking system visible to the public without anyone being hurt

**What Went Wrong:**
- A venue that did not exist reached #1 of 18,149 London restaurants in about six months using fake reviews from friends
- TripAdvisor accepted and listed a venue that did not exist, and its ranking rewarded a stream of positive reviews
- The story spread widely as a demonstration of how rankings can be manipulated

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Review sites need checks that the business exists and that reviewers are real, not only filters on the review text. For a business, the lesson is that rankings built on reviews can be moved by a small number of coordinated accounts, which can happen against a client too; a sudden burst of similar new-account reviews is the pattern to document and report.

**Estimated Impact:** Reached #1 of 18,149 London restaurants; one-night event for ten guests; a viral story (100 million views claimed by Vice) that became a reference for review-system manipulation.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms (explains why fake review bursts can distort rankings and how platforms get embarrassed by them)

**Key Sources:**
- Wikipedia, "The Shed at Dulwich" - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shed_at_Dulwich
- Wikipedia, "Oobah Butler" - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oobah_Butler
- NPR, "How A Fake Restaurant Became London's Top Spot On TripAdvisor" (9 December 2017) - https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2017/12/09/569601017/opening-the-hottest-fake-restaurant-in-town

## Case 34

**Company:** Google and Amazon (UK Competition and Markets Authority fake-review undertakings)
**Industry:** Search/maps platform and e-commerce marketplace
**Year:** 2025 (Google, 24 January 2025; Amazon, signed 8 May 2025 and announced 6 June 2025)
**Crisis Type:** Regulator action requiring platforms to police fake reviews

**Trigger Event:** The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) investigated whether Google and Amazon broke consumer law by not doing enough to detect and remove fake reviews, act on suspicious behaviour, or sanction the reviewers and businesses involved. The CMA estimated that as much as GBP 23 billion of annual UK consumer spending is potentially influenced by online reviews. In January 2025 Google gave undertakings; in June 2025 Amazon followed.

**Response Type:** Negotiated commitments (undertakings) rather than a finding of breach

**Response Speed Score:** 3/5 - Both companies agreed to changes without a contested enforcement outcome; Google's undertakings were announced on 24 January 2025 and Amazon's on 6 June 2025 (signed 8 May 2025).

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 - The commitments are published and include reporting to the regulator (Google will report to the CMA over 3 years).

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Consumer protection law enforced by the CMA; Amazon's undertakings were given under Part 8 of the Enterprise Act 2002 because the case was opened under the previous regime. The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCCA) explicitly bans fake reviews and has been in effect since 6 April 2025, and lets the CMA fine businesses up to 10% of global turnover for consumer-law infringements.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (by undertakings, with ongoing reporting) - Google's commitments: reviewers who repeatedly post fake or misleading reviews have those reviews deleted and are barred from posting new ones, wherever they are; businesses found to have boosted ratings with fake reviews get a prominent warning on their profiles and lose the ability to receive new reviews; businesses that repeatedly engage in fake-review activity have all their reviews deleted for 6 months or more; an easier reporting function including for incentives for positive reviews; reporting to the CMA over 3 years. Scope covers business reviews and star ratings on Google Search and Maps, not product reviews. Amazon committed to robust detection and removal of fake reviews and catalogue abuse (sellers attaching reviews from one product to another), sanctions including seller bans and reviewer bans with earlier reviews deleted, and easier reporting.

**What Went Right:**
- Both platforms agreed to concrete sanctions for businesses and reviewers, not only deletion of reviews
- Clear reporting channels, including for incentivised reviews (payment or reward for positive reviews)
- Reporting duty to the regulator for 3 years gives the commitments a check

**What Went Wrong:**
- Regulators found grounds for concern that the platforms were not detecting, acting on or sanctioning fake reviews enough
- Catalogue abuse (reviews of a well-reviewed product attached to a different one) allowed ratings to be borrowed
- The regulator's concern was that some businesses and reviewers involved in fake reviews were not being sanctioned

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Businesses should assume reviews they or their agencies influence are scrutinised: do not buy, incentivise-for-sentiment or write reviews, and use the platform's reporting function for competitors' or brokers' fake reviews, including incentive offers. For clients with UK customers the DMCCA now makes fake reviews explicitly unlawful with penalties of up to 10% of global turnover for businesses.

**Estimated Impact:** GBP 23 billion of annual UK consumer spending potentially influenced by reviews (CMA estimate); Google's sanctions to extend to non-UK businesses including EU businesses by the end of 2025 (per the CMA release); platform-level sanctions now exist for businesses that fake reviews.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (shows what platforms now owe reviewers and businesses in the UK and what reporting routes exist)

**Key Sources:**
- UK CMA, "CMA secures important changes from Google to tackle fake reviews" (24 January 2025) - https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cma-secures-important-changes-from-google-to-tackle-fake-reviews
- UK CMA, "Amazon gives undertakings to CMA to curb fake reviews" (6 June 2025) - https://www.gov.uk/government/news/amazon-gives-undertakings-to-cma-to-curb-fake-reviews

## Case 35

**Company:** Google Spain SL and Google Inc. (Google Spain v AEPD and Mario Costeja Gonzalez)
**Industry:** Internet search / data protection
**Year:** 2014 (judgment of the Court of Justice of the EU, 13 May 2014)
**Crisis Type:** Search-result delisting and the "right to be forgotten"

**Trigger Event:** In 1998 the newspaper La Vanguardia published two announcements of forced sales of properties over social security debts, naming Mario Costeja Gonzalez as an owner; a web version was later made available. In November 2009 he asked the newspaper to remove the data and it refused, citing a Ministry order. In February 2010 he asked Google Spain to erase the links; the request was forwarded to Google Inc. On 30 July 2010 the Spanish data protection agency (AEPD) rejected the complaint against the newspaper but upheld it against Google, ordering the links removed. Google appealed and the case was referred to the Court of Justice of the European Union.

**Response Type:** Contested (Google appealed the agency's order)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 - The complainant's first request to Google was in February 2010 and the final EU judgment came on 13 May 2014, more than four years later.

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 - The proceedings and reasoning are public; after the judgment Google launched an online form for EU and EFTA residents to request removal of links.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU data protection law (Directive 95/46/EC) read with Articles 7 and 8 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights. The Court held that a search engine operator is responsible for its processing of personal data appearing on third-party web pages and that EU law applied through Google's Spanish subsidiary, which sold advertising. It held that data that is "inadequate, irrelevant or no longer relevant or excessive in the light of the time that had elapsed" may have to be erased from search results, and that the data need not be prejudicial to the person. As a rule the person's rights override the operator's economic interest and the public's interest in finding the information, except where the person's public role justifies the interference.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (as a precedent) - Google created a removal request form; on 31 May 2014, the form's first day, it received over 12,000 requests.

**What Went Right:**
- The ruling gave individuals a route to ask for old, irrelevant links to be delisted from name searches without having to get the original publication removed
- The decision came with a balancing test that protects the public interest where the person has a public role
- Google built a request process quickly after the judgment

**What Went Wrong:**
- The complaint against the newspaper was rejected, so the announcement stayed with the publisher; the order applied to the search engine's results
- Years of litigation before a result
- The process depends on a case-by-case assessment of whether data is outdated or irrelevant

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** For an individual or business affected by old but accurate information, a delisting request on the grounds that it is inadequate, irrelevant, no longer relevant or excessive is the route in the EU and EFTA, and the strength of the request depends on the time that has passed and on whether the person has a public role. Document the dates and the lack of current relevance. The original article usually stays online, so plan for the source page as well as the search result.

**Estimated Impact:** Over 12,000 removal requests on the first day of Google's form (31 May 2014); established delisting as a data-protection right in the EU.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Search Results Management / Legal Takedown / ORM (core precedent for requesting delisting of outdated or irrelevant search results in the EU)

**Key Sources:**
- Wikipedia, "Google Spain v AEPD and Mario Costeja Gonzalez" (summary of the CJEU judgment C-131/12 and aftermath) - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Spain_v_AEPD_and_Mario_Costeja_Gonz%C3%A1lez
- EUR-Lex summary, "Right to be forgotten on the internet" - https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/right-to-be-forgotten-on-the-internet.html

## Case 36

**Company:** Arup (British engineering and design firm)
**Industry:** Engineering and design consultancy
**Year:** 2024 (incident reported to police in January 2024; company confirmed 16 May 2024)
**Crisis Type:** Deepfake impersonation fraud

**Trigger Event:** A finance employee in Hong Kong first suspected a phishing email, supposedly from the UK office, asking for a secret transaction. He then joined a video call with people he believed were the chief financial officer and other staff, who Hong Kong police later said were all deepfake re-creations. He agreed to send HK$200 million (about US$25.6 million) across 15 transactions. Arup notified Hong Kong police in January; police described the case publicly in February without naming the company; Arup confirmed to CNN on 16 May 2024 that it was the target.

**Response Type:** Reactive (police notified, public confirmation after the press named the company)

**Response Speed Score:** 4/5 - Arup notified the police in January 2024 and, once reporting identified the company, confirmed the incident in May.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 - Arup confirmed that fake voices and images were used and that its finances and systems were unaffected, but said it could not share details because the incident remained the subject of an ongoing investigation.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Criminal investigation by Hong Kong police (fraud). No regulator action is described in the sources.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved - the investigation was described as ongoing at the time of the reports.

**What Went Right:**
- The firm notified the police in January, early in the response
- A short, direct statement: fake voices and images were used; "our financial stability and business operations were not affected and none of our internal systems were compromised"
- Its global CIO publicly warned that attacks of this kind (invoice fraud, phishing, WhatsApp voice spoofing, deepfakes) are rising, and a regional chairman told staff to stay informed and alert

**What Went Wrong:**
- A multi-person video call with familiar faces and voices was enough to override the employee's initial suspicion
- The payment went out in 15 transactions before the fraud was detected
- The company was named in press reports (including the Financial Times) before it confirmed the incident

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Treat a video call as no proof of identity for payments: require a second, independent channel to confirm unusual transfer requests, and set approval limits that do not rely on one person. For reputation, prepare a short holding statement for a fraud like this (what happened, that systems and finances were not affected, that police are investigating, and what staff are told) so it can be used the day the press names the company.

**Estimated Impact:** HK$200 million (about US$25.6 million) paid in 15 transactions; international media coverage; the case became a reference for deepfake business fraud.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (a precedent for impersonation and deepfake incidents involving senior staff, and for the timing of statements)

**Key Sources:**
- CNN Business via ABC17, "British engineering giant Arup revealed as $25 million deepfake scam victim" (16 May 2024) - https://abc17news.com/money/cnn-business-consumer/2024/05/16/british-engineering-giant-arup-revealed-as-25-million-deepfake-scam-victim/
