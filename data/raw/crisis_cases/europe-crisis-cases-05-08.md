## Case 5

**Company:** Siemens AG
**Industry:** Industrial engineering / electronics conglomerate
**Year:** 2006–2008 (scandal broke Nov 2006, resolved Dec 2008)
**Crisis Type:** Systemic bribery / corruption, corporate governance failure

**Trigger Event:** In November 2006, roughly 200 German police officers raided Siemens' Munich headquarters after a bank flagged suspicious transactions in Siemens' telecom unit, opening an investigation that eventually uncovered a company-wide bribery system used to win contracts across dozens of countries.

**Response Type:** Delayed (initial) → Proactive (from 2007 onward)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — Siemens did not get ahead of the story; the raid forced its hand. It took until early 2007 for the company to launch a serious internal investigation, roughly two months after the raid.

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 — Once underway, the internal probe was unusually thorough for its time: Siemens hired an outside law firm, ran an internal amnesty program that got hundreds of employees to come forward, and ultimately quantified €1.3 billion in suspicious payments dating to 1999.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** German Criminal Code (breach of trust, tax evasion), U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA), SEC anti-bribery/books-and-records/internal-controls provisions, Munich Public Prosecutor's Office proceedings.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- Once the scale became undeniable, Siemens overhauled its Vorstand (management board) and installed new leadership with a mandate to clean house.
- The company ran a formal internal amnesty program, which produced hundreds of employee disclosures and gave investigators a fuller picture than prosecutors alone could have assembled.
- Siemens accepted an independent compliance monitor as part of its settlement — a costly but credibility-building move that gave regulators ongoing visibility into remediation.
- Siemens cooperated simultaneously with German, U.S. and other regulators rather than trying to compartmentalize the exposure by jurisdiction.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The bribery wasn't a rogue-employee problem — investigators found the "tone at the top" tolerated and effectively rewarded bribery for years, with almost no compliance guidance given to sales staff working in high-corruption markets.
- Leadership had opportunities to catch this earlier through normal audit and control processes and didn't.
- The financial exposure was compounded by the fact prosecutors could point to affected deals on nearly every continent (Venezuela, Israel, Mexico, Bangladesh, Argentina, China, Russia, Vietnam, Nigeria, Libya, Italy), making the "isolated incident" defense impossible.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** A conglomerate operating in high-corruption-risk markets needs pre-emptive, enforced compliance controls tied to specific transactions (not policy documents nobody reads), plus a whistleblower channel that routes to the board, not to the business unit under investigation. Once a raid happens, the credible move is not "no comment" but an immediate, dated public statement that an independent investigation has begun — Siemens eventually did this, but two months after the raid, not two days.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: ~$1.6 billion in combined fines and disgorgement to U.S. and German authorities (the largest FCPA settlement at the time), plus roughly €569 million to the Munich prosecutor and further tax charges — total penalties and related costs are usually cited near $2.6 billion once legal fees and the compliance monitor are included. Reputational: Siemens became the reference case for corporate bribery risk for the next decade; the case is still taught in business schools and cited in every major FCPA training program.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms, Legal/Regulatory framing, long-tail search dominance by scandal coverage — a useful template for clients facing "systemic practice" allegations rather than a single bad actor.

## Case 6

**Company:** H&M (Hennes & Mauritz)
**Industry:** Fast fashion / apparel retail
**Year:** 2018 (with a related 2020 follow-up)
**Crisis Type:** Reputational / racial-insensitivity crisis in advertising, social media backlash

**Trigger Event:** On 7–8 January 2018, H&M's UK website listed a children's hoodie modeled by a young Black boy printed with the words "Coolest Monkey in the Jungle," which social media users immediately flagged as evoking a long-standing racist trope; the image spread globally within hours.

**Response Type:** Reactive (first apology) → Delayed/Inconsistent (execution)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — H&M issued a public apology on 8 January, the same or next day, which sounds fast, but the apology only addressed "anyone offended," not the substance of the complaint, and the product kept appearing on other regional H&M sites for days afterward.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — H&M never explained how the image cleared internal review, who approved it, or what specific process failure allowed it. The response focused on optics (pulling the image) rather than disclosure (explaining the failure).

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No direct statutory breach (this was a reputational/PR crisis, not a legal one), but it intersected with UK advertising standards norms and triggered internal HR/diversity policy review.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- H&M pulled the image from its UK site within roughly 24 hours of the backlash peaking.
- The company confirmed the product would not be sold in the U.S. market at all.
- Within about a week, H&M announced a broader commitment to diversity and inclusion review, and within two weeks appointed its first-ever Global Leader for Diversity and Inclusiveness (Annie Wu), giving the response a concrete structural outcome rather than just words.
- Two and a half years later, in June 2020 (amid the George Floyd protests), H&M pledged $500,000 to the NAACP Legal Defense and Education Fund, Color of Change, and the ACLU — a sign the internal reform effort had some staying power rather than fading after the news cycle.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The same sweatshirt listing was found live on H&M's European sites the same morning the apology went out, and on Spanish-market sites the next day — undermining the claim that the item had been fully removed.
- The initial statement apologized for "offending people" rather than acknowledging the racist connotation directly, which read to critics as minimizing rather than owning the mistake.
- Store-level fallout followed: reports of vandalism/destroyed displays at H&M stores in South Africa weeks later show the reputational damage compounded rather than resolved quickly.
- High-profile brand partners distanced themselves publicly — musician The Weeknd, who had been a H&M campaign face since 2017, announced he would stop working with the brand.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Any apparel brand with a global e-commerce footprint needs a single kill-switch that removes a flagged product from every regional storefront simultaneously — not a country-by-country manual takedown that leaves the same "evidence" live in other markets during the exact hours global media is screenshotting it. The apology itself should name the specific harm ("this evoked racist imagery associating Black people with monkeys") rather than a generic "sorry if this offended," which independent crisis-comms research consistently shows reads as evasive.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: difficult to isolate precisely, but H&M shares dropped in the days following, and the brand absorbed store-level disruption costs in South Africa. Reputational: the episode remains one of the most-cited "fashion brand racism" case studies globally and resurfaces in listicles and press coverage years later, meaning it still shapes search results tied to the H&M brand.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (persistent negative search association), Crisis Comms (apology-wording failure), social media backlash management — a strong template for advising clients on multi-market takedown protocols and apology drafting.

## Case 7

**Company:** Ryanair
**Industry:** Aviation / low-cost airline
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Operational failure with major consumer-facing fallout (mass flight cancellations), leadership credibility crisis

**Trigger Event:** In mid-September 2017, Ryanair abruptly cancelled around 2,100 flights after admitting it had mismanaged pilot annual-leave scheduling while transitioning to a new leave-year calendar mandated by the Irish Aviation Authority, stranding tens of thousands of passengers with almost no warning.

**Response Type:** Reactive, initially Denial-Silence on scope → later Proactive on remediation

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — CEO Michael O'Leary did publicly admit "this is our mess-up" within days, which is fast for an admission, but the company then repeatedly understated the scale: it said cancellations wouldn't extend past October, then announced 18,000 further flight cancellations through March just two days after that assurance.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — A union publicly claimed Ryanair knew about the looming pilot-leave/scheduling clash as far back as mid-2016 and did nothing, which — if accurate — means the "sudden mess-up" framing understated how foreseeable the crisis was. Ryanair's own COO memo referenced awareness of the leave-year change back to a "June quarterly update" the prior year.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) enforcement action under EU/UK Regulation 261/2004 (passenger compensation and rerouting rights); Ryanair was found to have initially informed passengers incorrectly of their rights and was formally ordered by the CAA to correct this.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (operationally), but with lasting reputational damage

**What Went Right:**
- O'Leary personally and publicly took ownership early ("this is our mess-up"), rather than deflecting to a scapegoat, which is unusual for the airline's normally combative public posture.
- Ryanair proactively slowed its growth for the winter season — grounding 25 aircraft (later 10 more) — specifically to build in enough slack to avoid a repeat, addressing the root cause rather than only the symptom.
- The company published a detailed public Q&A on its corporate site addressing pilot pay, pilot numbers, and the specifics of the rostering failure, which is a more transparent format than a standard press statement.
- Once the CAA formally intervened, Ryanair fully complied and reissued clear customer-rights information within days (by 29 September).

**What Went Wrong:**
- The initial passenger compensation offer (a flat £40 voucher) fell short of statutory EU261 rerouting/compensation obligations, forcing the regulator to step in and order Ryanair to correct its public communications about passenger rights — a avoidable regulatory escalation.
- The scale of cancellations was revealed in stages (2,100, then 18,000 more, ~715,000 passengers total) rather than all at once, which repeatedly reopened the story in the press and made Ryanair look either dishonest or out of control.
- The airline's Chief Operations Officer, who owned pilot scheduling, left the company shortly afterward — a departure that, while showing accountability, came only after sustained public and shareholder pressure, including calls for O'Leary himself to resign.
- Ryanair shares fell sharply (reports cited a drop of roughly €362 million in company value) as the crisis dragged through late September.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** When an operational failure is going to cascade (in this case, a leave-year transition affecting the whole pilot roster), the airline should disclose the full expected scope in one release, with a dated remediation timeline, instead of a rolling series of "worse than we said" announcements. Passenger-rights communications should be legally checked against EU261 before publication, not corrected retroactively under regulatory pressure — that sequencing itself becomes a second news cycle.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: roughly €50 million in passenger vouchers/compensation by the company's own estimate at the time, plus a significant but hard-to-isolate share-price hit during the crisis weeks. Reputational: reinforced Ryanair's pre-existing reputation for poor customer service and corporate arrogance, a narrative that resurfaces in most subsequent Ryanair PR issues (staff strikes, luggage fees, seating disputes) as background context.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (staged-disclosure failure pattern), regulatory escalation risk, ORM around a "repeat offender" reputation — useful as a warning case for clients on why partial/rolling disclosure is worse than one comprehensive statement.

## Case 8

**Company:** Thomas Cook Group
**Industry:** Travel and tourism (tour operator / airline)
**Year:** 2019
**Crisis Type:** Corporate collapse / insolvency with mass consumer impact

**Trigger Event:** On 23 September 2019, Thomas Cook — a 178-year-old British travel firm — declared compulsory liquidation after failing to secure last-minute rescue funding, ceasing all trading and flights immediately and leaving roughly 150,000 customers stranded abroad in 18 countries.

**Response Type:** Delayed (by the company, pre-collapse) / Proactive (by government and regulator, post-collapse)

**Response Speed Score:** 4/5 for the government/CAA response — the UK Civil Aviation Authority launched "Operation Matterhorn" the same day as the collapse, chartering aircraft and beginning repatriation flights within hours. Thomas Cook itself scores low, since the company had been signaling financial distress for months without a resolution plan reaching customers.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 — The CAA ran an unusually transparent, numbers-driven public operation, publishing daily repatriation counts. Thomas Cook's own pre-collapse communications with customers and travel agents, by contrast, gave little functional warning, since a functioning travel firm cannot pre-announce its own insolvency without accelerating it.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** UK insolvency law (compulsory liquidation, Official Receiver appointed), ATOL (Air Travel Organiser's Licensing) protections for package holidaymakers, and the government's statutory instruction to the CAA to carry out repatriation.

**Resolution Status:** Fatal (for the company itself); Resolved (for the consumer repatriation response)

**What Went Right:**
- The UK government and CAA activated a repatriation plan immediately, drawing on lessons learned from the 2017 Monarch Airlines collapse, and had contingency charter capacity already identified before the formal announcement.
- The CAA published a public, dated operational tally throughout (76,000 of 150,000 people home within 5 days; over half by day 5; operation fully complete by 7 October), which built public confidence during a chaotic period.
- The CAA secured unusual assets, including two A380s, to solve the scale problem despite the 737 MAX grounding limiting available charter aircraft globally at the time.
- The government made a clear, publicly stated policy decision (no taxpayer bailout of Thomas Cook itself) and explained its reasoning, avoiding the appearance of an ad hoc or panicked response even though the underlying decision was politically contested.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Thomas Cook's own board and communications gave customers essentially zero actionable warning; people found out their operator had collapsed only once flights had already stopped, with some already at their destination or mid-transit.
- The company's known financial distress (heavy debt load, competitive pressure from online travel agents, and Brexit-related booking uncertainty) had been public for months, meaning affected customers had no real opportunity to protect their own bookings or make alternative plans.
- Roughly 9,000 UK jobs (22,000 globally) were lost effectively overnight, and the abruptness of the collapse — rather than a phased wind-down — maximized both consumer and employee harm.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** This case is less about crisis-comms tactics and more about pre-crisis contingency: travel firms operating with ATOL-style consumer protection schemes should be required (and in the UK now increasingly are) to maintain repatriation-ready insurance/bonding sufficient that a government-funded emergency airline operation isn't needed. For the corporate side, the lesson for any distressed company is that the moment insolvency becomes a realistic scenario, contingency communications (what happens to customers, refunds, protections) should be prepared in advance even if they can't be published early — so execution isn't chaotic in the final 24 hours.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: Operation Matterhorn cost the UK taxpayer more than £100 million (cited around $120 million/£100m+ by the CAA); Thomas Cook itself was carrying roughly £1.7 billion in debt at collapse (widely reported figure) and the company's total market value was wiped out. Reputational: Thomas Cook effectively ceased to exist as a going concern (the brand was later revived by Chinese owner Fosun as an online-only travel agency, with no relation to the original operational business); the case remains a reference point in UK consumer-protection and insolvency-law discussions.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms under existential/insolvency conditions, ORM implications of sudden brand death and legacy search association, relevant to advising clients on pre-insolvency contingency communications planning.
