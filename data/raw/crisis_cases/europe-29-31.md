Case 29
Company: Meta (Facebook)
Industry: Technology / Social Media
Year: 2023 (record fine), part of a recurring pattern 2022–2024
Crisis Type: Data protection / regulatory non-compliance (recurring)
Trigger Event: Ireland's Data Protection Commission (DPC), Meta's lead EU regulator, found that Meta Ireland kept transferring EU user data to US servers using Standard Contractual Clauses that the EU's top court had already ruled inadequate in its 2020 Schrems II judgment — Meta continued the transfers for nearly three years after that ruling.
Response Type: Delayed / Reactive
Response Speed Score: 1
Transparency Score: 2
Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered: GDPR Article 46(1) (international data transfers), Chapter V; European Data Protection Board (EDPB) binding dispute-resolution decision under Article 65; Irish DPC as lead supervisory authority
Resolution Status: Partially Resolved — fine paid and compliance order issued, but Meta immediately announced it would appeal, and the underlying EU-US data transfer legal uncertainty took years more to resolve through diplomatic channels (EU-US Data Privacy Framework)
What Went Right: Meta had been operating on a genuinely difficult legal footing — Schrems II invalidated the primary transfer mechanism without offering a practical replacement, so Meta's data transfers found themselves in ambiguous territory shared by thousands of companies, not something unique to Meta's conduct. Meta did engage with the DPC's multi-year formal investigation process rather than stonewalling.
What Went Wrong: Meta kept transferring data on legal grounds (Standard Contractual Clauses plus supplementary measures) it knew were shaky since 2020, without pausing transfers or building a compliant interim solution while regulatory clarity was pending — the DPC found the clauses and supplementary measures did not compensate for gaps in US law. This is one entry in a broader pattern: Meta has now accumulated over €2 billion in separate GDPR fines since 2022 across different issues (data scraping, children's privacy on Instagram, breach notification failures), which regulators and the public increasingly read as a business model that treats fines as a cost of doing business rather than a signal to change practice.
Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: After Schrems II in July 2020, Meta should have proactively paused or restructured EU-US transfers pending a compliant framework, or invested in EU-based data localization sooner, rather than continuing the legally contested transfers for years while regulators built a case. For ORM clients: the compounding fine pattern (multiple distinct GDPR fines within a 2-year window) is itself a reputational risk separate from any single fine — repeated violations signal systemic disregard rather than isolated error, and that framing is what press coverage picks up on.
Estimated Impact: €1.2 billion fine (May 2023) — the largest GDPR fine ever issued, surpassing Amazon's prior €746m record; combined with other 2022–2024 DPC fines (€405m Instagram children's privacy, €265m data scraping, €251m breach-notification failures), Meta's total EU regulatory penalty exposure in this period exceeds €2 billion.
Onlyne Relevance: ORM / GDPR / Crisis Comms — the clearest example in the library of "pattern of fines" reputational risk, useful for explaining to clients why regulators and media treat repeat offenders differently from first-time violators
Key Sources: Irish Data Protection Commission press releases, Faegre Drinker, BDO, Global Privacy & Security Compliance Blog, Statista

Case 30
Company: Siemens AG
Industry: Industrial Engineering / Conglomerate
Year: Misconduct 1999–2006, exposed 2006, resolved 2008
Crisis Type: Bribery / corruption scandal
Trigger Event: In November 2006, roughly 200 German police officers raided Siemens' Munich headquarters as part of a money-laundering investigation, uncovering evidence of a systematic global bribery network — investigators ultimately found around 4,283 corrupt payments totaling approximately $1.4 billion made to foreign officials in at least ten countries between 1999 and 2006.
Response Type: Reactive, transitioning to Proactive
Response Speed Score: 3
Transparency Score: 5
Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered: US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA); German anti-corruption law (Munich Prosecutor's Office); OECD Anti-Bribery Convention
Resolution Status: Resolved — largest corruption settlement in corporate history at the time, company undertook full governance overhaul and survived
What Went Right: After the raid, Siemens' management overhauled internal controls, launched its own sweeping internal investigation, and hired an outside law firm to conduct an independent review — which uncovered even more suspicious payments (€1.3 billion) than the original raid found. Siemens introduced an internal amnesty program that got hundreds of employees to come forward with information, and cooperated extensively with US, German, and other authorities throughout the multi-year investigation. US prosecutors explicitly credited this cooperation with reducing the final fine from a potential range of $1.35–2.7 billion down to $800 million combined.
What Went Wrong: The bribery wasn't a rogue-actor problem — it was, in the DOJ's own words, standard operating procedure across much of the company's global operations for years, with false invoices and sham consultant agreements built into normal business processes, and Siemens' own management board had been ineffective at implementing controls even after Germany adopted OECD anti-bribery rules in 1999. The scale (bribes across more than ten countries, from Bangladesh telecoms to Argentine ID cards to Iraq's Oil-for-Food program) shows the corruption was deeply structural, not incidental.
Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: The controls Siemens built only after getting caught — internal amnesty programs, independent external investigation, real cooperation with regulators — are exactly what should have existed as ongoing infrastructure before 2006, not as crisis response. For ORM clients facing legacy compliance exposure: full voluntary disclosure and genuine structural reform, even when painful and expensive up front, measurably reduces penalties and rebuilds credibility faster than minimizing or contesting findings — Siemens is the reference case regulators still cite for cooperation credit.
Estimated Impact: $1.6 billion total in combined US and German fines/disgorgement ($350m SEC, $450m DOJ, ~$569m Munich prosecutor, plus an earlier ~$285m German fine in 2007) — the largest FCPA-related settlement ever at the time; the company survived and rebuilt its compliance reputation over the following decade, now frequently cited as a corporate turnaround case rather than a cautionary tale.
Onlyne Relevance: ORM / Crisis Comms — best case in the library for demonstrating that full cooperation and voluntary structural reform, even after a very bad start, can lead to reputational recovery
Key Sources: US DOJ press release, SEC press release, Miller & Chevalier, Stanford FCPA Clearinghouse, Stanford GSB case study

Case 31
Company: Nokia
Industry: Telecommunications / Consumer Electronics
Year: 2011–2013 (acute decline), sale completed September 2013
Crisis Type: Strategic/reputational decline (slow-motion business collapse rather than single acute incident)
Trigger Event: Nokia's smartphone market share collapsed from over 50% in 2007 to single digits by 2013 as Apple's iOS and Google's Android took over the market; in February 2011 CEO Stephen Elop publicly described the company's situation as a "burning platform," publicly signaling to the market and press that the company itself believed it was in existential crisis.
Response Type: Delayed
Response Speed Score: 2
Transparency Score: 3
Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered: None directly — this is a strategic/reputational crisis rather than a legal or regulatory one, though the eventual ~10,000 job cuts by end of 2013 triggered Finnish and EU labor consultation obligations
Resolution Status: Resolved via divestiture — Nokia sold its devices business to Microsoft in September 2013 for approximately €5.44 billion and pivoted entirely to network infrastructure, where it still operates today
What Went Right: Nokia's leadership was unusually candid publicly about the scale of the crisis rather than projecting false confidence — the "burning platform" framing was blunt, internally controversial, but honest, and it helped justify the difficult strategic pivot to a skeptical board and market. The eventual sale to Microsoft, while a retreat, let Nokia exit a collapsing business on negotiated terms rather than through bankruptcy, preserving the parent company and its infrastructure business.
What Went Wrong: The response was years too late relative to the actual shift in the market — Nokia clung to its aging Symbian operating system long after Apple's 2007 iPhone launch and Android's rapid rise made the competitive threat obvious, and the 2011 Windows Phone partnership was itself a bet on a third, ultimately unsuccessful ecosystem rather than joining the Android wave most competitors used to survive. Internally, bureaucratic decision-making and destructive competition between divisions reportedly blocked good ideas that existed inside the company from reaching the market. By the time Nokia acted decisively (2011 partnership announcement), the company had already lost the app-ecosystem and developer-mindshare war, meaning even a well-executed pivot couldn't recover lost ground — Lumia sales peaked at roughly 10.5 million units per quarter, a fraction of Apple's and Samsung's monthly volumes.
Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: The strategic decision point was years earlier than 2011 — a leading device maker needs a standing early-warning process for platform-level shifts (touchscreen UX, app ecosystems) and the organizational authority to act on it before market share erosion becomes irreversible, not just after a new CEO arrives and calls a crisis meeting. For ORM/reputation clients: this case shows that honest public acknowledgment of a crisis (unlike denial) can preserve stakeholder trust and enable a more dignified strategic exit, even when it can't reverse the underlying decline — transparency changes how a decline is remembered, even if it doesn't stop the decline itself.
Estimated Impact: Market value fell roughly 90% over six years; smartphone market share fell from over 50% (2007) to under 5% (2013); nearly 10,000 jobs cut by end of 2013; devices business sold to Microsoft for ~€5.44 billion (a fraction of Nokia's prior peak valuation); Nokia itself survived as a much smaller, infrastructure-focused company and remains a going concern today.
Onlyne Relevance: Crisis Comms — useful contrast case: not a scandal or breach, but a slow reputational decline, showing how transparent communication style affects legacy/brand perception even when it can't prevent the underlying business failure
Key Sources: INSEAD Knowledge, The Register, Tactyqal, CDO Times, Medium (Carsten Krause)
