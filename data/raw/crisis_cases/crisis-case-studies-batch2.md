## CASE-005

**Company:** Wells Fargo
**Industry:** Banking / Financial Services
**Year:** 2016 (misconduct spanned 2011–2015, publicly broke September 2016)
**Crisis Type:** Systemic internal fraud / sales practice scandal

**Trigger Event:** On September 8, 2016, the CFPB, OCC, and Los Angeles City Attorney fined Wells Fargo $185 million after finding that employees, under extreme sales quota pressure, had opened roughly 1.5 million unauthorized deposit accounts and over 500,000 unauthorized credit card accounts in customers' names without their knowledge, between 2011 and 2015.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, shifting to Delayed/Reactive under Congressional pressure

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 — This was not a single-day incident but a five-year pattern the bank had already fired roughly 5,300 employees over internally before the public fine, yet never disclosed the scale to the public or in regulatory filings until forced to by the joint CFPB/OCC/LA settlement. CEO John Stumpf's first instinct in a CNBC interview the same day the fine was announced was to reject calls to resign and frame it as isolated employee misconduct, not a systemic failure — a framing that collapsed within weeks under Senate testimony.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 — Stumpf initially blamed low-level employees ("bad apples") rather than the sales-quota system that incentivized the behavior; Senator Elizabeth Warren directly confronted him in a September 20, 2016 Senate hearing, telling him he should resign for having "squeezed employees to the breaking point so they would cheat customers." Under questioning, Stumpf admitted the bank had not disclosed the scale of the problem in regulatory filings until the summer of 2016 — three years after an LA Times investigation and a year after the LA City Attorney's own lawsuit had already surfaced the practice.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** CFPB enforcement action, OCC enforcement action, Servicemembers Civil Relief Act violations (separate $20M penalty), later a DOJ/SEC settlement. Regulators eventually imposed a Federal Reserve-mandated asset growth cap on the bank that lasted years.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — fines were paid and Stumpf was removed, but Wells Fargo's asset cap and reputational damage persisted for years, and additional scandal waves (auto-loan insurance, mortgage practices) kept surfacing through 2018.

**What Went Right:**
- Eventually fired 5,300 employees connected to the fraudulent account openings (though this happened quietly over years, before the public scandal broke, not as a visible response to it).
- Board clawed back $41 million in Stumpf's compensation and he was ultimately forced out on October 12, 2016 — about five weeks after the public fine.
- Announced it would eliminate the sales quotas that had driven the behavior.
- Refunded $2.6 million in fees to affected customers relatively early in the public phase.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Stumpf's first public posture was defensive, not accountable — refusing to consider resignation on CNBC the day the fine broke, then reversing course only after sustained congressional pressure.
- The "bad apples" framing (blaming low-level employees for what regulators described as a structural quota-driven incentive system) was directly contradicted by the CFPB's own findings and fell apart under questioning.
- Leadership had known about the problem internally for years (fired employees over it since at least 2011) without ever disclosing the scale publicly or in filings — this is the core transparency failure, not the initial fraud itself.
- Stumpf sold company stock during a period when he had already learned about the fake-account practices — he defended this as having "proper ethics approvals," but it echoed the same optics problem that damaged Equifax executives a year later.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** A five-year pattern of firing thousands of employees for the same misconduct should have triggered a root-cause review and voluntary public disclosure long before regulators forced it — internal awareness without external transparency is functionally concealment, even without an explicit cover-up. Leadership's first public response to a systemic scandal should acknowledge the system failure immediately, not frame it as isolated bad actors; that framing only works if it's actually true, and here it demonstrably wasn't.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: $185 million initial fine, $41 million compensation clawback from Stumpf, later years of Fed-mandated asset growth caps limiting the bank's ability to grow; stock dropped roughly 10% following the scandal's public break.
- Reputational: Became a multi-year drag — the bank faced a cascade of further disclosed scandals (auto insurance, mortgage practices) through 2018 that were widely read as evidence the culture problem was never actually fixed, not just a single bad episode.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM — strong case for clients on why "blame the employees, not the system" collapses under scrutiny, and why known-but-undisclosed problems are treated by regulators and the public as concealment.

## CASE-006

**Company:** Boeing
**Industry:** Aerospace / Aviation Manufacturing
**Year:** 2018–2019
**Crisis Type:** Product safety failure / mass-casualty design defect

**Trigger Event:** Lion Air Flight 610, a Boeing 737 MAX 8, crashed into the Java Sea on October 29, 2018, killing all 189 aboard; less than five months later, on March 10, 2019, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — the same aircraft model — crashed near Addis Ababa, killing all 157 aboard. Both were later tied to the same automated flight-control system, MCAS.

**Response Type:** Delayed/Denial-Silence, only becoming Reactive under regulatory and political pressure

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 — After the first crash, Boeing issued an operational bulletin eight days later but did not ground the fleet or disclose MCAS's full role. After the second, nearly identical crash five months later, Boeing continued to defend the aircraft's safety even as country after country grounded the plane; the U.S. FAA — Boeing's home regulator — was the last major regulator to ground the aircraft, doing so on March 13, 2019, three days after the second crash and only after nearly the entire rest of the world had already acted, reportedly under direct political pressure.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 — It later emerged Boeing had known from an internal 2018 FAA risk review that there was a "high risk" of future 737 MAX accidents but the plane was not grounded at the time. A subsequent 18-month U.S. House Transportation Committee investigation concluded Boeing could have prevented both crashes and found the FAA had delegated too much of its own safety oversight to Boeing itself. Whistleblower Ed Pierson testified he had formally warned leadership about production shortcuts multiple times and was ignored.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FAA airworthiness certification process, DOJ criminal investigation. Boeing entered a $2.5 billion deferred-prosecution agreement with the Department of Justice. The FAA's certification-delegation process was also formally reformed as a result.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — the aircraft was eventually re-certified and returned to service (November 2020, after 1 year, 8 months and 5 days grounded — 619 days), but a 2024 Alaska Airlines door-plug incident on the same aircraft family reopened safety and oversight questions, and the CEO installed after the 2019–2020 crisis (Dave Calhoun) also stepped down amid that later incident.

**What Went Right:**
- Eventually suspended 737 MAX production (January 2020) and replaced CEO Dennis Muilenburg with David Calhoun — genuine leadership accountability, though only after over a year of pressure.
- Cooperated with the FAA's re-certification process, including software and training fixes to MCAS.
- Entered the DOJ deferred-prosecution agreement rather than contesting criminal charges indefinitely.

**What Went Wrong:**
- After the first crash, Boeing's public and regulatory response stopped at a bulletin — it did not ground the aircraft or fully disclose MCAS's behavior, despite having internal risk data suggesting a "high risk" of another accident.
- After the second, nearly identical crash, Boeing continued defending the plane's safety publicly while most of the world's aviation regulators grounded it before the FAA did — the optics of the manufacturer's own home regulator being the *last*, not first, to act was devastating.
- Internal whistleblower warnings about production shortcuts were documented and ignored well before either crash.
- The core design and disclosure failure — a single-sensor-dependent automated system that could repeatedly and aggressively push the aircraft's nose down without adequate pilot training or disclosure — was a known internal risk, not a surprise.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** After the first fatal crash tied to a new automated system, the aircraft should have been grounded pending full investigation rather than addressed with an operational bulletin — a second nearly-identical crash killing hundreds more people less than five months later is the clearest possible evidence that the first response was inadequate. A manufacturer's home regulator being the last to ground a plane after a mass-casualty pattern is a signal that oversight has been captured, not reassurance that the plane is safe.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Direct costs estimated at $20 billion, indirect costs (production halts, order cancellations, compensation) estimated around $60 billion total; $2.5 billion DOJ settlement.
- Reputational: 346 deaths across two crashes; Boeing's safety reputation and FAA oversight credibility both took years to partially rebuild, and the 2024 door-plug incident reopened the same "does Boeing cut corners on safety" narrative, showing the reputational damage was never fully resolved.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms — one of the clearest cases for "the second incident is unforgivable if the first one wasn't fully addressed." Useful for any client in a regulated, safety-critical industry on why partial transparency after an initial incident is a bigger risk than it looks.

## CASE-007

**Company:** Uber
**Industry:** Ride-hailing / Technology
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Compounding culture and governance crisis (harassment, IP theft, regulatory, leadership)

**Trigger Event:** On February 19, 2017, former Uber engineer Susan Fowler published a blog post detailing sexual harassment and a dismissive HR response during her time at the company; the post went viral and triggered a cascade of separate scandals (a Waymo trade-secret lawsuit, a leaked video of CEO Travis Kalanick berating a driver, a #DeleteUber campaign) that compounded over the following four months.

**Response Type:** Reactive, eventually forced into a real governance response under investor pressure

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — Uber launched an "urgent investigation" the same day Fowler's post went viral and brought in former U.S. Attorney General Eric Holder within a day to lead an independent review — a genuinely fast initial move. But the underlying culture and leadership problems kept surfacing new incidents for four straight months (the Waymo lawsuit, the driver-berating video, additional executive departures over undisclosed misconduct), showing the initial investigation didn't address root causes fast enough to stop the bleeding.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 — The Holder investigation was genuinely independent and its 47 recommendations were made public; Uber did disclose executive departures tied to misconduct (e.g., SVP Amit Singhal, let go days after joining for failing to disclose a prior harassment finding at Google). But Kalanick himself minimized his own culpability for months, and it took board member pressure — not proactive disclosure — to force his resignation.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FTC investigation into Uber's privacy and driver-earnings claims (resulting in a separate $20 million FTC fine, announced the same week as the Fowler post), Waymo/Alphabet trade secrets lawsuit, New York court ruling on driver employment status.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved for the immediate crisis (leadership change occurred) but several of the underlying legal matters (Waymo suit, driver classification issues) continued for years afterward.

**What Went Right:**
- Same-day "urgent investigation" announcement after the Fowler post, with a credible independent figure (Eric Holder) brought in within 24 hours — this genuinely bought Uber some credibility in the first 48 hours.
- Publicly released the Holder report's 47 recommendations on June 13, 2017, rather than keeping it internal.
- Board ultimately acted: a board member (Bonderman) resigned the same day as the Holder report over his own sexist remark made during the board's review, showing the accountability extended beyond just Kalanick.
- Kalanick did eventually step down (June 20, 2017) under investor pressure rather than the company protecting him indefinitely.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The February harassment post was just the first domino — over the following four months, new negative stories kept surfacing (the Waymo lawsuit, the leaked driver-argument video, more executive departures over undisclosed misconduct) faster than Uber could contain them, suggesting the company was reacting incident-by-incident rather than addressing an underlying culture problem holistically.
- Kalanick's own conduct became a recurring liability — the leaked video of him berating a driver required a personal apology ("I need to grow up") that read as reactive rather than a sign of real change.
- The #DeleteUber campaign (over 200,000 account deletions) was triggered by a separate misstep — turning off surge pricing during a taxi strike protesting Trump's travel ban, seen as strikebreaking — showing the crisis response wasn't coordinated across the company's own decisions.
- It took four months of compounding scandals before the board finally forced Kalanick out, rather than recognizing early that leadership itself was the core problem.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** When a harassment allegation surfaces at a company with other known culture red flags, leadership should treat it as a signal to review the whole culture immediately — not wait for each subsequent scandal to force a bigger response. A fast initial investigation is good, but if new, unrelated scandals keep surfacing every few weeks afterward, that is itself evidence the response didn't go deep enough the first time.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Not as directly quantifiable as a fine-driven case, since Uber was privately held in 2017; over 200,000 account deletions during the #DeleteUber campaign; separate $20 million FTC fine over driver-earnings claims.
- Reputational: Became shorthand in tech-industry crisis reporting for "toxic startup culture"; Kalanick's ouster is still referenced as a case study in when a board finally moves against a founder-CEO.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM — strong case on compounding-incident dynamics and viral employee-testimony crises; relevant for clients facing culture-related social media backlash where one post triggers a cascade.

## CASE-008

**Company:** Facebook (now Meta)
**Industry:** Social Media / Technology
**Year:** 2018 (data collection occurred 2014–2015)
**Crisis Type:** Data privacy breach / third-party data misuse

**Trigger Event:** In March 2018, The Guardian and The New York Times revealed that political consulting firm Cambridge Analytica had improperly obtained the personal data of up to 87 million Facebook users via a personality-quiz app, and used it to build psychological voter profiles — data Facebook had actually first learned about back in 2015 but had only privately asked Cambridge Analytica to delete rather than disclosing the exposure to users or the public.

**Response Type:** Delayed, verging on Denial-Silence for the initial multi-year gap, then Reactive once public

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — Facebook first learned of the data misuse in 2015 but did not publicly disclose it — it only became public when journalists broke the story in March 2018, three years later. Once public, Zuckerberg took several days to respond personally; his first public statement acknowledging the issue came several days after the initial reports, and he did not testify before Congress until April 10-11, 2018 — roughly a month after the story broke.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — The core transparency failure predates the 2018 news cycle: Facebook knew about the improper data harvesting in 2015 and handled it as a private matter with Cambridge Analytica (asking them to delete the data, without verifying they had) rather than notifying affected users or regulators. Once the story was public, Facebook did cooperate with the FTC investigation and Zuckerberg testified publicly, but the SEC separately found Facebook had misled investors about the risk of user data misuse, resulting in an additional $100 million SEC penalty layered on top of the FTC fine.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FTC's 2011 consent decree (the 2018 scandal was investigated specifically as a possible violation of that earlier agreement), SEC disclosure rules, congressional hearings under multiple committees.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved financially (settlements paid) but the underlying trust and regulatory relationship remained damaged for years — Meta paid a further $725 million in December 2022 to settle a related class-action privacy suit, showing the legal tail of this case ran nearly five years past the original story.

**What Went Right:**
- Zuckerberg did personally testify before Congress (April 2018) rather than sending a deputy, and again in 2020 as part of the FTC case — visible personal accountability at the top.
- Facebook did restrict third-party API access to friend data as part of the eventual settlement, addressing the actual technical vulnerability that allowed the harvesting.
- Agreed to new FTC-mandated privacy governance structures, including an independent privacy committee Zuckerberg does not control — a real structural change, not just a fine.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The core failure was the 2015 decision to handle the initial discovery privately (asking Cambridge Analytica to delete the data) instead of notifying users or regulators — this three-year gap between knowledge and disclosure is the single biggest transparency failure in the case, comparable to Equifax's 40-day gap but far longer.
- Facebook did not verify Cambridge Analytica had actually deleted the harvested data, and it turned out they hadn't — a due-diligence failure on top of the disclosure failure.
- The SEC separately found Facebook had misled investors about the risk of this kind of misuse even after knowing about it, adding a securities-fraud dimension on top of the privacy failure.
- Public usage data suggested the platform faced little immediate user attrition despite the scandal, which arguably reduced the internal urgency to respond faster — a caution that "the backlash didn't hurt usage" doesn't mean the regulatory and legal exposure wasn't severe.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The moment Facebook learned in 2015 that a third-party app had improperly harvested and shared tens of millions of users' data, that should have triggered mandatory user notification and regulatory disclosure at the time — not a private request to delete the data taken on trust. Verifying deletion, rather than just requesting it, was a basic due-diligence step that was skipped and became a major aggravating factor once it was discovered years later that the data still existed.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: $5 billion FTC fine (largest ever levied by the FTC against a tech company at the time), $100 million SEC penalty, a further $725 million Meta class-action settlement in 2022 — over $5.8 billion in total direct costs across all related settlements.
- Reputational: Became the defining case study for "data privacy scandal" in tech for years afterward; directly shaped the "Delete Facebook" movement and accelerated broader public and regulatory scrutiny of social media data practices worldwide (contributing to the environment that produced GDPR-style enforcement attention).

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — highly relevant for data-misuse and third-party-app disclosure cases; the "we knew but didn't tell anyone" pattern is directly comparable to Equifax and useful for any client-facing conversation about why early voluntary disclosure beats being caught by journalists years later.
