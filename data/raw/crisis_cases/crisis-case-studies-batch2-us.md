## Case 21

**Company:** Wells Fargo
**Industry:** Banking / Financial Services
**Year:** 2016 (roots back to 2002; fallout continued through 2020)
**Crisis Type:** Fraud / Unauthorized account creation / Sales culture failure

**Trigger Event:** On September 8, 2016, the CFPB, OCC, and Los Angeles City Attorney announced $185 million in fines after finding Wells Fargo employees had opened roughly 1.5 million unauthorized deposit accounts and 565,000+ unauthorized credit card accounts to hit aggressive cross-selling sales quotas. The number later grew to an estimated 3.5 million accounts.

**Response Type:** Reactive, sliding into Delayed
**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Consumer Financial Protection Act (CFPB enforcement), OCC safety-and-soundness authority, Dodd-Frank, later DOJ and SEC fraud investigations, Federal Reserve growth restriction order.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- Wells Fargo did eventually pay the $185M settlement without contesting the CFPB's findings, avoiding a prolonged public legal fight in the first weeks.
- The bank ended product sales quotas for retail bankers shortly after the settlement, removing the incentive structure that caused the fraud.
- CEO John Stumpf was eventually pushed out (October 12, 2016) and the board clawed back $41 million of his unvested equity — a rare instance of a major bank publicly penalizing a sitting CEO's compensation.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Stumpf's first public response was denial of systemic failure: he told CNBC on September 13, 2016 (5 days after the settlement) that he had no plans to resign and framed the fraud as the work of "1%" of employees, not a cultural or leadership problem.
- The bank had already fired roughly 5,300 low-level employees over the preceding years without disclosing the scale of the practice, so when the CFPB order became public, it looked like the company had known and stayed quiet rather than escalating internally.
- At his September 20, 2016 Senate Banking Committee hearing (12 days after the settlement), Stumpf was unable to answer basic questions about which executives knew what and when, which turned a policy story into a personal credibility crisis.
- The internal Sales Practices Investigation Report, released in April 2017, showed the board and senior executives had known about the problem as far back as 2002 — 14 years of institutional awareness before any public accountability. That single fact retroactively destroyed the credibility of every earlier "isolated incident" statement.
- The number of fake accounts kept growing after the original disclosure (from ~2.1 million to 3.5 million by 2017), which read as a company that had not done a real internal accounting before going public, or had done one and understated it.
- Additional misconduct kept surfacing for two more years (forced auto insurance charged to ~570,000 customers in 2017, wrongful mortgage fee charges to 110,000 customers in 2017), each one reopening the story and signaling the first response had not actually fixed the underlying culture.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Once the CFPB order was public, Wells Fargo needed a same-week statement acknowledging the practice was structural (driven by the cross-selling sales targets), not employee-level misconduct, paired with an independent third-party audit announced immediately rather than emerging piecemeal over the next 18 months. The CEO should not have gone on television to defend the sales culture five days after a $185M regulatory finding — that appearance is what converted a regulatory story into a personal accountability story. A single, complete account of scope (accounts affected, years involved, executive awareness) delivered in the first 30 days would have prevented the repeated "new revelation" news cycles that ran from 2016 into 2018.

**Estimated Impact:** $185 million initial CFPB/OCC/LA settlement; a further $1 billion CFPB/OCC fine in 2018 for auto-insurance and mortgage abuses; a $3 billion DOJ/SEC settlement in 2020; a Federal Reserve-imposed asset growth cap that held Wells Fargo's balance sheet near $1.95 trillion for roughly seven years (lifted in 2025); tens of millions in executive compensation clawbacks; loss of its position as the most valuable U.S. bank to JPMorgan Chase in 2016.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / Search Results Management — this is a textbook example of how a company's own public statements ("isolated incident," "1% of employees") become the most-searched, most-cited counter-evidence once a fuller record emerges. Any client statement made in week one should be treated as a permanent, re-surfaceable asset.

**Key Sources:** Congressional Research Service (congress.gov) timeline of the Wells Fargo scandal; Wikipedia "Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal"; CBS News/AP reporting on the 3.5 million account revision; Yahoo Finance "Wells Fargo scandals: The complete timeline"; ABC News timeline of the accounts scandal.

## Case 22

**Company:** Theranos
**Industry:** Healthcare / Medical Diagnostics (Biotech)
**Year:** 2015–2018
**Crisis Type:** Product/technology fraud, investor fraud, regulatory violation

**Trigger Event:** On October 15, 2015, Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published an investigation showing Theranos's "Edison" blood-testing device did not work as claimed, and that most of the company's tests were secretly run on standard commercial lab equipment rather than its proprietary technology.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to legal intimidation
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** CMS/CLIA laboratory certification standards, SEC securities fraud statutes, DOJ criminal wire fraud statutes.

**Resolution Status:** Fatal

**What Went Right:**
- After FDA questions, the company did stop using its "nanotainer" blood-collection devices for most tests within a day of the article (October 16, 2015) — one of the only fast, concrete actions taken.
- Founder Elizabeth Holmes made herself available for media interviews rather than going completely silent in the first days, which at least kept the company in the conversation rather than appearing to hide (though the content of those interviews was denial, not disclosure).

**What Went Wrong:**
- Theranos's core response strategy was to discredit the reporter rather than address the substance of the allegations. The company enlisted high-profile attorney David Boies to threaten legal action against Carreyrou and the Journal, and pressured former employees who had spoken to him.
- Holmes publicly called the allegations "shocked"-inducing but did not produce data, third-party validation, or independent audits to rebut the specific technical claims — the response was reputational defense, not evidentiary defense.
- A CMS inspection of the company's Newark, California lab (conducted fall 2015) found conditions posing "immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety." Theranos's response was to claim the inspection was outdated and "does not reflect the current state of the lab" rather than to disclose what had actually been wrong.
- Walgreens, a major retail partner, halted Theranos testing at its Palo Alto center and suspended use of the Newark lab in the weeks following the CMS letter — a partner-driven credibility collapse that the company could not spin around.
- The company continued operating, testing patients, and raising money for roughly three more years after the fraud was substantially exposed, which is what escalated the case from a reputational crisis to criminal fraud charges.
- Employees who raised internal concerns (including Tyler Shultz, grandson of board member George Shultz) were threatened with litigation rather than heard — turning whistleblowers into hostile witnesses for the prosecution.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The moment CMS found "immediate jeopardy" conditions, an honest company halts patient testing and commissions an independent, published technical audit of its device accuracy — not a legal campaign against the journalist who found the problem. Legal intimidation of a credentialed reporter and named former employees is close to the single worst move available in a credibility crisis: it signals guilt, invites more sourcing, and converts sympathetic ex-employees into cooperating witnesses. The only defensible path once technical claims are challenged is producing third-party validation data; Theranos never did, because it could not.

**Resolution Status detail:** Theranos dissolved in September 2018. Holmes was convicted on four counts of fraud in January 2022 and sentenced to over 11 years in prison; former COO/President Sunny Balwani was convicted separately and sentenced to nearly 13 years.

**Estimated Impact:** Valuation collapsed from a peak of $9 billion to $0; Holmes's estimated paper net worth of $4.7 billion was wiped out; investors lost more than $700 million; criminal convictions and prison sentences for both top executives; the company's name is now a standard shorthand in Silicon Valley for founder-driven fraud.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — the defining lesson here is negative: using legal threats against journalists or whistleblowers as a first-line reputation defense is high-risk and frequently backfires by validating the story. This case is a strong reference point for advising clients against aggressive suppression tactics when facing credible investigative claims, and for personality-rights context around how thoroughly a founder's personal brand can be destroyed once it was built entirely on unverifiable claims.

**Key Sources:** John Carreyrou / Wall Street Journal original reporting (Oct 15, 2015); MedCity News "Theranos Doomsday Clock" timeline; businessjournalism.org account of the WSJ investigation process; Fast Company retrospective on Theranos's initial response to the exposé; Ethics Unwrapped (UT Austin) case summary.

## Case 24

**Company:** WeWork (The We Company)
**Industry:** Commercial Real Estate / Co-working / Tech-adjacent
**Year:** 2019
**Crisis Type:** Governance failure, valuation collapse, founder self-dealing

**Trigger Event:** WeWork's IPO registration (S-1), filed August 14, 2019, publicly disclosed nearly $700 million in losses for the first half of 2019 alongside extensive governance red flags — including CEO Adam Neumann's supervoting shares, his personal ownership of buildings leased back to the company, and a $5.9 million payment the company made to him for rights to the "We" trademark.

**Response Type:** Reactive
**Response Speed Score:** 2/5
**Transparency Score:** 2/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** SEC S-1 registration and disclosure requirements (no major SEC enforcement action against the company itself; the crisis played out through public/investor scrutiny of disclosures rather than a regulatory violation finding).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved (company survived short-term via a SoftBank bailout, later filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in November 2023)

**What Went Right:**
- WeWork did make real governance concessions once pressure mounted: it reduced Neumann's supervoting share ratio from 20-to-1 to 10-to-1, removed a provision letting his spouse choose his successor, and had Neumann return the $5.9 million trademark payment.
- The board acted on removing Neumann relatively decisively once institutional investors made clear the IPO could not proceed with him as CEO — he resigned September 24, 2019, six weeks after the S-1 was filed.
- New co-CEOs Artie Minson and Sebastian Gunningham immediately began visible cost-cutting (selling Neumann's private jet, exploring sales of side businesses), signaling a break from the prior spending culture.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The governance concessions came out one at a time over roughly three weeks, each one following a specific press report and investor complaint rather than being bundled into one proactive reform package. This drip-feed pattern made the company look like it was being forced into each change rather than leading with a plan.
- The company's S-1 leaned on a non-standard metric ("community-adjusted EBITDA") that stripped out major categories of normal operating cost — a presentation choice that read as an attempt to obscure the severity of the losses rather than disclose them plainly, and it became a symbol of the whole episode.
- WeWork proceeded with roadshow plans even as the marketed valuation collapsed from $47 billion to as low as $10 billion, prolonging the public spectacle instead of pausing to reset expectations early.
- Neumann remained non-executive chairman after stepping down as CEO rather than fully exiting, which kept the self-dealing narrative alive in coverage of the withdrawal.
- The IPO was formally withdrawn on September 30, 2019 — by which point WeWork had spent roughly six weeks as a daily negative news story, with each governance disclosure treated as new evidence rather than resolved history.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Related-party transactions of this scale (a CEO personally leasing buildings back to his own company, personally profiting from a trademark sale to the company he controls) should be resolved or fully unwound before any public filing, not disclosed and then walked back reactively during a live roadshow. Once investor pushback started, a single comprehensive governance overhaul — voting rights, board composition, related-party transactions, and CEO role — announced together would have signaled control of the situation. Releasing changes incrementally in response to each new negative story is the single clearest self-inflicted wound in this case.

**Estimated Impact:** Valuation collapsed from $47 billion (January 2019) to under $8 billion in a SoftBank rescue by the end of 2019, with SoftBank taking roughly 80% control; Neumann received an exit package reported at up to $1.7 billion despite the collapse, which itself became a secondary reputational story; the company went public via SPAC merger in October 2021 and filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in November 2023, with shares trading near $0.84 at filing.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Personality Rights / Crisis Comms — WeWork is a strong case for founder-brand-as-company-brand risk: Neumann's personal financial conduct became inseparable from the company's investment story, and reactive, sequential concessions amplified rather than contained the damage. Useful reference for clients where a single high-profile individual's conduct is the central reputational liability.

**Key Sources:** Forbes "WeWork's Rise To $47 Billion — And Fall To Bankruptcy: A Timeline"; Reuters/Yahoo Finance "TIMELINE-From prized startup to possible bankruptcy"; CNBC reporting on the S-1 withdrawal (Sept 30, 2019); TechCrunch reporting on the IPO delay; Harvard Business School case analysis of the WeWork S-1 (referenced via secondary reporting).

## Case 25

**Company:** Uber Technologies
**Industry:** Ride-hailing / Technology
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Workplace culture crisis (sexual harassment, discrimination), leadership crisis

**Trigger Event:** On February 19, 2017, former Uber engineer Susan Fowler published a personal blog post detailing sustained sexual harassment by her manager and HR's refusal to act on her complaints, arriving on top of an existing #DeleteUber boycott from January 2017.

**Response Type:** Reactive (fast initial move, slow underlying fix)
**Response Speed Score:** 3/5
**Transparency Score:** 3/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EEOC workplace discrimination standards, FTC enforcement (a separate $20 million FTC fine over driver-earnings claims landed in the same news cycle), DOJ investigation into Uber's "Greyball" regulator-evasion software, New York employment classification ruling on driver benefits.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (leadership change completed; reputational recovery took years)

**What Went Right:**
- CEO Travis Kalanick responded to the Fowler post within 24 hours, calling the allegations "abhorrent" and launching an "urgent investigation" the same news cycle.
- The next day, February 20, 2017, Uber announced it had retained former U.S. Attorney General Eric Holder to lead an independent investigation — a credible, named third party rather than an internal-only review.
- The Holder investigation produced a public report on June 13, 2017, with 47 concrete recommendations, and Uber disclosed that more than 20 employees had already been fired as a result of the internal review by early June — a rare case of a company publishing specific disciplinary numbers.
- Board member David Bonderman resigned the same day as the Holder report (June 13, 2017) after making a sexist remark during the board's discussion of the findings — a fast, visible consequence rather than a quiet exit.
- The company ultimately replaced Kalanick with Dara Khosrowshahi in August 2017, bringing in outside leadership explicitly to reset the culture.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The "urgent investigation" pledge was fast, but it took four full months — from February 19 to June 20, 2017 — for Kalanick to actually leave the CEO role, during which a continuous stream of additional negative stories broke (a leaked video of Kalanick berating an Uber driver, revelations of an executive trip to a South Korean escort bar, an internal "sex memo," and executives allegedly mishandling a rape victim's medical records).
- Kalanick's response to the driver-argument video (Feb 28, 2017) was defensive before it became apologetic, initially telling the driver "some people don't like to take responsibility for their own shit" before later apologizing and saying he needed to "grow up."
- Kalanick did not resign voluntarily; he left only after five of Uber's largest investors directly demanded his removal on June 20, 2017 — meaning the final, most consequential step was forced rather than led.
- Each new revelation during the four-month window reset the news cycle, so the company never got credit for the fast initial response — by the time Kalanick left, the story was already framed as "months too late" rather than "swift accountability."

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The 24-hour acknowledgment and next-day independent investigator hire were genuinely good crisis practice and are worth preserving as a model. The failure was afterward: leadership needed to get ahead of the additional culture stories (the driver video, the escort-bar trip, the sex memo) with disclosure and consequences on Uber's own timeline, rather than letting each one break independently in the press over four months. A company that has already announced an independent investigation should use that credibility to proactively surface and address further problems, not wait for reporters to find them one at a time.

**Estimated Impact:** More than a dozen senior executives departed Uber within the following year; the #DeleteUber campaign in January 2017 was tied to over 200,000 app account deletions; company valuation and IPO prospects were affected, with Uber's eventual 2019 public offering priced well below earlier private-market expectations; Kalanick fully exited the board in December 2019 after selling over 90% of his remaining shares.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / Search Results Management — this is a strong reference for advising clients that a fast first statement does not "bank" credibility if it is followed by a slow drip of further bad news; search results and public narrative get anchored to the most recent negative story, not the earliest good-faith response. Also relevant to personality-rights and executive-conduct cases given the viral dashcam video's role in the crisis.

**Key Sources:** CNBC "Uber timeline: Why did CEO Travis Kalanick leave?"; Fortune "Every Event That Led to Uber CEO Travis Kalanick's Resignation"; Business & Human Rights Resource Centre summary of the Holder investigation outcome; PBS NewsHour/AP reporting on Kalanick's resignation and later board exit.
