## Case ID: 09
**Company:** Carillion plc
**Industry:** Construction / Facilities Management / Government Outsourcing
**Year:** 2017–2018
**Crisis Type:** Financial collapse / Corporate governance failure / Insolvency

**Trigger Event:** On 10 July 2017, Carillion issued a shock profit warning disclosing an £845m write-down on contracts, wiping out roughly seven years of accumulated profit. Two more profit warnings followed (September, November 2017), and on 15 January 2018 the company collapsed into compulsory liquidation with £1.5bn in debt and a £2.6bn pension deficit.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence (in the lead-up) → Reactive (post-collapse)

**Response Speed Score:** 1
Carillion had been aware of deteriorating contract performance for years before the July 2017 warning. Even after the first warning, the board kept paying dividends and issuing reassurances up to weeks before liquidation. There was no proactive disclosure — each bad number was forced out by accounting reviews or lenders, not volunteered.

**Transparency Score:** 1
A UK parliamentary inquiry later found that Carillion's accounts were "systematically manipulated" to present an optimistic picture, in defiance of internal controls. The finance director of a decade was described by MPs as the architect of these policies. No independent investigation was initiated by the company itself — it took the Official Receiver and later the FCA years to establish what had actually happened.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** UK Insolvency Act 1986 (compulsory liquidation); Financial Reporting Council and FCA investigations into directors and auditors (KPMG); parliamentary Work and Pensions / BEIS Select Committee joint inquiry; FCA enforcement action (in 2026, former CEO Richard Howson was fined £237,000 and former finance directors Richard Adam and Zafar Khan were fined £232,800 and £138,900 respectively for misleading investors).

**Resolution Status:** Fatal (company ceased to exist; shareholders wiped out; 43,000 jobs and thousands of supply-chain SMEs affected)

**What Went Right:**
- The UK government activated Cobra crisis meetings and contingency plans within hours of liquidation, which — because contingency planning had quietly started in July 2017 and accelerated from October 2017 — kept public services (schools, hospitals, prisons) running without interruption.
- Once collapse was inevitable, the Official Receiver and Special Managers moved fast (liquidation order 15 January, fast-tracked conduct investigation announced 16 January) to protect creditors and manage an orderly wind-down.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Management continued paying dividends (£79m in the year of collapse alone, and roughly £554m/£333m net cash-gap over the preceding years, depending on the source) even as cash generation deteriorated — signalling stability that didn't exist.
- The board treated a July 2017 profit warning as a one-off shock rather than disclosing the systemic contract-costing problems behind it; two further warnings (September, November) each came as fresh surprises to markets rather than tracking a previously flagged risk.
- No credible, board-led narrative of "what happened and what we're doing about it" was ever issued; communications were reactive statements timed to results announcements, not a proactive crisis-comms plan.
- Auditor and internal-control failures were not surfaced by the company; they were dug out later by select committees and regulators.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
A construction/outsourcing group carrying this much contract risk needed real-time, honest contract-provisioning disclosure the moment margin erosion was detected internally — not an "enhanced review" triggered only when a covenant was at risk. The board should have suspended dividends and commissioned an independent forensic review of contract accounting the moment the first amber risk signals appeared (well before July 2017), and communicated that review publicly. For an ORM/crisis-comms team: the lesson is that the "quiet drip of bad news" strategy destroys more trust than one hard, complete disclosure — each subsequent profit warning compounded reputational damage because each one contradicted the reassurance that preceded it.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: ~£7bn in total liabilities; shares lost ~70% of value in the two days after the first warning and were effectively worthless by liquidation; £2.6bn pension deficit became the largest single loss in the UK Pension Protection Fund's history.
- Reputational: Became the byword in the UK for outsourcing-model failure; triggered a national political debate about government reliance on large outsourcers (Jeremy Corbyn called it a "watershed moment"); lasting damage to trust in PFI/PPP contracting model and in "Big Four" audit oversight (KPMG faced its own record fine).

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM — textbook case of reputational contagion from a drip-fed disclosure strategy; relevant to any client managing investor and public-sector stakeholder communications during a solvency crisis.

**Key Sources:**
- National Audit Office, "Investigation into the government's handling of the collapse of Carillion" (June 2018)
- House of Commons Library, "The collapse of Carillion," CBP-8206 (2018) and "Carillion collapse: what went wrong?"
- UK Parliament, Work and Pensions / BEIS Select Committees joint inquiry report (2018)
- The Corporate Governance Institute, "What happened to Carillion?" (FCA fines, 2026)
- Reuters/Fortune, BBC News contemporaneous coverage (January–November 2017–2018)

## Case ID: 10
**Company:** Samsung Electronics (Galaxy Note 7 — European recall)
**Industry:** Consumer Electronics
**Year:** 2016
**Crisis Type:** Product safety recall / Battery fire hazard

**Trigger Event:** Following the Galaxy Note 7's launch on 19 August 2016, multiple units — including many issued as "safe" replacements — began overheating and catching fire due to battery design and manufacturing defects, triggering a global recall.

**Response Type:** Reactive, then Proactive once the scale became clear

**Response Speed Score:** 3
Samsung had not yet formally launched the Note 7 for retail sale in most of Europe (only pre-order units had shipped) when the first recall was announced on 2 September 2016, so Europe avoided the worst of the initial crisis. However, Samsung's first "fix" (a battery swap announced in September) turned out to be inadequate — replacement units also caught fire — forcing a second, global recall on 13 October 2016 and full discontinuation. The need for a second recall after declaring the problem solved is itself a serious speed-and-accuracy failure, even though the second response (full production halt) was decisive once it came.

**Transparency Score:** 4
Once the scale of the problem was undeniable, Samsung was unusually transparent for a major electronics firm: it published a public engineering investigation into the root cause (battery design and manufacturing flaws across two separate suppliers), took a $5.3bn charge, and briefed regulators and press extensively. Samsung's Europe marketing chief (David Lowes) gave regular, specific public updates on exchange-programme progress (e.g., "57% of affected units swapped" by late October 2016). The main transparency failure was the initial (September) claim that replacement units were safe when they were not.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU General Product Safety Directive / national consumer-safety regulators in France, Germany and the UK (the three largest European markets for Note 7 pre-orders); US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) recall coordinated in parallel; airline and transport bans (EU aviation authorities banned the device on flights, mirroring FAA/DOT action).

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (for Samsung as a company); Fatal (for the Note 7 product line, which was permanently discontinued)

**What Went Right:**
- Samsung published a detailed, engineering-led root-cause report explaining the battery failures came from two independent supplier defects — this scientific transparency helped rebuild trust faster than a vague apology would have.
- In Europe specifically, Samsung pushed a software update capping battery charge at 60% to reduce fire risk while the exchange programme was ongoing, and used a visual marker (green battery icon) so consumers and retailers could distinguish safe from unsafe units at a glance.
- Regular, quantified public updates on exchange programme progress (percentage of units swapped, target relaunch dates) gave media and regulators a concrete, verifiable narrative to report instead of speculation.
- Samsung Europe held back the full relaunch (initially planned for 28 October) explicitly pending completion of the exchange programme, prioritising safety optics over speed to market.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The September "fix" was announced and executed before the true root cause was understood, and replacement units also failed — this second failure, after Samsung had told the world the problem was solved, is the single biggest reputational cost of the crisis.
- Europe's launch had already been delayed once (from 2 September) due to the initial recall, compounding consumer skepticism before the product even reached the mass market.
- Coordination across different national telecom carriers and retailers in Europe (UK, Germany, France) was uneven, leading to inconsistent consumer messaging about exchange deadlines.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Before declaring a fix, Samsung needed independent, adversarial third-party validation of the replacement battery — not just internal QA sign-off — given the severity (fire risk) and the fact the first batch had already failed catastrophically. For any client managing a two-stage product recall: never announce "problem solved" until an external lab or regulator has stress-tested the fix under real-world conditions; a second failure after a public "all clear" costs far more credibility than a longer, more cautious first fix would have.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Samsung booked an estimated $5.3bn charge related to the crisis; roughly 2.5–3 million units recalled worldwide.
- Reputational: Short-to-medium-term brand damage was real but ultimately contained — Samsung's Galaxy S8 launch the following year performed strongly, suggesting the transparent, engineering-led response protected long-term brand equity despite the acute damage.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM / Legal Takedown (product-safety recall messaging, search-result management during an active safety crisis, coordinating carrier/retailer messaging across multiple European markets).

**Key Sources:**
- BBC News, "Galaxy Note 7: Timeline of Samsung's phone woes" (2016)
- Fortune/Reuters, "Here's the Timeline of Samsung's Galaxy Note 7 Recall Crisis" (October 2016)
- US CPSC official recall notices (15 September 2016; 13 October 2016)

## Case ID: 11
**Company:** Parmalat S.p.A.
**Industry:** Food & Beverage (Dairy)
**Year:** 2003
**Crisis Type:** Accounting fraud / Corporate collapse ("Europe's Enron")

**Trigger Event:** On 19 December 2003, Parmalat publicly admitted that a €3.95–4bn cash balance held by its Cayman Islands subsidiary Bonlat at Bank of America did not exist. The company had defaulted days earlier on a routine €150m bond redemption despite claiming billions in liquid assets on its balance sheet.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, followed by forced Reactive disclosure

**Response Speed Score:** 1
The fraud had been running since roughly 1990 — 13 years of falsified accounts, fictitious assets, and a complex web of offshore subsidiaries used to hide debt. The company only admitted the truth when it was cornered: Bank of America refused to confirm the Cayman Islands account existed after being asked to verify it, and auditors (Grant Thornton, later Deloitte) found irregularities that could no longer be concealed. There was no voluntary early disclosure at any point.

**Transparency Score:** 1
Founder and CEO Calisto Tanzi denied any knowledge or orchestration of fraud even during his trial, describing the situation as a "house of mirrors" caused by others. Full transparency only came from external forces: prosecutors, a court-appointed administrator (Enrico Bondi), and criminal investigators — not from voluntary company disclosure or an independent board-commissioned investigation.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Italian bankruptcy/insolvency law (extraordinary administration, "Marzano Law" fast-track bankruptcy procedure enacted partly in response to this case); Italian criminal prosecution for fraud, fraudulent bankruptcy and criminal association; US SEC civil fraud suit alleging Parmalat wrongfully induced over $1.5bn from American investors; multiple civil suits against banks (including Bank of America, Citigroup) across several jurisdictions.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — the company itself was restructured and relisted in 2005 under new management (Enrico Bondi), and pursued recovery litigation against banks for years afterward; Tanzi and other executives were criminally convicted, but over 135,000 small retail bondholders lost most of their invested savings.

**What Went Right:**
- Once the truth surfaced, the Italian government moved quickly to appoint an independent administrator (Enrico Bondi) and fast-tracked new insolvency legislation, which enabled the underlying dairy business to keep operating and eventually relist rather than liquidate entirely — unusual for a fraud of this scale.
- Post-scandal management aggressively pursued recovery litigation against the banks that had facilitated or enabled the fraud, recovering significant settlements over the following decade.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Thirteen years of systematically falsified accounts went undetected/unreported by internal controls, auditors, and the board — a near-total governance failure at every level.
- Management response even after the crisis broke was denial rather than cooperation: Tanzi's public statements continued to deflect blame onto banks and "distorted" finances rather than acknowledging the fraud.
- No proactive investor communication or crisis plan existed; the collapse played out almost entirely through leaks, forced disclosures, and prosecutorial action rather than company-led disclosure.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
This is fundamentally a governance and audit-integrity failure, not a communications failure that better messaging could have fixed — but for an ORM/crisis-comms team advising on the aftermath, the lesson is that public denial by a founder-CEO after criminal fraud has been confirmed by regulators (as Tanzi did) actively worsens both legal exposure and reputational recovery. Once fraud is confirmed by external investigators, continued denial from leadership should be treated as a distinct reputational crisis on top of the original one — successors (like Bondi) who publicly and completely separate themselves from prior leadership's narrative recover trust faster.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: €14bn hole in the balance sheet — the largest corporate bankruptcy in European history at the time; over 135,000 small retail bondholders affected.
- Reputational: Became a landmark case in European corporate governance reform, directly influencing Italian securities law and auditor-oversight rules; the Parmalat name itself survived (post-restructuring) but the Tanzi family's reputation was permanently destroyed.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown — relevant as a historical benchmark for the reputational cost of prolonged concealment versus early, board-led disclosure; also relevant to personality-rights/legacy management questions given Tanzi's later public rehabilitation attempts before his 2022 death.

**Key Sources:**
- Reuters/Euronews, "Calisto Tanzi, Parmalat founder convicted over huge 2003 bankruptcy, dies at 83" (2022)
- Wikipedia, "Parmalat bankruptcy timeline" (sourced to contemporaneous Reuters/AP/BBC reporting)
- Al Jazeera, "Parmalat founder convicted of fraud" (2008)
- Directors' Institute, "Parmalat Scandal" case summary

## Case ID: 12
**Company:** Banco Popular Español
**Industry:** Banking / Financial Services
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Bank run / Solvency crisis / Regulatory resolution

**Trigger Event:** After new chairman Emilio Saracho (appointed December 2016) disclosed unexpected additional provisions and confirmed the sudden departure of the CEO on 3 April 2017, depositor and institutional confidence collapsed. Accelerating deposit outflows and press reports that the bank had approached rivals for an emergency takeover triggered a full-blown bank run; on 6 June 2017 the ECB declared Banco Popular "failing or likely to fail," and on 7 June the Single Resolution Board sold it to Banco Santander for €1.

**Response Type:** Delayed / Denial, then forced Reactive resolution

**Response Speed Score:** 2
Warning signs (rating downgrades, provisioning problems) were visible from February 2017, and deposit outflows were already material by April. Management's public posture throughout April–May was reassurance and denial even as the situation deteriorated — Saracho told press in May the bank had "liquidity until the end of the year" and denied reports of an emergency sale process, statements that were undercut within weeks by the actual resolution. The formal resolution process itself, once triggered, moved extremely fast: the entire execution occurred between roughly 15:30 on Tuesday 6 June and market open on Wednesday 7 June — a same-day, overnight resolution designed precisely to avoid a disorderly public bank run.

**Transparency Score:** 2
Contradictory public statements from the chairman (denying an urgent sale process while simultaneously running one) damaged credibility. The resolution itself was executed with minimal public disclosure beforehand, by design (any advance signal would have accelerated the run further) — but this meant depositors, retail shareholders and subordinated bondholders had no real warning their instruments would be wiped to zero. Post-resolution, bondholder and shareholder groups spent over a year in legal battles specifically to force disclosure of the valuation reports underlying the decision, with lawyers publicly criticising the SRB, Spanish government and Santander for "continuing their dedicated effort to avoid transparency."

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD) and SRM Regulation (EU) 806/2014 — this was the Single Resolution Board's first-ever resolution action; Spanish Law 11/2015 on recovery and resolution of credit institutions; FROB (Spanish national resolution authority) implementation; subsequent EU General Court and Spanish Audiencia Nacional litigation from wiped-out AT1/Tier 2 bondholders and shareholders.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (from a systemic/depositor-protection standpoint — no depositor lost money, no public funds used, the bank continued operating the next morning as part of Santander) but Partially Resolved/Unresolved for equity and subordinated bondholders, who were wiped out and pursued years of litigation seeking transparency and compensation.

**What Went Right:**
- The resolution mechanism itself worked exactly as EU regulators intended: no depositor lost a euro, no taxpayer money was used, and the bank reopened as a Santander subsidiary the next business day with zero interruption to customers — a genuine success for the post-2008 EU resolution framework's core objective (financial stability).
- Santander committed immediately to a €7bn capital raise to absorb Popular without weakening its own solvency, giving the market a credible backstop.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Chairman Saracho's public denials of an emergency sale process in May 2017, contradicted by the actual outcome weeks later, destroyed management credibility with the exact audience (depositors and institutional counterparties) most likely to panic — arguably accelerating the run rather than calming it.
- Communications were reactive and defensive throughout: "urgent" language mixed with reassurance ("deposits are not in danger") sent mixed signals that increased uncertainty rather than reducing it.
- Equity and AT1/Tier2 holders — many of them retail investors who had bought shares in a 2016 rights issue on management's assurance the bank was solvent — received no warning and no meaningful post-resolution transparency, fueling over a year of litigation and lasting reputational damage to the resolution authorities as well as the bank.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Once a chairman publicly uses the word "urgent" about a bank's situation, denial of parallel sale talks is not a viable communications strategy — it will be exposed and will do more damage than a calibrated, honest acknowledgment that "the board is exploring all strategic options to strengthen the balance sheet." For an ORM/crisis-comms team advising financial institutions: bank crises require pre-agreed dual-track communications (regulatory-mandated confidentiality before resolution, paired with pre-drafted stakeholder messaging ready to deploy the moment resolution is triggered) — Banco Popular had neither, leaving retail shareholders to learn their investment was worthless from the morning news.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Independent valuation estimated the bank's value at between -€2bn and -€8.2bn; shareholders and AT1/Tier2 holders (roughly €2bn in AT1/Tier2 instruments alone) were wiped to zero; share price had fallen from over €1 to €0.75 in the days immediately before resolution amid the run.
- Reputational: Lasting damage to Banco Popular's brand (dissolved into Santander); reputational cost also fell on Spain's banking sector broadly and on the SRB itself, which faced sustained "lack of transparency" criticism from bondholder groups and legal commentators well into 2018 and beyond.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM — a clear case study in how leadership statements that later prove false (even if well-intentioned reassurance) accelerate rather than contain a stakeholder panic; also relevant to reputation management for regulators/institutions, not just companies.
