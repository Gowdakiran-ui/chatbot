## Case ID: 25
**Company:** Boohoo Group plc
**Industry:** Fast Fashion / E-Commerce Retail
**Year:** 2020
**Crisis Type:** Supply-chain labour exploitation / Modern slavery allegations

**Trigger Event:** On 5 July 2020, the Sunday Times published an undercover investigation revealing that a factory in Leicester supplying Boohoo's Nasty Gal brand paid workers as little as £3.50/hour (less than half the UK minimum wage of £8.72) and had no Covid-19 safety measures, despite Leicester being under a localised lockdown at the time.

**Response Type:** Reactive, escalating to Proactive within days

**Response Speed Score:** 3
Boohoo's first public statement came within roughly 24 hours (Monday 6 July), calling conditions "totally unacceptable" if true and announcing a supplier investigation — a reasonably fast initial reaction. But the company's early framing (disowning the specific factory, "Jaswal Fashions," as not a declared supplier) proved only partially accurate once the independent review later found broader, longer-standing failings across the Leicester supply chain — so the fast initial response was not fully accurate, which is what caps this score below 5.

**Transparency Score:** 3
Boohoo commissioned an independent QC-led review (barrister Alison Levitt) within three days of the story breaking (announced 8 July) — a genuinely proactive step most retailers don't take that fast. But the review's findings, delivered to the board on 24 September 2020, concluded there were "many failings" in the supply chain, which sat uncomfortably next to the company's initial framing that the specific factory named wasn't even a declared supplier. The review cleared Boohoo of deliberately profiting from exploitation but confirmed years of inadequate monitoring — a mixed transparency picture: real independent investigation, but initial company statements understated the scope of the problem.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** UK Modern Slavery Act 2015 (supply-chain transparency obligations); National Crime Agency modern slavery/human trafficking investigation into Leicester's textile sector; scrutiny from the UK Parliament's Environmental Audit Committee; UK employment law (minimum wage enforcement, HMRC).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — Boohoo survived and remains listed, retained its Levitt-recommended governance reforms, but a later University of Bath study found the company subsequently moved manufacturing out of Leicester, leaving thousands of local garment workers without work, and reputational scrutiny of the brand has recurred periodically since.

**What Went Right:**
- Boohoo issued a same-day public statement acknowledging the allegations were serious ("totally unacceptable" if true) rather than an outright denial, which limited the initial credibility gap.
- The company commissioned a genuinely independent review led by an external QC within three days — a fast, credible commitment compared to typical corporate response times to supply-chain scandals.
- The review's full findings were delivered to the board and substantially disclosed publicly (September 2020), rather than buried — an unusually high level of disclosure for a supply-chain scandal.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Boohoo's initial defensive framing — that the named factory wasn't a "declared supplier" — created a "we didn't know" narrative that was later undercut by the review's finding of "many failings" and prior warnings the company had received about Leicester conditions for years (the University of Leicester had flagged systemic problems in the same supply base as early as 2015).
- Major retail partners (Next, Asos, Zalando) dropped Boohoo from their platforms within days, a stronger and faster reputational judgment from commercial partners than from the company's own initial response — a signal Boohoo was reacting to public/commercial pressure more than getting ahead of it.
- Share price fell roughly 16% the Monday after the story broke and a further 12% the following day (roughly a 25%+ two-day drop), reflecting investor judgment that management had been blindsided by a risk it should have already controlled given years of prior warnings about Leicester conditions.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Given that concerns about Leicester garment supply-chain conditions had been publicly documented since at least 2015 (University of Leicester research) and repeatedly flagged by NGOs like Labour Behind the Label before the Sunday Times story, Boohoo needed proactive, published third-party supply-chain audits years before the crisis — not a reactive review triggered by a newspaper investigation. For an ORM/crisis-comms team: when a company's first public statement disowns direct knowledge of a problem that outside researchers had already flagged for five years, that gap between "we didn't know" and "this was knowable" becomes the story that keeps running — as it did with the 2023 University of Bath follow-up study.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Share price fell over 40% in the two weeks following the story (from around 350p to a low near 180p); Next, Asos, and Zalando all removed Boohoo brands from their platforms.
- Reputational: Boohoo became the reference case for "fast fashion labour exploitation" in UK media for years afterward; long-term reputational drag persisted well beyond the immediate news cycle, resurfacing whenever fast-fashion supply chains are discussed in UK press or Parliament.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms — a clear example of how a fast initial statement combined with a credible independent review can limit (but not eliminate) damage, and how pre-existing, documented risk signals turn a "crisis" into a "we should have known" reputational narrative that resurfaces for years.

**Key Sources:**
- BBC News, "Boohoo to investigate Leicester supplier over exploitation claims" (6 July 2020) and "Boohoo dropped by Next, Asos and Zalando over exploitation claims" (7 July 2020)
- Corporate Justice Coalition, "Leicester's sweat shops: abuses in Boohoo's value chains (UK)" case study
- AOL/Yahoo Finance, "Boohoo's found 'many failings' in working conditions and low pay in Leicester supply chain" (September 2020 Levitt Review coverage)
- FashionUnited, "Timeline: Behind Boohoo's boulevard of broken promises" (2024, citing University of Bath research)
- Fortune, "Investors sink shares in online retailer boohoo on reports of labor abuses in its supply chain" (July 2020)

## Case ID: 26
**Company:** Deliveroo Holdings plc
**Industry:** Food Delivery / Gig Economy Platform
**Year:** 2021–2024 (ongoing multi-jurisdiction litigation)
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory/legal battle over worker classification, spanning UK, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Italy

**Trigger Event:** In the run-up to Deliveroo's March 2021 London IPO — one of the UK's largest tech listings — trade unions (IWGB) and investor advocacy groups (ShareAction) publicly campaigned against the company's self-employed rider model just as the UK Supreme Court had ruled (February 2021) that Uber drivers must be classed as "workers," directly threatening the economics underpinning Deliveroo's business model.

**Response Type:** Proactive on legal defense, Reactive/Denial-adjacent on stakeholder communications

**Response Speed Score:** 2
Deliveroo did not adjust its investor messaging or business-model framing ahead of the IPO despite the Uber Supreme Court ruling landing just five weeks before its float — a foreseeable regulatory risk that should have been pre-addressed in the prospectus narrative. The company's response to investor concerns was essentially "we believe our model is legally distinct," which market events (mass fund-manager abstention) proved insufficiently reassuring.

**Transparency Score:** 3
Deliveroo's IPO prospectus did disclose the labour-classification risk, and the company has been consistently transparent about individual court outcomes as they've landed across multiple countries (UK, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Italy) rather than concealing losses — for example, publicly acknowledging the 2020 Paris labour court ruling against it (later overturned on appeal) and the 2021 Milan joint fine alongside Just Eat, Uber Eats and Glovo. But the company did not proactively address investor concerns about labour-model risk before the IPO in a way that reassured the market, leaving the risk to surface as a stock-price shock instead.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** UK Supreme Court employment status law (IWGB v CAC, decided November 2023, ultimately in Deliveroo's favour); French labour courts (Paris labour court "undeclared work" ruling, 2020, overturned on appeal; ongoing criminal referral of former Deliveroo France directors); Italian Labour Inspectorate and Milan prosecutors (€733m joint fine against four platforms in February 2021, reduced to €15,700 per company by December 2021 with mandatory safety-training conditions); Belgian Labour Tribunal (self-employed ruling December 2021, overturned to employee status on appeal December 2023); Dutch courts (FNV v Deliveroo, January 2019); EU Platform Work Directive (adopted by the Council, based on a December 2021 Commission proposal, setting EU-wide criteria for employee-vs-contractor status).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — Deliveroo won its core UK Supreme Court case (November 2023, riders confirmed self-employed due to a broad substitution clause) but lost or faces ongoing exposure in Belgium (overturned to employee status in 2023) and France (criminal proceedings against former directors continuing); the EU Platform Work Directive creates future compliance risk across all member states regardless of individual national court wins.

**What Went Right:**
- Deliveroo's UK legal strategy — building a genuinely broad, "unfettered" substitution clause into rider contracts — was specific and effective, and directly persuaded the Supreme Court that riders were not in an employment relationship, distinguishing Deliveroo's case from Uber's.
- The company continued operating and communicating consistently across jurisdictions even as it accumulated a patchwork of wins and losses, rather than making sweeping claims that its model was universally legally settled.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Timing the IPO five weeks after the Uber Supreme Court "worker status" ruling, without a clear pre-emptive investor narrative on why Deliveroo's model was different, meant the market absorbed regulatory risk in real time on debut day: shares fell up to 30% and were down 26% by close, wiping roughly £2 billion off the valuation.
- At least six major UK fund managers (including Legal & General, the UK's largest asset manager) publicly announced they would abstain from the IPO specifically over labour-rights and governance concerns before trading even began — a foreseeable reputational and financial cost that better pre-IPO stakeholder engagement might have reduced.
- The inconsistent, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction legal outcomes (self-employed in the UK, initially self-employed then employee in Belgium, "undeclared work" in France before being overturned) created a confusing public narrative that made it hard for any single stakeholder group — investors, riders, regulators — to know where the company actually stood at any given time.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Given that the Uber Supreme Court ruling was known five weeks before the float, Deliveroo's IPO roadshow needed a proactive, quantified narrative addressing labour-model risk head-on — for example, publishing its own legal analysis of why the substitution-clause structure differed materially from Uber's arrangement, rather than leaving investors and press to draw their own conclusions from the Uber precedent. For an ORM/crisis-comms team advising a platform business before a public listing: a known, adjacent legal precedent (a competitor's court loss) must be addressed explicitly in investor communications before it is priced in by the market on debut day — waiting for questions rather than pre-empting them cost roughly £2bn in valuation within hours.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: IPO valuation cut from a sought $12bn to $10.5bn before listing; shares fell as much as 30% and closed down 26% on debut (31 March 2021), wiping over £2bn off valuation in a single day; press dubbed it the "Floperoo."
- Reputational: Became the widely cited case study for "ESG risk mispriced at IPO" in UK financial press for years afterward; the "Floperoo" label persisted in coverage as late as 2025, even after the company turned its first annual profit.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms — a strong case study in how unaddressed regulatory-precedent risk translates directly into an acute reputational and financial event on a specific, foreseeable date (IPO day); also relevant for ORM work tracking multi-jurisdiction legal-outcome narratives that can contradict each other in public perception.

**Key Sources:**
- CNBC, "Deliveroo IPO: Amazon-backed firm starts trading in London today" (31 March 2021) and "Deliveroo Shares Slump Over 20% on Debut in London"
- Private Equity Stakeholder Project, "Deliveroo's IPO Tanks after Workers Protest, Investors Revolt"
- Lexology, "Supreme Court confirms Deliveroo riders are not 'in employment'" (November 2023 UK Supreme Court ruling analysis)
- Sifted, "Gig economy rules around Europe: what startups need to know" (Italy, France detail)

## Case ID: 27
**Company:** Google / Alphabet Inc.
**Industry:** Technology / Digital Advertising & Search
**Year:** 2017–2026 (ongoing, multi-decision EU antitrust enforcement)
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory antitrust enforcement / Market dominance abuse findings

**Trigger Event:** Beginning with a €2.42bn fine on 27 June 2017 for illegally favouring its own Google Shopping comparison service in search results, the European Commission built a sustained, decade-long antitrust enforcement programme against Google covering three major abuse-of-dominance cases (Shopping, Android, AdSense) plus subsequent Digital Markets Act (DMA) enforcement.

**Response Type:** Reactive-Legalistic (appeal everything, comply minimally, repeat)

**Response Speed Score:** 2
Google's consistent pattern across all major EU decisions has been to appeal to the maximum extent possible rather than move quickly to remedy the underlying conduct — the Android case alone ran from a 2018 fine through to a final, exhausted appeal at the EU's top court in July 2026, eight years later. Compliance changes (e.g., letting rivals bid for default search placement on Android from June 2021, three years after the original fine) came only after protracted legal exhaustion, not promptly after each finding.

**Transparency Score:** 2
Google has consistently contested the Commission's factual findings rather than acknowledging the underlying conduct, even after losing appeals (e.g., a spokesperson statement after the 2021 Shopping appeal loss reframed the ruling as being about display formatting rather than conceding anti-competitive intent). The DMA-era 2026 compliance dispute (Google splitting a €890m fine into Search and Android app-steering components) shows the same pattern continuing: partial, minimal visible changes (e.g., removing the dedicated Flights unit) rather than a clear public account of remedied practices.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) Article 102 (abuse of dominant market position) for the Shopping, Android, and AdSense cases; EU Digital Markets Act (DMA), in force from 2024, for the newer Search self-preferencing and Android app-steering non-compliance decisions; Court of Justice of the European Union (ECJ) and EU General Court as the appellate bodies.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved (Shopping and Android fines are now final and enforceable after exhausted appeals) / Unresolved (DMA compliance disputes, including the 2026 €890m Search/Android decision, remain active with a 60-day compliance deadline and potential daily penalties of up to 5% of Alphabet's average worldwide daily turnover for continued non-compliance)

**What Went Right:**
- Google did make some structural product changes under sustained pressure — for example, opening up Android default-search-engine choice screens from June 2021 and removing the dedicated Flights unit from search results pages under 2026 DMA pressure — showing eventual, if slow, responsiveness to regulatory requirements.
- The company has continued operating normally and paying fines as they become final, without triggering any systemic business disruption, demonstrating financial resilience even against a cumulative total exceeding €8 billion in fines prior to the 2026 Android confirmation.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The consistent "appeal everything" strategy meant each case dragged on for years (Android: 2018 fine to 2026 final confirmation — eight years), during which the underlying competitive harm to rivals continued largely unaddressed, compounding both the competitive and reputational damage.
- Google's public responses after losing appeals have tended to minimise or reframe the findings (e.g., stressing narrow procedural wins like the court's finding that harm to general search wasn't proven, rather than addressing the core finding of anti-competitive self-preferencing) — a pattern that reads as deflection to regulators, press, and rivals alike, and has not slowed the pace of new investigations.
- The pattern of "new case opens roughly every 1–2 years" (Shopping 2017, Android 2018, AdSense 2019, adtech 2025, DMA Search/Android 2026) suggests remedies applied after each individual fine were not broad enough to prevent regulators identifying fresh, related conduct — indicating a compliance approach that reacts narrowly to each specific finding rather than addressing the underlying pattern the Commission keeps identifying.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
A company facing a recurring, decade-plus pattern of antitrust findings in the same regulatory relationship needs a fundamentally different strategy than case-by-case appeals: a comprehensive, proactively negotiated global compliance framework with the Commission covering all product lines at once, rather than litigating each product (Shopping, then Android, then AdSense, then adtech, then DMA) as if it were an isolated dispute. For an ORM/crisis-comms team advising a dominant-platform client facing regulatory scrutiny: the "fight every case to the highest court" approach preserves near-term cash and operational flexibility but produces a compounding reputational cost — each new fine gets covered against the backdrop of "Google's decade of antitrust battles," meaning the reputational damage of case #5 is larger than case #1 purely due to the accumulated pattern, regardless of that case's individual merits.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Cumulative EU antitrust fines exceeded €8.25 billion by 2019 (Shopping €2.42bn + Android €4.34bn + AdSense €1.49bn) prior to the 2026 final Android confirmation (€4.1bn, superseding/replacing part of the earlier Android figure after appeal adjustments) and the 2025 adtech fine of €2.95bn; 2026 DMA non-compliance fines added a further €890m, split €460m (Search self-preferencing) and €430m (Android app-steering).
- Reputational: Became the definitive global case study for "Big Tech vs. EU regulator" coverage; each new fine is now reported with reference to the full running total and timeline, meaning cumulative reputational framing ("Google's decade of antitrust battles") outweighs the standalone news value of any single decision.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown — the clearest possible illustration of how a "litigate everything, concede nothing" regulatory strategy compounds reputational cost over time even when individual legal appeals have some merit; highly relevant for any client facing recurring regulatory scrutiny in the same jurisdiction.

## Case ID: 28
**Company:** Apple Inc.
**Industry:** Technology / Consumer Electronics & Digital Services
**Year:** 2016–2026 (ongoing, multi-decision EU tax and antitrust enforcement)
**Crisis Type:** State aid / tax structuring dispute + Digital Markets Act antitrust enforcement

**Trigger Event:** In 2016, the European Commission ordered Ireland to recover up to €13 billion in back taxes from Apple, ruling that a two-decade-long Irish tax arrangement gave Apple an illegal selective advantage over other companies — a dispute that ran for eight years through the courts before Europe's top court definitively ruled against Apple on 10 September 2024, the same day Apple was unveiling new iPhone and Apple Watch products.

**Response Type:** Reactive-Legalistic (parallel to Google's pattern: appeal to the highest available court, comply only when legally exhausted)

**Response Speed Score:** 2
Apple and Ireland jointly appealed the original 2016 Commission decision in 2019, won at the EU General Court in 2020 (a four-year process), only for the Commission's own appeal to succeed at the Court of Justice of the EU in September 2024 — an eight-year total dispute before final resolution. On the separate App Store front, Apple was fined €1.8bn in March 2024 for anti-steering conduct in music streaming after a multi-year investigation, and again fined €500m in April 2025 under the DMA for continued anti-steering practices — indicating the underlying conduct was not meaningfully changed between the two fines just over a year apart.

**Transparency Score:** 2
Apple has consistently disputed both the substance and the timing of EU findings (e.g., publicly suggesting the March 2024 music-streaming fine was timed suspiciously close to unrelated DMA compliance deadlines) rather than engaging substantively with the underlying findings. On the tax case, Apple maintained throughout that it followed the law and paid all taxes owed everywhere it operates, a position the company held even after the final adverse ruling, and it booked the resulting one-time $10bn tax charge as a legal/accounting event rather than accompanying it with any acknowledgment of the underlying finding.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU state aid rules under Article 107 TFEU (the Ireland tax case); EU Treaty Article 102 abuse-of-dominance rules and the EU Digital Markets Act (App Store anti-steering cases); Court of Justice of the European Union and EU General Court as appellate venues; ongoing DMA non-compliance procedures opened by the Commission in June 2024 specifically addressing Apple's Core Technology Fee and steering restrictions.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (the €13bn Ireland tax case — final CJEU ruling, September 2024, funds now being transferred from escrow to the Irish state) / Unresolved (the €500m April 2025 DMA anti-steering fine is under active appeal at the EU General Court, filed by Apple in mid-2025)

**What Went Right:**
- Apple absorbed the final €13bn tax ruling without operational disruption, booking a clean one-time $10bn income tax charge in its Q4 FY2024 filing — a financially disciplined, orderly response to an adverse final judgment rather than further disruptive litigation once the highest court had ruled.
- Apple did make some visible compliance changes under DMA pressure — for example, proposing new terms in August 2024 that allow developers who opt in to steer users to alternative payment options, showing a degree of eventual responsiveness distinct from pure stonewalling.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Being fined twice for materially the same anti-steering conduct within about 13 months (March 2024 music-streaming fine, then April 2025 DMA fine) signals that Apple's post-first-fine changes were not sufficient to satisfy regulators — a pattern of minimal, incremental compliance that invites repeat enforcement rather than resolving the underlying regulatory concern in one move.
- Apple's public framing of the 2024 music-streaming fine as suspiciously timed relative to its own DMA compliance changes reads as deflection rather than engagement with the Commission's actual finding (that Apple banned developers from telling users about cheaper subscription options outside the App Store) — similar to Google's pattern of reframing losses rather than addressing findings directly.
- The Core Technology Fee structure Apple proposed in response to DMA pressure was itself challenged by the Commission as still restrictive (fees reportedly as high as 17% in Apple's first proposal), suggesting Apple's compliance proposals were negotiating positions rather than genuine attempts to resolve the Commission's concerns — prolonging the dispute further.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Given that the EU's anti-steering concerns were well telegraphed well before the March 2024 fine, Apple needed a single, comprehensive compliance overhaul addressing steering, fees, and alternative payment options together — not an incremental, fee-adjusted response that regulators would predictably challenge again within a year. For an ORM/crisis-comms team advising a platform company under DMA-style ongoing regulatory scrutiny: minimal, negotiated compliance steps that a regulator is likely to reject invite a second enforcement cycle and a second wave of negative coverage — a company is better served (both legally and reputationally) by over-complying once than by testing the regulator's tolerance repeatedly, given media coverage compounds with each additional fine in the same dispute area.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: €13bn (~$14.4bn) tax repayment to Ireland, finalised September 2024, plus a $10bn one-time income tax charge booked by Apple; €1.8bn music-streaming anti-steering fine (March 2024); €500m DMA anti-steering fine (April 2025, under appeal).
- Reputational: Apple's EU tax and App Store disputes have become a recurring reference point in "Big Tech regulatory pushback" coverage; the case is frequently cited alongside Google's antitrust history as evidence of a broader EU-vs-US-tech regulatory pattern, meaning reputational framing extends beyond Apple specifically into industry-wide narratives Apple cannot fully control through its own communications.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown — illustrates how incremental, minimally-compliant regulatory responses can trigger repeat enforcement cycles that compound negative coverage; also relevant to reputation management around high-profile product launch dates being overshadowed by unrelated legal news (the September 2024 tax ruling landing the same day as new iPhone/Watch announcements).
