## Case 21

**Company:** Rio Tinto
**Industry:** Mining (dual-listed on London Stock Exchange and ASX)
**Year:** 2020
**Crisis Type:** Cultural heritage destruction, governance failure, ESG/investor crisis

**Trigger Event:** On 24 May 2020, Rio Tinto legally detonated explosives at Juukan Gorge in Western Australia, destroying two rock shelters with evidence of continuous human habitation dating back 46,000 years, despite the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura (PKKP) traditional owners having urgently objected just 11 days before the blast.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence (first response) → Delayed → Proactive (once investor pressure mounted)

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 — Rio Tinto's first public statement came three days after the blast (27 May) and was a limited expression of regret that the PKKP's "recently expressed concerns" hadn't surfaced earlier — a framing PKKP itself rejected within days as false, saying they'd flagged the site's significance long before. The CEO did not address the incident publicly at all until 12 June, nearly three weeks after the blast.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 — Once the Board Review was commissioned, it was published (24 August 2020) and was unusually candid: it found Rio Tinto had three viable mine-expansion options that would have preserved the site and chose a fourth that destroyed it, and blamed "a series of decisions, actions and omissions" rather than one bad actor. That level of self-incrimination is rare. But it took a Senate inquiry and sustained investor revolt to get there — it wasn't volunteered early.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No law was actually broken — Rio Tinto held a legal permit under Western Australian heritage legislation (a fact the company repeatedly noted in its defense). The crisis instead ran through corporate governance channels: an Australian Senate Joint Standing Committee inquiry, LSE/ASX dual-listing disclosure obligations, and shareholder/proxy-adviser pressure.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- The Board Review, once published, was genuinely independent in tone and did not shield executives — it explicitly named the decision sequence and the executives responsible.
- Rio Tinto initially cut bonuses for the three executives found responsible (CEO Jean-Sébastien Jacques, iron ore chief Chris Salisbury, and corporate relations head Simone Niven).
- All three ultimately resigned by 11 September 2020 — just under four months after the blast — which is fast relative to typical board-level accountability timelines for a non-illegal act.
- The chairman, Simon Thompson, gave a direct, unhedged public statement ("What happened at Juukan was wrong") rather than a legally cautious non-admission.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The initial 27 May statement tried to shift blame onto the timing of PKKP's objections rather than owning the company's own internal decision failure — this framing was publicly and immediately disputed by PKKP on 5 June, creating a second, avoidable news cycle.
- CEO silence for nearly three weeks after the blast, during a period when protests were already happening outside Rio Tinto's offices in Perth, let the story be defined entirely by critics and media.
- The resigning executives initially retained their long-term incentive payouts, and a year later a remuneration committee awarded large payouts to all three — undermining the accountability narrative the resignations were meant to establish, and triggering a second wave of criticism that led to further board departures (chairman Simon Thompson stepping down in 2021).
- The company's legal defense ("we broke no law") was accurate but reputationally counterproductive — it read as prioritizing compliance over conscience.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** When a company has 11 days' advance notice that a stakeholder group objects to an action, the response protocol should trigger before the action, not after. Post-incident, the CEO — not just the chairman — needs to be the first and fastest public voice; letting three weeks pass allows external actors to set the narrative permanently. Remuneration decisions must be sequenced consistently with public accountability messaging — announcing resignations "for accountability" and then paying full incentives in the next cycle reopens the same wound.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: no direct fine, since the act was legal, but sustained reputational cost to ESG ratings and a well-documented multi-month share-price and investor-confidence hit tied to the governance crisis; three senior executives and eventually the chairman lost their roles. Reputational: Juukan Gorge became the reference case for "social license to operate" failures in mining and is now a standard citation in ESG and heritage-management training globally, including outside the mining sector.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (delayed CEO response), ORM (a legal-but-reputationally-fatal act — a useful template distinguishing legal compliance from reputational survival), governance/ESG search association management.

## Case 22

**Company:** Uber
**Industry:** Ride-hailing / gig-economy transport
**Year:** 2016–2020 (London licensing and worker-status crisis)
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory crisis + employment-classification crisis

**Trigger Event:** On 22 September 2017, Transport for London (TfL) informed Uber it would not renew its private-hire operator licence, citing use of a secret tool ("Greyball") to evade regulators, failure to properly report serious crimes, and inadequate driver background/medical checks — a decision that put roughly 40,000 London drivers' livelihoods in immediate limbo.

**Response Type:** Reactive (2017) → Proactive (multi-year remediation) → Reactive again (second licence refusal, 2019)

**Response Speed Score:** 3/5 — Uber responded publicly the same day, calling the decision "extraordinary and wrong" and confirming it would appeal, which is fast. But the substance of the response (disputing TfL's characterization rather than immediately committing to fix the specific practices cited) meant the real remediation work — which took roughly a year to produce a court-satisfying result — started slowly.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — Uber's use of "Greyball," a tool built specifically to help identify and block regulators from accessing the app, was the kind of concealment tactic that directly damages a transparency score; it wasn't something Uber disclosed voluntarily — it came out through investigative reporting and regulatory findings.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** TfL private-hire operator licensing law; UK Employment Rights Act 1996, Working Time Regulations 1998, and National Minimum Wage Act 1998 (via the separate driver-status litigation); the case ultimately reached the UK Employment Appeal Tribunal (November 2017) and later the Supreme Court.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- Uber did not shut down operations during the appeal — UK process allowed it to keep operating, and the company used that window to make substantive changes rather than only legal arguments.
- By 2018, a court granted Uber a 15-month probationary licence specifically because Uber had visibly changed reporting and safety processes, showing the remediation was real, not just promised.
- In 2020, when TfL again refused to renew Uber's licence (a separate, later crisis over unauthorized/impostor drivers), Uber didn't dispute the safety concern's legitimacy — CEO Dara Khosrowshahi publicly stated "we understand we're held to a high bar, as we should be" alongside disputing the specific decision, a more credible mixed response than pure denial.
- Uber ultimately won an 18-month licence in 2020 with 21 attached conditions, including regular independent reporting — accepting ongoing third-party oversight rather than resisting it.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The Greyball tool itself is the core failure: building technology specifically to evade a regulator is very hard to spin as anything other than deliberate concealment, and it undercut every subsequent Uber safety claim for years.
- The parallel employment-status litigation (drivers classified as "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay) ran for years without Uber conceding the substance — the Employment Tribunal ruling was upheld on appeal in November 2017, and Uber continued contesting it up to the UK Supreme Court, prolonging reputational exposure rather than settling early.
- London's licence was revoked twice within roughly three years (2017 and 2019) for different but related "fitness" concerns (crime reporting/background checks, then unauthorized drivers), reinforcing a "repeat offender" pattern rather than a one-off failure.
- Uber's initial framing in 2017 ("we've always followed TfL rules on reporting serious incidents") was directly contradicted by TfL's own findings, which is a credibility-destroying move — never publicly assert compliance you can't back up when the regulator has already found otherwise.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** A company operating in heavily regulated markets should never build tools designed to evade the regulator — the reputational and legal cost of a tool like Greyball being discovered vastly exceeds any short-term operational benefit. On the labor side, once a first-instance tribunal rules against you on a worker-classification question, prolonged appeals often cost more in reputation (and eventually in legal fees) than reaching a negotiated settlement that lets the company control the transition narrative and timeline itself.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: significant, though hard to isolate precisely — legal costs across years of litigation, driver disruption costs, and lost bookings during periods of operating uncertainty in one of Uber's most important global markets; the broader worker-classification exposure across Europe has had knock-on regulatory costs in multiple jurisdictions. Reputational: London licensing fights became a recurring media narrative that shaped Uber's "move fast, ask forgiveness" reputation for years, and the case is a standard reference point in gig-economy regulation discussions across the EU.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (credibility-destroying denial pattern), regulatory/legal takedown risk, ORM around "repeat regulatory failure" search association — useful template for platform/gig-economy clients navigating EU labor and licensing scrutiny.

## Case 23

**Company:** Ryanair
**Industry:** Aviation / low-cost airline
**Year:** 2017–2018
**Crisis Type:** Labor relations crisis (union recognition dispute, coordinated multi-country strikes)

**Trigger Event:** After decades of refusing to recognize pilot or cabin crew unions — central to its low-cost operating model — Ryanair faced coordinated strike threats across six European countries in December 2017, and when it did not fully resolve underlying pay and conditions disputes, pilots and cabin crew across Ireland, Germany, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Belgium and the Netherlands staged escalating strikes through mid-to-late 2018.

**Response Type:** Delayed → Reactive → Proactive (union recognition U-turn)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — Ryanair only agreed to recognize unions on 15 December 2017, in the final days before threatened Christmas strikes, after refusing to do so for the airline's entire 34-year history up to that point. The concession came under direct strike-deadline pressure rather than ahead of it.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 — CEO Michael O'Leary was unusually direct about the reversal itself ("recognizing unions will be a significant change for Ryanair, but we have delivered radical change before"), which is more candid than a typical corporate non-admission. But the company continued to publicly dispute the legitimacy and necessity of strikes throughout 2018 even as they escalated, which undercut the credibility of the earlier concession.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** EU freedom-of-association and collective-bargaining norms; multiple national labor laws (Ireland, Germany, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Belgium); the European Commission was formally petitioned by unions to intervene on "social dumping" grounds, and EU authorities publicly told Ryanair to respect European law on local employment contracts (27 September 2018).

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- Ryanair's December 2017 union-recognition reversal was a genuine structural concession, not just a statement — it created the collective-bargaining framework that later produced real agreements (e.g., a collective labour agreement with Italian union ANPAC by 28 August 2018).
- The company kept flights running for the large majority of customers during strikes by being transparent about which routes were affected — e.g., publicly noting in July 2018 that Irish strikes would affect only ~7% of total flights, and committing to email affected customers in advance.
- Ryanair reached a country-specific concession in Belgium (agreeing to follow Belgian employment law for Belgian-contracted staff from a set date) rather than insisting on a one-size-fits-all Irish-contract model — a targeted fix rather than a blanket refusal.
- By offering a 20% pilot pay increase ahead of the July 2018 Dublin strike, Ryanair showed willingness to negotiate on substance, not just process.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The core dispute — Ryanair employing staff in multiple countries under Irish contracts rather than local ones — was left unresolved for years, meaning the same grievance kept resurfacing as new strikes rather than being settled once.
- Management's public tone frequently escalated rather than de-escalated: CEO O'Leary described strikes as avoidable "wildcat" actions and warned of job cuts in response to a September 2018 German walkout, a combative posture that hardened union positions rather than inviting settlement.
- The September 2018 strike wave was the largest coordinated action in the airline's history, hitting six countries simultaneously and cancelling roughly 250 flights in a single day — showing that the December 2017 concession had not actually resolved the underlying issues fast enough to prevent further escalation.
- Unions explicitly and publicly called out Ryanair for "social dumping" to EU institutions, which shifted the dispute from a labor-relations story into a pan-European regulatory-pressure story — a much larger reputational surface area than a national pay dispute.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Once a company concedes on a core structural issue (union recognition), the follow-through negotiations need a clear timeline communicated publicly, so each subsequent strike doesn't look like a broken promise. Combative public rhetoric from the CEO during live disputes tends to convert a labor-relations story into a governance/culture story — a more measured, consistent tone (as used in the Belgian localized-law concession) de-escalates faster than confrontational statements aimed at investors and media.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: tens of millions of euros in voucher/rebooking costs across the multiple 2017–2018 disruption waves, plus a documented share-price rise of over 5% when Ryanair reached its first union agreement (Aug 2018), showing the market explicitly rewarded resolution over confrontation. Reputational: reinforced Ryanair's existing "difficult employer" reputation, which continues to surface in subsequent labor disputes and media coverage of the airline.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (tone management during protracted disputes), labor/ESG reputational risk, useful as a contrast case to Case 7 (Ryanair's 2017 rostering crisis) — same company, same pattern of concession-under-pressure rather than proactive resolution.

## Case 24

**Company:** H&M (Hennes & Mauritz)
**Industry:** Fast fashion / apparel retail
**Year:** 2021
**Crisis Type:** Supply-chain ethics crisis with geopolitical/market-access fallout

**Trigger Event:** On 24 March 2021, Chinese state-affiliated media (the Communist Youth League) resurfaced and publicly attacked a September 2020 H&M statement expressing concern about forced-labor allegations in Xinjiang cotton production, triggering an immediate, coordinated consumer boycott and removal of H&M from major Chinese e-commerce and map platforms within 24 hours.

**Response Type:** Delayed (the statement itself was 6 months old before it became a crisis) → Reactive/Ambiguous once the boycott hit

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — H&M had no real opportunity to respond "fast" to the boycott's trigger, since the triggering statement was already six months old; the crisis wasn't a new failure but an old, unresolved position being weaponized. H&M's in-crisis response (a Weibo statement saying it "consistently respects Chinese consumers") arrived within roughly 24–48 hours of the boycott starting but was widely seen as vague and non-committal — it neither reaffirmed nor withdrew the original Xinjiang position.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — H&M never clearly and publicly restated its actual sourcing position once the backlash began; the original statement was taken down from H&M's own website during the crisis, which read to observers as retreating rather than standing behind a documented ethical-sourcing decision.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No direct EU or UK regulatory action; the crisis intersects with the Better Cotton Initiative's (a Switzerland-based industry body) supply-chain standards and, more broadly, with EU forced-labor and supply-chain due-diligence policy discussions that intensified partly because of this case.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved / Ongoing tension

**What Went Right:**
- H&M did not reverse its underlying ethical position under commercial pressure — it did not resume Xinjiang cotton sourcing to placate the Chinese market, preserving credibility with European and North American stakeholders and investors focused on supply-chain ethics.
- The original 2020 statement itself was substantively well-grounded, citing specific concerns raised by civil society organizations and media rather than vague language — a genuinely researched position, not a reactive PR move.
- H&M avoided directly attacking the Chinese government or state media in its public statements, which limited (though did not prevent) further escalation.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The statement sat dormant and unaddressed for six months with no proactive communications plan for the geopolitical risk it obviously carried, given that Nike and other brands making similar statements faced the same predictable dynamic.
- Once the boycott began, H&M's response tried to be simultaneously reassuring to Chinese consumers and consistent with its ethical position — and satisfied neither audience: the statement was criticized in China as insincere and criticized by human-rights advocates internationally as weak.
- The company lost effectively all e-commerce visibility in China within a day (removed from Tmall, Taobao, JD.com, and map/navigation apps) — a total market-access shutdown that H&M had no contingency plan for.
- Chinese celebrity brand ambassadors publicly cut ties within days, amplifying the story into entertainment and lifestyle media well beyond its original business-press audience.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Any European or Western company making a supply-chain ethics statement about a politically sensitive sourcing region needs a prepared bilateral communications plan before publishing — one message calibrated for the market where the statement matters ethically, and a clear, pre-agreed internal position for how to respond if the statement becomes a flashpoint in a different market. The response, when the crisis hit, needed to pick a lane: either clearly reaffirm the ethical position and accept the commercial cost, or don't make the statement in the first place — the vague middle-ground response satisfied no one and prolonged the story.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: significant and immediate — complete removal from China's largest e-commerce platforms represented a major short-term revenue hit in one of H&M's largest growth markets; the company's China sales did not fully recover to pre-boycott levels for an extended period afterward. Reputational: mixed — reputational damage in China was severe and long-lasting, while reputational standing among European ESG-focused investors and human-rights organizations was largely preserved or even strengthened; the case is now a standard example of "reputation is not one audience" risk.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (market-specific reputational bifurcation), Crisis Comms (a case where "please everyone" messaging failed both audiences), supply-chain due-diligence risk — highly relevant for clients with global operations facing geopolitically sensitive sourcing questions.
