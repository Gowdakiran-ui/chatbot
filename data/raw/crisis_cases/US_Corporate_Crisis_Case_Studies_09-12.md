## Case 9

**Company:** Starbucks Corporation
**Industry:** Food & Beverage / Retail
**Year:** 2018
**Crisis Type:** Racial discrimination / social bias incident, employee conduct

**Trigger Event:** On April 12, 2018, a Starbucks manager at a Philadelphia store called police on two Black men, Rashon Nelson and Donte Robinson, who were waiting at a table for a business meeting without having ordered yet. Officers handcuffed and removed the men in front of other customers; a bystander's video of the arrest went viral on Twitter within days.

**Response Type:** Delayed, then Proactive
**Response Speed Score:** 3/5
**Transparency Score:** 4/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No federal charges were filed against Starbucks; this was a reputational/civil-rights matter rather than a regulatory one. The men later reached a private financial settlement with the city of Philadelphia (a symbolic $1 each plus a program fund) and a separate confidential settlement with Starbucks. Public accommodation / anti-discrimination norms (Title II, Civil Rights Act) framed the public debate even though no formal charge was brought.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved

**What Went Right:**
- Starbucks issued its first statement the same evening the video went viral, calling the outcome "reprehensible."
- CEO Kevin Johnson personally flew to Philadelphia within days, met city officials, and gave a face-to-face apology to the two men.
- Johnson did a live, on-camera interview with Good Morning America (aired the Monday after the Thursday incident) in which he said "I personally apologize," rather than routing the apology only through a press release.
- Starbucks closed more than 8,000 US company-owned stores on the afternoon of May 29, 2018 — about six weeks after the incident — for company-wide racial-bias training for roughly 175,000 employees, a costly and visible corrective action.
- The company did not blame the two men or minimize the incident publicly, despite the local police commissioner initially defending the officers.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The first statement was a generic, non-specific apology; MIT Sloan's review of the case notes the company had to issue a second, more targeted statement from Johnson later the same day because the first one read as boilerplate and did not address the specific incident.
- It took roughly three to four days of sustained protest, a trending #BoycottStarbucks hashtag, and a sit-in before the company committed to systemic action (the training day), rather than announcing that quickly.
- The store manager who called police was not publicly disciplined in a way that satisfied critics; Starbucks' own regional VP acknowledged the company had "set her up" without adequate guidance, which read to some as deflecting individual accountability onto systemic factors.
- Some community leaders, including local activist Asa Khalif, publicly rejected the apology as "lukewarm" and accused Starbucks of image management rather than substantive change.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The first public statement after any bias or discrimination incident should be specific to the facts of the incident, not generic corporate language — vague statements read as insincere and force a costly do-over within the same news cycle. A company should also announce a concrete corrective step (training, policy change, personnel review) within 48–72 hours rather than waiting a week to a month; the six-week gap between the incident and the actual training day left Starbucks looking reactive to sustained protest pressure rather than acting on its own initiative.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: the closure of over 8,000 stores for one afternoon was estimated by outside analysts at roughly $12–17 million in lost sales; no material long-term stock impact was recorded. Reputational: significant short-term brand damage and boycott calls, but Starbucks' racial-bias training became a widely cited case study in crisis response, ultimately stabilizing brand perception within about two to three months.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms, ORM (viral video amplification, boycott hashtag management, search-result narrative control post-crisis)

## Case 10

**Company:** Theranos, Inc.
**Industry:** Health Technology / Medical Diagnostics
**Year:** 2015–2018
**Crisis Type:** Corporate fraud, product safety, investor deception

**Trigger Event:** In October 2015, Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published an investigation revealing that Theranos's blood-testing technology did not work as claimed — the company was running the vast majority of its tests on conventional third-party machines rather than its proprietary "Edison" devices, while telling investors, regulators, and patients otherwise.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to Delayed
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) lab-certification enforcement; SEC civil fraud charges (filed March 2018) for violating Section 17(a) of the Securities Act and Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act; DOJ criminal wire-fraud indictment of Elizabeth Holmes and Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani (June 2018) under 18 U.S.C. § 1343.

**Resolution Status:** Fatal (company dissolved; founder criminally convicted)

**What Went Right:**
- Very little in the immediate response. Once regulatory and legal exposure became undeniable, Theranos did allow CMS to inspect its Newark, California lab and did eventually stop patient testing at Walgreens locations rather than continue exposing patients to inaccurate results.
- After the SEC charges, Holmes and the company settled relatively quickly (within days) rather than prolonging civil litigation, which limited some additional exposure.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Theranos's initial public response to the October 2015 WSJ story was outright denial, claiming Carreyrou's reporting was inaccurate and that the CMS investigation was outdated and did not reflect the lab's "current state" — a claim CMS's own findings contradicted.
- The company used aggressive legal intimidation against whistleblowers (Tyler Shultz and Erika Cheung) and threatened Carreyrou himself, which is documented in Carreyrou's book *Bad Blood* and in subsequent reporting — a strategy that suppressed information rather than addressing it.
- CMS's fall 2015 inspection found the Newark lab caused "immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety," yet Theranos continued operating and marketing its tests to consumers for months afterward.
- Leadership concealed the true testing methodology from board members, investors, and business partners (including Walgreens and Safeway) for years, and continued raising money — over $700 million total — based on the false claims even after internal staff raised concerns.
- There was no meaningful public accountability or corrective communication until regulators forced disclosure; the company never proactively self-reported.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The moment internal engineers and lab staff (as later testimony showed, as early as 2013–2014) raised credible doubts about test accuracy, Theranos should have paused patient-facing testing and commissioned an independent technical audit rather than suppressing dissent through NDAs and legal threats. When investigative reporting is credible and sourced from insiders, flat denial is not a viable strategy — it converts a product-quality problem into a fraud and concealment problem, which is what triggered the criminal charges. Any health-technology company should have a technical crisis protocol: independent verification and voluntary reporting to CMS/FDA before a journalist forces the issue.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: Theranos's valuation collapsed from a peak of roughly $9 billion to zero; the company dissolved in September 2018. Investors lost the full $700+ million raised. Reputational: catastrophic and personal — Holmes was convicted on four counts of investor fraud in January 2022 and sentenced to over 11 years in prison; Balwani was separately convicted. The case remains one of the most cited startup-fraud examples in US business history and is the subject of multiple books, a podcast, and an HBO documentary.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (long-tail reputation contamination — "Theranos" is now a permanent negative-association keyword), Crisis Comms (case study in denial failure), Legal Takedown context (whistleblower suppression is the opposite of best practice)

**Key Sources:**

- NPR, "SEC Charges Theranos Founder Elizabeth Holmes With 'Elaborate, Years-Long Fraud,'" March 15, 2018
- The Washington Post via Fox News, indictment coverage, June 2018
- Ethics Unwrapped (UT Austin), "Theranos' Bad Blood"

## Case 11

**Company:** General Motors Company (GM)
**Industry:** Automotive Manufacturing
**Year:** 2014 (recall crisis); resolution through 2015
**Crisis Type:** Product safety defect, corporate concealment, regulatory violation

**Trigger Event:** On February 7, 2014, GM issued its first recall — about 800,000 Chevrolet Cobalts and Pontiac G5s — for an ignition switch defect that could slip out of the "run" position while driving, cutting power steering, power brakes, and disabling the airbags. GM internal records later showed engineers had identified the defect as early as 2001–2004 and rejected a low-cost fix in 2005 for cost reasons.

**Response Type:** Delayed (for the underlying defect), transitioning to Proactive (once public)
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 (measured from first internal knowledge) — the public recall itself, once triggered, was followed by relatively fast subsequent disclosure
**Transparency Score:** 2/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act (five-day defect-reporting requirement, which GM violated by roughly a decade); NHTSA civil penalty ($35 million, the maximum allowed at the time, for the reporting delay); DOJ criminal investigation resulting in a three-year Deferred Prosecution Agreement (September 2015) with a $900 million forfeiture and wire-fraud/concealment admissions; extensive product-liability litigation (In re: General Motors LLC Ignition Switch Litigation, MDL 14-2543).

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (through settlement, compensation fund, and corporate restructuring) but with fatal underlying outcomes — 124 deaths formally recognized through the compensation program

**What Went Right:**
- Once the scale of the problem became public in early-to-mid 2014, CEO Mary Barra (who had just become CEO in January 2014) testified before Congress, commissioned an independent internal investigation led by attorney Anton Valukas, and publicly released its findings rather than keeping them confidential.
- GM fired 15 employees identified by the Valukas report as having known about the defect and failed to act, a visible accountability action.
- GM created and funded an independent victim compensation program administered by Kenneth Feinberg, which ultimately recognized 124 deaths and paid out roughly $594.5 million to claimants — notably, GM did not contest claims using the standard legal defenses (like the bankruptcy shield) available to it, which was viewed as a meaningful goodwill gesture.
- Barra publicly and repeatedly took ownership, stating the company would not "shirk from ... responsibility" and describing the failure as a product of a broken internal culture rather than isolated bad actors.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The core failure was a decade-long concealment: internal emails and engineering records showed GM knew about the switch problem by the mid-2000s and chose not to fix it due to a low-cost part (roughly 57 cents) that would have required retooling.
- GM ordered 500,000 replacement ignition switches from its supplier roughly two months before publicly announcing the recall (per emails reported by the Wall Street Journal in November 2014), showing the company was already acting internally while still not disclosing to regulators or the public.
- The company disputed the death toll for a period even after Barra's public contrition, initially standing by a lower official number (13 deaths) versus the eventually recognized 124.
- Legal and engineering teams reportedly settled individual lawsuits quietly over the years without connecting the pattern internally or escalating it to leadership or regulators, a systemic information-siloing failure documented in the Valukas report.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** A safety defect identified by engineers should trigger a mandatory escalation and disclosure process regardless of unit cost to fix — GM's own reporting obligation under federal law is five days from discovery, and the company missed that window by roughly a decade. Product-liability settlements that quietly resolve individual cases without a cross-functional pattern review are a governance failure; GM needed (and later built, post-crisis) a centralized safety-issue tracking system that flags recurring complaints tied to the same part number. Public leadership should acknowledge the full scope of harm early and avoid disputing casualty figures, since revised-upward death tolls compound reputational damage far more than one accurate, higher initial figure would have.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: $900 million criminal forfeiture, $35 million NHTSA fine, roughly $595 million in victim compensation, and billions more in related litigation and recall costs (over 30 million vehicles recalled worldwide). Reputational: severe short-term damage and congressional criticism, but Barra's handling of the public phase of the crisis (transparency, firings, compensation fund) is frequently cited in business-school case studies as a relatively strong recovery model layered on top of an indefensible original failure.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms (executive accountability messaging), Legal Takedown / regulatory framework awareness, ORM (long-term "GM ignition switch" search association management)

**Key Sources:**

- IBTimes UK, "General Motors to pay $900m fine over faulty ignition switches that led to over 100 deaths," 2015

## Case 12

**Company:** Takata Corporation (Japanese manufacturer; crisis centered on US market/regulatory response)
**Industry:** Automotive Parts (airbag inflators)
**Year:** 2013–2017 (US regulatory and criminal resolution through 2017)
**Crisis Type:** Product safety defect, regulatory concealment, mass recall

**Trigger Event:** Beginning in 2013–2014, multiple automakers (Honda, Toyota, Nissan, Mazda, GM, and others) recalled millions of US vehicles after it emerged that Takata's ammonium-nitrate-based airbag inflators could rupture violently when deployed, spraying metal shrapnel into the passenger cabin — Takata executives had reportedly known of rupture risk since as early as 2000 without informing automakers or regulators.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to Delayed under regulatory pressure
**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
**Transparency Score:** 1/5

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** NHTSA defect-reporting and recall enforcement authority; record civil penalty (up to $200 million, announced November 2015); DOJ criminal wire-fraud prosecution resulting in a guilty plea (February 27, 2017) and $1 billion settlement ($25 million criminal fine, $125 million victim compensation fund, $850 million restitution to automakers); indictment of three Takata executives for falsifying test data; Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing (June 2017).

**Resolution Status:** Fatal (company ceased to exist as an independent entity; assets acquired by Key Safety Systems/Joyson)

**What Went Right:**
- Once under sustained federal pressure in late 2014, Takata did eventually cooperate with NHTSA's expanded nationwide recall demand rather than continuing to resist it indefinitely, and its CEO stepped down in late December 2014 along with pay cuts for top executives as accountability gestures.
- Takata ultimately pleaded guilty rather than contesting the criminal case through trial, which allowed a compensation structure for victims and automakers to be established more quickly than prolonged litigation would have.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Internally, Takata executives identified inflator rupture risk as early as 2000 and, per DOJ findings, manipulated and falsified testing data for over a decade rather than disclosing the defect — this is the central fact pattern behind the wire-fraud charges.
- Takata initially resisted NHTSA's requests to expand recalls beyond four high-humidity states (Florida, and Gulf Coast/Southern states) in 2014, arguing the ammonium-nitrate risk was climate-specific, even as deaths mounted; it took a formal NHTSA "special order" and continued congressional pressure through December 2014 before Takata agreed to a nationwide recall.
- At a December 2014 congressional hearing, a Takata senior VP testified defending the company's position while automaker representatives and lawmakers openly criticized Takata's slow and incomplete data-sharing.
- The defect ultimately became the largest automotive recall in US history — roughly 67–70 million inflators — because early, narrow recalls repeatedly proved insufficient and had to be expanded in stages (2014, 2015, 2016, 2019).
- At least 16–27 deaths worldwide (over a dozen in the US) and 400+ injuries are linked to the defect, several occurring after Takata had internal knowledge of the risk.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** A parts supplier that identifies a catastrophic failure mode in a safety-critical product should report to regulators and all client automakers immediately and support an appropriately scoped recall from the outset — not negotiate the geographic or climate scope of a recall while people are dying. Falsifying test data to conceal a known defect converts a product-engineering failure into a criminal fraud case; any credible internal test-data discrepancy should trigger a third-party audit before external reporting, not after. Given that inflator degradation was linked to heat and humidity, Takata's data should have driven a broader, faster geographic rollout rather than the narrowest possible recall scope, which required NHTSA to force four separate recall expansions over roughly two years.

**Estimated Impact:** Financial: $1 billion in fines/restitution, company liquidation via Chapter 11 bankruptcy in June 2017, tens of billions in industry-wide recall and replacement costs borne by automakers. Reputational: total — Takata's brand no longer exists as an independent company; the case remains a standard reference point in automotive-safety and product-liability crisis training worldwide.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM (permanent negative brand association even post-acquisition), Crisis Comms (case study in regulatory stonewalling), Legal Takedown / regulatory-framework relevance for supply-chain safety clients

### Notes on scoring methodology applied above
Response Speed and Transparency scores were assigned per the stated rubric: a score of 1 reflects either no proper public response or a response later shown to be false/misleading; a score of 5 would require an accurate, accountable response within 24–48 hours. Theranos and Takata both score at the floor on both dimensions because their public-facing statements during the critical window were affirmatively false or evasive, not merely slow. GM's *public* crisis response (post-February 2014) was considerably better than its *internal* decade-long concealment — the score above reflects the concealment period, since that is the period during which the actual crisis was generated. Starbucks is the only case in this set where a same-day response occurred and the company was not concealing prior knowledge of misconduct, hence the higher scores.
