Case ID: 30
Company: Exxon Corporation
Industry: Oil & gas
Year: 1989

Crisis Type: Environmental disaster / industrial accident

Trigger Event: The tanker Exxon Valdez struck Bligh Reef in Alaska's Prince William Sound just after midnight on March 24, 1989, spilling roughly 11 million gallons of crude oil into a remote, ecologically sensitive area.

Response Type: Denial-Silence, then Delayed
Response Speed Score: 1
Transparency Score: 2

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: Clean Water Act; state of Alaska civil suit; federal criminal charges (later the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, passed directly because of this spill)

Resolution Status: Partially Resolved

What Went Right:

By summer 1989, Exxon had scaled up to roughly 10,000 workers, 1,000 vessels, 38 skimmers, and 72 aircraft on the cleanup — a genuinely massive resource commitment once mobilized.
Exxon ultimately funded cleanup and restitution totaling roughly $2.1–$2.2 billion in cleanup costs, plus a 1991 settlement of $25 million (criminal plea), $100 million (criminal restitution), and $900 million (civil settlement) with state and federal governments.
Set up a media center in Valdez to centralize press access rather than leaving it fragmented.

What Went Wrong:

CEO Lawrence Rawl did not travel to Alaska and made no public statement for roughly a week — missing what crisis-communications literature calls the "golden hour." Silence was read publicly as indifference.
Exxon had no crisis communications plan and no dedicated PR leadership in place when the spill happened, despite operating tankers in a documented high-risk strait.
When Rawl finally did speak — first via a full-page newspaper apology ad, later in interviews — he focused on cleanup logistics, not accountability, and largely avoided personal apology or admission of fault until the 1991 legal settlement forced the issue.
Cleanup equipment that industry contingency plans promised was on hand was in practice unreasonably slow to deploy and inadequate for the scale of the spill — a National Response Team report to the President explicitly found industry and government plans "wholly insufficient."
Alaska separately sued Exxon for mismanaging the response itself, seeking over $1 billion in damages — meaning the response, not just the spill, became a second legal liability.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: A company operating a known high-risk asset (tankers in a narrow strait) needs a standing crisis response plan and a media-trained executive ready to appear on-site within 24–48 hours — not a week later via a newspaper ad. The CEO's physical presence at the site in the first 48 hours is not optional theater; its absence became the lasting image of the crisis, arguably more than the spill itself. Equipment and personnel contingency plans need to be pre-staged and tested, not just written down — "we have a plan" that fails on execution is functionally the same as no plan.

Estimated Impact: ~$2.1–2.2 billion in cleanup costs; ~$1 billion in settlement/restitution beyond that; killed an estimated 250,000+ seabirds and roughly 3,000 sea otters; Rawl's handling of the crisis is still taught in business schools as a textbook case of reputational mismanagement; directly triggered the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, a permanent regulatory legacy.

Onlyne Relevance: Crisis Comms — the foundational case for "CEO visibility in the first 48 hours" as a crisis-response principle; still the reference point cited against companies that go quiet after a disaster.

Key Sources: EPA.gov Exxon Valdez Spill Profile; NOAA response.restoration.noaa.gov; Christian Science Monitor (April 1989, apology ad text); Auburn University Harbert College case archive ("The Wreck of the Exxon Valdez"); Britannica.

Case ID: 31
Company: Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E)
Industry: Utilities / energy
Year: 2017–2018

Crisis Type: Infrastructure failure leading to mass-casualty disaster

Trigger Event: Aging and poorly maintained PG&E electrical equipment ignited a series of Northern California wildfires — at least 17 in October 2017 and the November 2018 Camp Fire, which destroyed the town of Paradise and killed 85 people.

Response Type: Delayed, bordering on Denial-Silence in the early phase
Response Speed Score: 2
Transparency Score: 2

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) investigation; CAL FIRE investigation; Butte County criminal prosecution; federal Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceedings; inverse condemnation liability under California law

Resolution Status: Partially Resolved

What Went Right:

Once CAL FIRE's investigative findings became undeniable, PG&E did not fight the criminal case to trial — it pleaded guilty in June 2020 to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter and one felony count of unlawfully starting a fire, a rare instance of a major corporation accepting direct criminal accountability rather than prolonged denial.
Reached large-scale victim compensation: a $1 billion settlement with 18 public entities, an $11 billion settlement with insurance companies, and a Fire Victim Trust to pay individual claims.
Used Chapter 11 bankruptcy (filed January 29, 2019) as a structured mechanism to fund the roughly $30 billion in wildfire liabilities rather than leaving victims to chase an insolvent company outside of court oversight.

What Went Wrong:

CEO Geisha Williams resigned the day before the bankruptcy announcement, and the bankruptcy filing itself came across as reactive damage control rather than a proactively announced plan — there was no clear, early public accounting of what caused the fires or what PG&E was doing about known equipment risk before liability numbers forced the company's hand.
The CPUC's investigative report cited 12 separate violations of state law tied to the Camp Fire, and Butte County's DA said equipment maintenance failures were the root cause — meaning this was a known, preventable risk, not a surprise.
The pattern repeated: PG&E had already caused fires in 2015 (Butte Fire) and 2017 (North Bay fires) tied to equipment failures before the 2018 Camp Fire, and the same maintenance/vegetation-management gaps kept resurfacing across incidents rather than being fixed after the first one.
The Butte County DA publicly noted the state could only fine PG&E $10,000 per death because corporations can't be jailed — highlighting how disproportionately small the criminal penalty ($3.5 million total) was relative to 85 deaths, which fed a public narrative that PG&E treated fines as a cost of doing business.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: When a company has already had two prior fire-liability incidents (2015, 2017) tied to the same category of equipment failure, the third incident is not a "crisis" in the classic sense — it's a foreseeable recurrence, and it should be treated publicly as such: an immediate, detailed account of what changed (or didn't) after the prior incidents, not a resignation-then-bankruptcy sequence that reads as retreat. Infrastructure-liability companies need pre-built financial and legal response frameworks (insurance, victim trusts, maintenance audit trails) ready before the third strike, not assembled under bankruptcy court pressure after it.

Estimated Impact: ~$30 billion in total wildfire liabilities; Chapter 11 bankruptcy (filed 2019); $2.137 billion CPUC penalty; $11 billion insurance settlement; $1 billion public-entity settlement; 85 deaths in the Camp Fire alone; first-ever homicide charges against a major U.S. utility.

Onlyne Relevance: Crisis Comms / ORM — a case study in repeated-incident reputational compounding: each new fire eroded the credibility of PG&E's prior "we've fixed it" messaging.

Key Sources: NPR (PG&E guilty plea, June 2020); California Wildfire Lawsuit Info (timeline); Wisner Baum law firm case summary; PBS NewsHour (bankruptcy announcement); Reuters via Yahoo News (equipment/Camp Fire charges).

Case ID: 32
Company: United Airlines
Industry: Aviation
Year: 2017

Crisis Type: Customer mistreatment / viral incident

Trigger Event: On April 9, 2017, airport police forcibly dragged Dr. David Dao, a paying passenger, off an overbooked United Express flight at Chicago O'Hare after he refused to give up his seat; the incident, including his bloodied face, was filmed by other passengers and went viral within hours.

Response Type: Denial-Silence initially, then Delayed correction
Response Speed Score: 2
Transparency Score: 2

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: No direct federal regulatory violation, but triggered a U.S. Senate Commerce Committee inquiry and formal information demand to United's CEO; renewed FAA/DOT scrutiny of airline overbooking practices

Resolution Status: Resolved (reputationally costly, but resolved)

What Went Right:

After the initial missteps, United commissioned and released a full internal review of the incident and adopted concrete policy changes, including no longer using law enforcement to remove passengers for space and requiring crew booking 60 minutes before departure.
CEO Oscar Munoz eventually delivered a direct, unambiguous apology and accepted "full responsibility," explicitly not blaming the city of Chicago or Dao — this was cited by Dao's own attorney as praiseworthy.
Reached a confidential settlement with Dao within about three weeks of the incident, avoiding a prolonged public lawsuit that would have kept the story in the news longer.

What Went Wrong:

Munoz's first statement, issued the same day, referred to "re-accommodating" customers — corporate euphemism that read as tone-deaf against video of a bloodied man being dragged down an aisle, and it became the most-mocked line of the entire crisis.
Munoz then sent an internal email (which leaked) describing Dao as "disruptive" and "belligerent" — a characterization that directly contradicted what the video showed and what other passengers described, turning a service failure into a credibility failure.
It took roughly three days and mounting public/political pressure (including criticism from the sitting U.S. President) before Munoz issued a real apology calling the event "truly horrific" — the gap between the incident and a genuine apology is what fueled the viral backlash cycle, not the original incident alone.
When later asked whether anyone, including himself, would be fired over the incident, Munoz said no — a response that read as insufficient accountability even after the apology tour.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: The first public statement after a filmed incident should never use internal operational language ("re-accommodate") to describe what the public is watching happen to a human being — the statement needs to match what viewers can see with their own eyes, or it will be treated as spin. Internal communications (like the leaked email) need to be written as if they will become public, because in a viral-video crisis they usually do. And the apology needs to happen within the first news cycle, not the third — Munoz's real, credible apology on day three came too late to prevent the story from becoming about the response rather than the incident.

Estimated Impact: United's stock dropped roughly $1.4 billion in market value within days of the video going viral (subsequently recovered); confidential settlement paid to Dao; lasting brand association with the phrase "re-accommodate"; prompted industry-wide airline policy changes on overbooking and law-enforcement removal.

Onlyne Relevance: Crisis Comms / ORM — the reference case for "language mismatch" crises, where the words used in the official statement, more than the incident itself, drove the search and social backlash.

Key Sources: CNBC (April 11, 2017, Munoz apology); NBC News (initial and follow-up statements); Encyclopedia MDPI (United Express Flight 3411 incident summary); Patch.com (settlement and second apology); CBC News (no-firing statement).

Case ID: 33
Company: Starbucks
Industry: Food & beverage / retail
Year: 2018

Crisis Type: Racial discrimination incident / viral video

Trigger Event: On April 12, 2018, a Philadelphia Starbucks manager called police on two Black men, Rashon Nelson and Donte Robinson, who were waiting at a table for a business meeting without having purchased anything; police handcuffed and arrested them, and video of the arrest went viral.

Response Type: Reactive (fast) then Proactive
Response Speed Score: 4
Transparency Score: 4

Legal/Regulatory Framework Triggered: No federal charge against Starbucks itself; triggered civil settlement with the City of Philadelphia and separate settlement with Nelson and Robinson; renewed scrutiny of company policies against Title II-adjacent public accommodation norms

Resolution Status: Resolved

What Went Right:

CEO Kevin Johnson called the arrests "reprehensible" and apologized publicly within days, then personally flew from Seattle to Philadelphia over the following weekend to meet the mayor, police commissioner, and eventually apologize face-to-face to Nelson and Robinson.
On April 17 — five days after the incident — Starbucks announced it would close more than 8,000 U.S. company-owned stores on May 29 for company-wide racial-bias training for roughly 175,000 employees, at an estimated cost of $12 million, and actually followed through on the date.
Built the training curriculum with outside experts, including Bryan Stevenson (Equal Justice Initiative), Sherrilyn Ifill (NAACP Legal Defense Fund), and former U.S. Attorney General Eric Holder — bringing in credible third-party voices rather than designing the response internally alone.
Confirmed the employee who called police no longer worked at the store, and reached settlements with both the city ($1 each plus a program for young entrepreneurs) and with Nelson and Robinson (undisclosed sum plus a free-college-tuition program offer) within about three weeks.
Johnson did not shift blame to police or the city; he accepted the incident as a Starbucks policy and culture failure.

What Went Wrong:

The underlying store policy that let an employee call police on customers for simply waiting without a purchase was itself the root cause, and it existed nationwide before this incident forced a review — meaning the crisis exposed a systemic policy gap Starbucks hadn't proactively addressed.
Philadelphia's police commissioner had to separately walk back his own initial defense of the officers days later, which kept the story alive across two authorities rather than resolving quickly.
A second, unrelated incident (a racial slur written on a customer's cup at a California store) surfaced during the same news cycle, undercutting the "isolated incident" framing Starbucks needed for the story to de-escalate.

Best Practice / What Should Have Happened: This case is a strong positive contrast to Exxon/PG&E/United: apologize fast, apologize specifically (not "we regret any inconvenience"), fix the underlying policy rather than just the individual incident, and bring in credible external experts rather than self-designing the fix. The main lingering lesson is upstream — the store policy that made the arrest possible in the first place should have been audited before an incident forced it, not after.

Estimated Impact: ~$12 million cost for the one-day, 8,000-store training closure; undisclosed settlement with Nelson and Robinson; $1 symbolic settlement plus youth entrepreneurship program funding for Philadelphia; short-term stock and same-store sales impact was limited — largely attributed to the speed and visibility of the response; reputational recovery was faster than the other cases in this set.

Onlyne Relevance: ORM / Crisis Comms — the go-to "fast and credible apology" comparison case when advising clients on what a well-executed response looks like versus Exxon, PG&E, or United.

Key Sources: NBC News (April 23, 2018, apology and Nelson/Robinson interview); MIT Sloan (cost and analysis); NPR (May 29, 2018, training day); Press Reader/AP wire coverage (April 18, 2018, closure announcement); ABC News (April 23, 2018, police commissioner apology).
