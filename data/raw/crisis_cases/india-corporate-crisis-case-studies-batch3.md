## Case 009

**Company:** DHFL (Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited)
**Industry:** Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) / Housing Finance
**Year:** 2019
**Crisis Type:** Financial Fraud / Fund Diversion / NBFC Collapse

**Trigger Event:** On 29 January 2019, investigative outlet Cobrapost published a report alleging DHFL's promoters (the Wadhawan family) had siphoned more than ₹31,000 crore of public money through loans to shell companies, published after market hours the same day the story broke, sending DHFL's stock down further on top of an already 60% decline since the September 2018 IL&FS-triggered NBFC liquidity crisis.

**Response Type:** Denial, then Delayed-Reactive

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*DHFL issued a same-day-ish denial calling the Cobrapost allegations "baseless," which technically meets a fast response-speed window but failed the "stayed accurate" test — the company's own later-appointed forensic auditor (KPMG) confirmed fund siphoning to promoter-linked entities in October 2019, roughly nine months later, directly contradicting the initial denial. DHFL did appoint an independent firm (TP Ostwal & Associates) to review the allegations, but that review gave a broad "clean chit" while still flagging discrepancies — a mixed signal that didn't resolve the credibility question the way a truly independent, adversarial audit would have.*

**Transparency Score:** 1
*The gap between DHFL's initial denial and the eventual confirmed findings (KPMG's special review covering April 2015–March 2019, confirming siphoning to promoter-led entities) is the core transparency failure: the company spent most of 2019 disputing the story while its own commissioned reviewers were finding real problems. DHFL also deferred its FY19 (March quarter) financial results in June 2019, citing "non-availability of directors," at a time when sources indicated the real reason was the auditor (Deloitte) raising additional queries about related-party fund deployment — a euphemism for a governance problem, not a scheduling one.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) probe recommended by the Registrar of Companies (Mumbai) in October 2019, following its own report citing "good enough evidence for money diversion and fund siphoning"; Enforcement Directorate action under PMLA; CBI case against Rana Kapoor and the Wadhawan brothers over a related Yes Bank–DHFL quid-pro-quo loan arrangement; eventual RBI supersession of the DHFL board and referral to the NCLT under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (following the same broad regulatory playbook used for IL&FS).

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (via forced resolution, not remediation)
*DHFL was eventually taken over via NCLT-supervised insolvency resolution; Piramal Capital & Housing Finance acquired DHFL in 2021 after a competitive bidding process. The underlying fraud allegations against the Wadhawans remain in prosecution (CBI/ED cases), so criminal accountability is not yet fully resolved even though the corporate entity itself has been restructured and continues operating under new ownership.*

**What Went Right:**
- DHFL did commission an outside review (TP Ostwal & Associates) relatively quickly after the Cobrapost story broke, rather than relying purely on internal assurance — even though the review's conclusions were later undercut by KPMG's more thorough forensic audit.
- The regulatory system around DHFL worked as a check even where the company didn't: the Registrar of Companies, SFIO, KPMG's forensic audit, and eventually RBI/NCLT all functioned as independent verification layers that surfaced the truth despite company denials.
- The eventual NCLT-run bidding process for DHFL's sale (leading to the Piramal acquisition) was conducted with reasonable market transparency, preserving some enterprise value rather than a disorderly collapse.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Calling detailed, evidence-based allegations "baseless" the same day they were published — before any real investigation had occurred — was the single most damaging move: it set up an inevitable credibility collapse once independent forensic findings (KPMG, RoC) confirmed the substance of the claims months later.
- DHFL deferred disclosure of a routine quarterly financial result rather than being upfront that its own auditor had raised specific, serious concerns about related-party fund deployment — using process language ("non-availability of directors") to obscure a substantive governance problem.
- The company continued to raise public deposits and maintain investor-facing communications suggesting business as usual for months after credible fraud allegations surfaced, without proactively segmenting or flagging risk to smaller retail depositors and bondholders who had the least ability to independently assess the company's real financial state.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
When facing detailed, well-sourced fraud allegations, a company should never issue a flat denial before commissioning a genuinely independent, forensic-grade audit — a same-day "baseless" statement that is later contradicted by the company's own commissioned auditors is far more reputationally damaging than an initial "we take this seriously and are commissioning an independent review, findings to follow within X weeks" holding statement. Any deferred financial disclosure should be accompanied by a clear, specific reason (auditor queries on related-party transactions) rather than a vague administrative excuse — vague excuses are read by the market as confirmation that something is being hidden, often accelerating the very stock and confidence collapse the company is trying to avoid. Retail depositors and bondholders, who lack access to forensic information, deserve the most conservative and protective communication in a fund-diversion scenario — DHFL's continued normal-business messaging while allegations were live left the least sophisticated stakeholders most exposed.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Cobrapost alleged over ₹31,000 crore in diverted funds; total lender exposure across 32 Indian and foreign banks reached roughly ₹97,000 crore; DHFL's market cap had already fallen 60% before the Cobrapost report and continued declining afterward; SBI and Bank of Baroda alone had exposures of over ₹11,000 crore and ₹4,000 crore respectively. Reputational: DHFL became, alongside IL&FS, one of the two defining case studies of India's 2018–2019 NBFC/shadow-banking crisis, and the case materially damaged trust in NBFC sector self-reporting and rating agency oversight.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (a clear cautionary example of why an immediate "baseless" denial to detailed fraud allegations is a high-risk strategy that can backfire badly once independent findings emerge)

## Case 010

**Company:** Jet Airways
**Industry:** Aviation
**Year:** 2019
**Crisis Type:** Financial Collapse / Operational Shutdown / Governance Crisis

**Trigger Event:** After years of accumulating losses (posting losses in 8 of its final 10 years) and mounting debt exceeding $1 billion, Jet Airways had stopped paying employee salaries since January 2019; on 17 April 2019, with lessors repossessing aircraft and the company unable to secure emergency funding, the airline suspended all flight operations entirely.

**Response Type:** Delayed / Reactive

**Response Speed Score:** 2
*The company and lenders were aware of the severity of the crisis well before the April grounding — early January 2019 meetings between Jet Airways and lead lender SBI with aircraft lessors were meant to project a rescue plan, but sources describe the bank failing to provide details and founder Naresh Goyal reportedly reacting with visible frustration at lessors during the meeting, which itself accelerated distrust and prompted lessors to begin repossessing aircraft. Founder Naresh Goyal and his wife did step down from the board on 25 March 2019 as a condition set by prospective rescuer Etihad and lender SBI — a clear, if very late-stage, governance concession — but this came only weeks before total operational collapse, not early enough to prevent it.*

**Transparency Score:** 2
*SBI publicly described bankruptcy as a "last option" as late as March 2019 while simultaneously acknowledging Jet's debt exceeded $1 billion and salary payments had already stopped for months — a gap between public reassurance and internal severity that likely delayed decisive market and employee-facing action. Two independent directors, Ashok Chawla and Sharad Sharma, resigned in June 2019 explicitly citing the board's continuing non-compliance with Companies Act provisions and the resolution plan "not moving forward" — an unusually direct, specific public statement of governance failure from departing directors, which added a rare moment of real transparency to an otherwise opaque process.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Companies Act compliance requirements (explicitly cited by resigning independent directors); SEBI LODR disclosure norms; eventual referral by the SBI-led lender consortium to the NCLT under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (June 2019); later Enforcement Directorate chargesheet against Naresh Goyal (2024) alleging fund diversion and fraud related to a ₹538 crore Canara Bank case, with his wife Anita Goyal also arrested and later granted bail in a related chargesheet (November 2023).

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved / Effectively Fatal (as an operating airline)
*Jet Airways has not resumed commercial flight operations since April 2019. An NCLT-approved resolution plan (led by the Jalan-Kalrock Consortium) was eventually approved after a prolonged bidding process, but implementation has been repeatedly delayed — as recently as 2024, the company was still reporting delays in even finalizing financial results, and the Supreme Court ultimately ordered liquidation of the airline, ending any realistic prospect of the brand flying again.*

**What Went Right:**
- SBI, as lead lender, did attempt a structured, multi-lender rescue process (interim management committee, bidding process for a stake sale) rather than allowing an immediate disorderly default — this created at least a chance at revival, even though it ultimately failed.
- The government reportedly pushed for a rescue-without-bankruptcy approach specifically to protect thousands of jobs, showing at least an initial policy-level effort to manage the human impact of the collapse.
- Independent directors who resigned in June 2019 did so with specific, documented reasons tied to actual governance failures rather than quiet, unexplained departures — this gave the market and employees real information rather than more ambiguity.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Salaries went unpaid from January 2019, but the company continued operating and the situation was only escalated to full grounding in April — a three-month period in which employees bore financial hardship without a clear, binding commitment on when or whether they would be paid.
- SBI's public "bankruptcy is a last option" framing in March 2019, even as the airline was demonstrably already failing operationally, created a mismatch between public messaging and internal reality that likely delayed both employee and creditor decision-making.
- Naresh Goyal's confrontational conduct in the January 2019 lessor meeting (described as "banging his fist on a table" and defying lessors) is cited by multiple sources as a specific trigger that accelerated aircraft repossessions — a case where a leader's in-person conduct during a high-stakes negotiation directly worsened the crisis trajectory.
- The exit of the founder came only after outside parties (Etihad, SBI) forced the condition — not as a proactive governance correction — meaning the single most consequential leadership change happened reactively and too late to preserve the airline's cash position.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Once salary non-payment began in January 2019, the company and lead lender should have communicated a clear, binding timeline to employees and lessors rather than allowing months of ambiguity — uncertainty itself was a major driver of lessor repossessions and eventual total grounding. Leadership conduct in creditor and lessor negotiations matters concretely: a defiant or confrontational posture toward parties who control the physical assets (aircraft) an airline needs to operate is operationally self-defeating, not just a communications misstep. Governance changes that outside stakeholders are going to force eventually (founder stepping back) are far more valuable if made proactively and early — Jet's board or major shareholders should have pushed for a change in control months before Etihad and SBI made it a hard condition, by which point the company had already burned through its cash runway.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Total liabilities of roughly ₹25,000 crore (~$3.5 billion) including public-sector bank dues of ₹8,500 crore and other creditor claims; shareholder value largely wiped out (shares fell over 50% intraday on the NCLT referral news alone). Reputational: The collapse of one of India's oldest and most recognized private airlines became a widely cited case study in Indian aviation-sector mismanagement and founder-control risk; thousands of job losses drew sustained public and political attention.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / ORM (illustrates how public reassurance that contradicts visible operational reality — unpaid salaries, grounded planes — accelerates rather than contains a reputational and financial crisis)

## Case 011

**Company:** Sahara Group / Subrata Roy
**Industry:** Diversified Conglomerate (Financial Services, Real Estate, Media)
**Year:** 2011–2014 (acute crisis); resolution ongoing through 2023
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory Non-Compliance / Investor Fund Non-Refund / Founder Arrest

**Trigger Event:** In 2011, SEBI ruled that two Sahara Group companies (Sahara India Real Estate Corporation and Sahara Housing Investment Corporation) had raised approximately ₹24,000 crore from an estimated 3 crore investors through Optionally Fully Convertible Debentures (OFCDs) in violation of securities regulations, and ordered immediate refunds with interest — an order Sahara contested rather than complied with.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence, escalating to open defiance of court orders

**Response Speed Score:** 1
*Sahara did not comply with SEBI's 2011 order, and continued contesting it through appeals rather than refunding investors, even after the Supreme Court upheld SEBI's directions on 31 August 2012 (ordering repayment within 90 days, later extended). Rather than complying with the extended deadline, the company disputed the very amount it owed, claiming its liability was closer to ₹5,120 crore (having already repaid investors directly, bypassing SEBI) versus the ₹24,000 crore SEBI said remained outstanding — a dispute over facts that persisted for years rather than being resolved through documentation Sahara could have supplied promptly.*

**Transparency Score:** 1
*SEBI froze the bank accounts of the two Sahara companies in February 2013 after they failed to refund the money as ordered — a strong signal of concealment/non-cooperation rather than an honest accounting dispute. When Subrata Roy failed to appear before the Supreme Court in February 2014 (citing his mother's illness) after previously being denied an exemption from personal appearance, the court's own characterization was pointed: "the arms of this court are very long... we are issuing a warrant against him." Roy's own public statement after his arrest — accusing media of "bullying and character assassination" rather than substantively addressing the refund shortfall — reinforced a pattern of deflection rather than disclosure.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** SEBI Act enforcement order (2011); Supreme Court order (31 August 2012) upholding SEBI's refund directive with 15% interest; SEBI account-freeze action (February 2013); Supreme Court contempt/non-bailable warrant proceedings leading to Roy's arrest (28 February–4 March 2014) and imprisonment at Tihar Jail; ongoing court-supervised asset sales (including the high-profile Aamby Valley property) to recover investor funds; a Supreme Court-directed refund mechanism later placed under the Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS) with a public online claims portal, continuing even after Roy's death in November 2023.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved (over a decade-plus timeline)
*Roy spent over two years in custody/parole conditions from March 2014 before securing extended parole, and the underlying refund process was still incomplete as of his death in November 2023 — media reporting at that time noted over ₹25,000 crore still held with SEBI/refund mechanisms and unresolved claims from Sahara's cooperative societies. The Supreme Court directed that fund recovery and refund processing continue after Roy's death, meaning the matter remained open more than a decade after the original 2011 order.*

**What Went Right:**
- Once forced by court order and arrest, Sahara did engage with the court-supervised process, including periodic deposits toward the refund account (e.g., ₹247 crore deposited against a ₹552 crore target by mid-2017) and allowed court-ordered asset sales (Aamby Valley) to proceed as a recovery mechanism.
- The Supreme Court and SEBI maintained a consistent, rules-based process throughout — freezing accounts, ordering asset auctions, and later establishing a formal, publicly accessible refund portal — which gave affected investors a durable path to eventual recovery even without company cooperation.
- Roy's public statements after arrest, while largely deflective, did include an "unconditional apology" filed with the court for non-appearance, at least formally acknowledging the process's legitimacy rather than escalating further defiance.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The company disputed a clear regulatory order for years rather than either complying or providing verifiable documentation to support its lower liability claim — leaving nearly three crore investors in limbo for years while the dispute over "how much is actually owed" played out in court rather than through transparent, audited accounting.
- SEBI's account freeze in 2013 (roughly 18 months after the original order) indicates the company had ample time to comply voluntarily and chose not to, converting what could have been an orderly regulatory correction into a multi-year legal battle culminating in a founder's arrest.
- Roy's framing of intense media and regulatory scrutiny as "bullying and character assassination" rather than directly engaging with the substance of the refund shortfall is a textbook example of deflection that does nothing to rebuild credibility with either regulators or the millions of small investors owed money.
- The prolonged, decade-plus timeline (2011 order to still-incomplete resolution in 2023 and beyond) meant generations of small investors — many from rural and semi-urban India, per SEBI's investor base description — waited years or decades for money that was legally theirs.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
Once SEBI issued a clear refund order in 2011, the company's most credible path was full, audited compliance within the original timeline — even a partial, good-faith initial payment alongside a transparent accounting of total liability would have preserved far more institutional credibility than years of disputed appeals. Given the crisis involved millions of retail investors, Sahara should have proactively published its own investor-facing repayment ledger and process rather than leaving investors to rely entirely on court filings and media coverage for information about their own money. For any company facing a repayment or refund order from a financial regulator, the practical lesson is that disputing the order's validity for years, rather than complying and separately contesting the specific amount through documentation, converts a manageable regulatory correction into an existential legal, personal-liberty, and reputational crisis.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Original disputed amount was approximately ₹24,000 crore (with 15% interest accruing), owed to an estimated 3 crore (30 million) investors; over ₹25,000 crore reportedly remained with SEBI/refund mechanisms as of Roy's 2023 death, reflecting both accrued interest and the scale of the unresolved claims process. Reputational: Roy and Sahara became a defining Indian case study of regulatory non-compliance and founder accountability, with the personal imprisonment of a major business figure marking one of the starkest instances of an Indian court enforcing compliance against a large conglomerate's leadership.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms / ORM (a stark example of how sustained denial and non-compliance with a regulator — rather than proactive resolution — can escalate into personal imprisonment of leadership and reputational damage lasting well over a decade)

## Case 012

**Company:** Reliance Communications (RCom) / Anil Ambani
**Industry:** Telecommunications
**Year:** 2019 (acute crisis); debt buildup from 2016–2017
**Crisis Type:** Debt Default / Contempt of Court / Insolvency

**Trigger Event:** After Reliance Communications failed to pay telecom equipment maker Ericsson roughly ₹1,500 crore in dues stemming from a 2014 network management contract, and subsequently missed a Supreme Court-ordered payment deadline of 15 December 2018 for a reduced ₹550 crore settlement, Ericsson filed contempt petitions against RCom chairman Anil Ambani, leading to a Supreme Court ruling on 20 February 2019 that held Ambani personally guilty of contempt.

**Response Type:** Delayed / Reactive (compliance only under direct legal threat of imprisonment)

**Response Speed Score:** 1
*RCom had multiple opportunities to settle before matters escalated to personal contempt proceedings against its chairman: an NCLAT-ordered December 2018 deadline was missed, a subsequent Supreme Court deadline of 15 December 2018 (with 12% annual interest for delay) was also missed, and only after being explicitly told by the Supreme Court on 20 February 2019 that Ambani would face three months' imprisonment unless ₹453 crore was paid within four weeks did the company actually make the payment — using funds Ambani publicly credited to brother Mukesh Ambani and sister-in-law Nita Ambani, rather than RCom's own resources.*

<br>

**Transparency Score:** 2
*The underlying facts of the case were relatively clear and well-documented through the courts — RCom did not deny owing the money, and Ambani's public acknowledgment that he needed his brother's financial help to make the court-ordered payment was, in its own way, an unusually candid admission of the company's inability to meet even a reduced, court-mediated settlement. However, RCom's broader conduct — including a disputed August 2018 undertaking that payment would come "upon sale of assets" (which Ericsson's lawyers flagged as a bad-faith conditional promise) and a January 2019 offer to pay only if 25 separate contempt petitions and arbitration proceedings were withdrawn — reflects a pattern of using procedural and conditional maneuvering rather than straightforward compliance.*

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) arbitration award (2016) in Ericsson's favor; NCLT and NCLAT insolvency-adjacent proceedings; Supreme Court contempt of court proceedings and non-bailable threat of imprisonment against Ambani personally; RCom's own bankruptcy/insolvency filing (February 2019) after failing to sell assets to repay lenders; subsequent, separate CBI and Enforcement Directorate cases (fraud classification of RCom accounts by multiple banks including SBI, Bank of India, and Bank of Maharashtra; a CBI chargesheet naming both Anil Ambani and Rana Kapoor in a ₹2,796 crore case; fresh PMLA cases filed by the ED) that emerged in the years following the initial contempt case.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved
*Ambani avoided imprisonment by making the court-ordered Ericsson payment in March 2019, but RCom itself never resumed normal operations and remains under insolvency resolution years later, with the broader RCom-linked bank fraud allegations (SBI, Bank of India, Bank of Maharashtra classifications; CBI chargesheet) continuing to develop as recently as 2025 — indicating the underlying financial and legal issues around the group extend well beyond the original Ericsson dispute and remain actively contested.*

**What Went Right:**
- Once faced with a binding, personal-imprisonment deadline, Ambani did comply and make the payment within the court's timeframe, avoiding a scenario where a major Indian business figure was jailed for contempt — a worse reputational and legal outcome than compliance, however delayed.
- Ambani's public, direct acknowledgment of receiving financial help from his brother and sister-in-law to make the payment was a moment of unusually direct transparency about the company's actual financial incapacity, rather than obscuring the source of funds.
- The broader RCom insolvency process did proceed through formal, structured NCLT/NCLAT channels rather than an uncontrolled default, giving creditors (including smaller vendors like the PR firm Fortuna, which separately sought insolvency proceedings over unpaid dues) a defined legal process to pursue claims.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Multiple court-set deadlines (NCLAT's, then the Supreme Court's 15 December 2018 date) were missed before any real payment materialized, converting what could have been a contained commercial dispute into an escalating personal-liability crisis for the company's chairman.
- RCom's conditional payment offers — proposing to pay only if numerous other legal proceedings and contempt petitions were simultaneously withdrawn — read as attempts to leverage a legitimate debt as a bargaining chip rather than honoring a court-sanctioned settlement, which is likely part of why the court ultimately ruled contempt rather than accepting further negotiation.
- The company's stock and its broader group companies (Reliance Capital, Reliance Infrastructure, Reliance Power, Reliance Home Finance) all fell sharply (up to 10.3%) on the contempt ruling alone, showing how a single unresolved vendor dispute, left to escalate to the point of personal contempt proceedings, damaged market confidence across an entire group of affiliated companies, not just RCom.
- The case set a visible precedent — and the underlying financial distress at RCom did not resolve with the Ericsson payment; subsequent bank fraud classifications and fresh CBI/ED cases in later years indicate the core financial governance problems were never actually fixed, only the immediate imprisonment threat was averted.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
A company facing a clear, arbitration-confirmed debt (the 2016 ICC award) should prioritize meeting court-mediated settlement deadlines exactly as agreed, rather than proposing conditional undertakings ("payment upon sale of assets") that create ambiguity a creditor can reasonably challenge — this ambiguity is what directly enabled Ericsson's contempt filing. Once a court gives an explicit personal-liberty deadline (jail time for the chairman), immediate, unconditional compliance is the only credible option — RCom's attempt to link payment to withdrawal of 25 other legal proceedings in the final weeks was a negotiating tactic that backfired by hardening the court's stance. For any group with cross-affiliated listed companies, leadership should recognize that an unresolved vendor dispute at one entity can spill over into stock-price damage across an entire group — early, decisive settlement of clearly valid debts protects value well beyond the disputing entity itself.

**Estimated Impact:**
Financial: Immediate Ericsson settlement was ₹453 crore (paid in March 2019, on top of an earlier ₹118 crore deposit) against a broader RCom debt exceeding ₹46,000 crore at the time of its 2019 bankruptcy filing; Reliance Group stocks lost billions in combined market value in a single trading day (20 February 2019) following the contempt ruling. Reputational: The case became one of the most visible instances of an Indian billionaire facing genuine personal-imprisonment risk over a corporate debt, and it foreshadowed years of continuing fraud investigations against RCom and its group companies that persisted well beyond the original Ericsson dispute.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown / ORM (illustrates the reputational cost of conditional, negotiating-posture responses to a legitimate, court-recognized debt, and how personal-liability escalation can spill over into an entire affiliated group's market value)
