## CASE-001

**Company:** Johnson & Johnson (Tylenol)
**Industry:** Pharmaceuticals / Consumer Health
**Year:** 1982
**Crisis Type:** Product tampering / mass casualty (sabotage, not manufacturing defect)

**Trigger Event:** Starting September 29, 1982, seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been laced with cyanide after leaving the factory. The tampering happened at the retail level — someone opened bottles on store shelves, injected poison, and put them back.

**Response Type:** Proactive

**Response Speed Score:** 5/5 — Media and law enforcement linked the deaths to Tylenol within days of the first death (Sept 29–30). J&J pulled Tylenol advertising nationally almost immediately and warned consumers not to take any Tylenol product before the cause was even confirmed. By October 5 — seven days after the first reported death — J&J issued a full nationwide recall of all Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules, pulling 31 million bottles (about $100M+ retail value at the time). This is still the reference point for "how fast is fast enough."

**Transparency Score:** 5/5 — CEO James Burke put himself in front of press conferences early and repeatedly, gave regular updates as facts changed, opened the company's manufacturing process to FDA and press scrutiny, and did not initially know if the tampering happened in the plant or the store — he said so publicly instead of guessing.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No direct regulator had jurisdiction over "someone poisoned a product after purchase," but the case directly caused new law: the 1983 federal "Tylenol bill" made product tampering a federal offense, and the FDA's 1989 tamper-evident packaging regulations trace back to this event.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (for the company — the murders themselves remain unsolved to this day)

**What Went Right:**
- Pulled all advertising and issued a national consumer warning before knowing the full scope of the problem — chose to over-warn rather than wait for certainty.
- Full nationwide recall (not just Chicago) even though the tampering was geographically confined — this cost more but removed all ambiguity for the public.
- CEO was the visible face of the crisis from day one; no proxy spokesperson, no legal-only statements.
- Set up a toll-free consumer hotline and cooperated fully with FDA and Chicago police.
- Reintroduced Tylenol afterward in new triple-seal tamper-evident packaging and offered free replacements/exchanges for any product consumers had thrown away, plus discount coupons — a costed decision to prioritize consumer trust over near-term margin.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Not much on the response side — this is the industry-standard "gold standard" case. The pre-crisis gap was that J&J, like the rest of the industry, had no tamper-evident packaging at all, which is what made the attack possible in the first place.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** This case largely *is* the best practice. The one transferable lesson: the recall and warning went out before J&J had legal certainty about where the tampering occurred. Waiting for full facts before acting would have cost more lives and more brand equity — the calculated over-response was correct.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Recall cost estimated at $100M+ in 1982 dollars (product destroyed, replacement units, packaging redesign). Tylenol's market share reportedly dropped from ~35% to ~8% within weeks.
- Reputational: Recovered to regain roughly 30% market share within about a year of relaunch — market share loss was temporary, brand trust was actually strengthened long-term. Still taught as the reference model in business schools and crisis comms training today.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms — textbook example of "own the message before someone else does." Directly useful for client pitches on speed-to-response and CEO visibility during a product-safety crisis.

## CASE-002

**Company:** BP (British Petroleum)
**Industry:** Oil & Gas
**Year:** 2010
**Crisis Type:** Industrial disaster / environmental catastrophe

**Trigger Event:** On April 20, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig exploded in the Gulf of Mexico, killing 11 workers and triggering an uncontrolled leak that spilled roughly 4.9 million barrels of oil into the Gulf over 87 days before it was capped on July 15, 2010.

**Response Type:** Reactive, drifting into Denial-Silence at the leadership-communications level even as operational response scaled up

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — BP acted fast operationally (chartering response vessels, deploying booms) but its public communications badly lagged the scale of the disaster for weeks, and CEO Tony Hayward's public statements repeatedly understated the spill's size and impact even as evidence mounted.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 — BP was widely accused of underestimating flow-rate estimates in public statements for weeks after independent scientists' estimates were far higher; it took sustained government and media pressure (and live video feed of the leak, which BP resisted releasing) before BP's public numbers converged with reality.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** U.S. Clean Water Act, Oil Pollution Act, Department of Justice criminal investigation. BP pleaded guilty in 2013 to 14 criminal counts including 11 felony counts of manslaughter (for the 11 rig deaths) and agreed to pay $4 billion in criminal penalties. Total costs across settlements, cleanup, and fines eventually topped $65 billion.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved — the well was capped and financial/legal obligations were eventually settled, but BP's reputation in the U.S. Gulf region took years to partially recover, and the CEO lost his job as a direct result.

**What Went Right:**
- Set up a $20 billion escrow claims fund in June 2010 under government pressure — a large, concrete financial commitment that did help process compensation claims (145,000+ claims received, $319M+ paid out within months).
- Eventually did remove leadership: Hayward was replaced by American Robert Dudley effective October 1, 2010, signaling accountability once the damage was done.
- Pled guilty to criminal charges rather than fighting them out for years, which allowed some financial closure.

**What Went Wrong:**
- CEO Tony Hayward became the face of the crisis for all the wrong reasons: he was photographed at a yacht race while oil was still washing up on Gulf beaches, and said in a press interview "I want my life back" — a line that became a symbol of corporate tone-deafness during a disaster that killed 11 people.
- Public statements minimized both the leak's size and BP's role for weeks, eroding trust faster than facts on the ground.
- Confused, contradictory messaging about who was actually running point on the response (chairman said Hayward was handing off day-to-day control; other BP officials said he wasn't) — this kind of internal contradiction played out in public and made the company look disorganized at the worst possible moment.
- BP's stock lost more than $100 billion in market value in the months after the spill — much of that damage tied directly to how the crisis was communicated, not just the physical event.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** The CEO or a single, consistently briefed spokesperson should have owned every public update from day one, with numbers that matched independent scientific estimates rather than lagging them — credibility gaps get filled by media speculation, which is worse than the truth. Any executive photographed enjoying leisure time during an active fatal disaster is a communications failure that should never reach the public; personal conduct during a crisis needs the same discipline as the official statements.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Over $65 billion in total costs (cleanup, fines, settlements, claims) by various estimates; $4 billion in criminal penalties alone; over $100 billion in market value lost.
- Reputational: BP's U.S. brand trust metrics dropped sharply and took years to recover; Hayward's exit was widely covered as a direct consequence of communications failure, not just the spill itself.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Crisis Comms — strong cautionary case for "the messenger becomes the story." Useful for clients on why a single visible spokesperson with consistent, fact-matched numbers matters more than operational spend.

**Key Sources:**
- Fox News / AP wire coverage, BP CEO replacement reporting (June–July 2010)
- CBC News, "Former BP CEO Hayward makes brief appearance at oil spill trial"
- Deccan Herald, BP spill cost reporting (August 2010)
- AOL/DailyFinance, "Consequences: BP's CEO Should Get No Pay This Year"

## CASE-003

**Company:** United Airlines
**Industry:** Aviation
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Viral customer-mistreatment incident / social media backlash

**Trigger Event:** On April 9, 2017, Dr. David Dao, a passenger on United Express Flight 3411, was violently dragged off an overbooked flight at Chicago O'Hare by airport security after refusing to give up his seat. Passengers filmed the incident on phones; the video went viral within hours.

**Response Type:** Delayed, with an initial Denial-Silence phase before correcting course

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 — United's first statement (Sunday night/Monday) apologized only for having to "re-accommodate" customers and did not address the violence. CEO Oscar Munoz's first internal memo described Dao as "disruptive and belligerent" — a message that leaked and made things worse. It took until Tuesday — roughly 2 days after the incident — for Munoz to issue a real apology taking "full responsibility," and by then the story had already gone global and United's stock had dropped by roughly $1 billion in market value at the Tuesday open.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 — Munoz's early characterization of Dao as "belligerent" was directly contradicted by passenger video and other passengers' accounts (one called him "very polite, matter-of-fact"), which damaged credibility once the discrepancy became public. Transparency improved later: United completed and published a policy review by the promised deadline (April 30) and changed its overbooking/law-enforcement-removal policy.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** No new federal regulation, but the incident triggered Department of Transportation scrutiny of airline overbooking practices and renewed congressional attention to passenger rights. Dao filed a lawsuit against United and the City of Chicago, settled confidentially within about three weeks of the incident.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved — legal claim settled, policy changed, company survived — but reputational damage was severe and immediate.

**What Went Right:**
- Eventually did have the CEO apologize on camera, take personal responsibility, and commit to a public deadline (April 30) for a policy review — and that review was delivered on time.
- Changed the actual policy: United announced it would no longer call law enforcement to physically remove a paid, seated passenger for overbooking.
- Settled the lawsuit with Dao quickly (within about three weeks) rather than dragging out litigation and keeping the story alive.
- Compensated other passengers on the flight for the cost of their tickets.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The first public response minimized the incident by using corporate euphemism ("re-accommodate") instead of naming what happened.
- The leaked internal memo blaming the passenger, before any real investigation, was the single most damaging communications decision in the case — it directly contradicted video evidence that was already circulating publicly.
- The gap between the incident (Sunday night) and a real apology (Tuesday) let the story define itself on social media for two full days with no credible company voice countering it.
- Munoz stated no employee — including himself — would be fired, which read publicly as a lack of accountability even as he was apologizing.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** When there is video evidence circulating publicly, any internal or public statement must be checked against that video before it goes out — contradicting visual evidence in your own words is far more damaging than staying silent for a few extra hours to get the facts straight. The first statement after a viral incident should address what viewers actually saw, not a euphemism for it.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: United's market value dropped by roughly $1 billion at the Tuesday market open following the incident; stock partially recovered within the week.
- Reputational: Became one of the most widely referenced "PR disaster" case studies of the decade; "United's Image Takes Online Beating In Memes" became its own news cycle, extending the story's life well past the original incident.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms — highly relevant for social-media-driven virality cases. Strong example for clients of why an internal memo can leak and become the public story, and why euphemistic first statements backfire.

**Key Sources:**

- NBC News, "United CEO Issues New Apology For 'Horrific Event'"
- Patch, "United CEO Apologizes Again, Promises Changes"

## CASE-004

**Company:** Equifax
**Industry:** Credit Reporting / Financial Data
**Year:** 2017
**Crisis Type:** Data breach / cybersecurity failure

**Trigger Event:** Attackers exploited an unpatched Apache Struts vulnerability (CVE-2017-5638) — for which a patch had already been available for two months — to access Equifax's systems between May and July 2017, exposing personal data (names, SSNs, birth dates, addresses) of approximately 147 million people.

**Response Type:** Delayed, bordering on Denial-Silence during the internal gap period

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 — Equifax discovered the breach internally on July 29, 2017, but did not disclose it publicly until September 7, 2017 — a 40-day gap. During that window, three Equifax executives (including the CFO) sold a combined $1.8 million in company stock, which drew separate insider-trading scrutiny and made the delay look deliberate rather than merely cautious.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 — The 40-day silence, combined with executives trading stock during that window, is close to a textbook case of concealment even though no false statements were made publicly (because nothing was said at all). The company's own security team had ordered vulnerable systems patched within 48 hours of an internal alert months before the breach, but never followed up to confirm it was done — a process failure that became public during regulatory investigation.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FTC Act (unfair/deceptive practices), state data breach notification laws across all 50 states, CFPB oversight. Resulted in a global settlement with the FTC, CFPB, and 48 states/DC/Puerto Rico.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (financially/legally, via settlement) but the underlying trust damage to a company whose entire business model is "we are trustworthy stewards of your financial data" was severe and structural.

**What Went Right:**
- Once disclosed, Equifax did offer free credit monitoring and eventually funded a large consumer compensation pool.
- CEO Richard Smith stepped down (announced as "retirement") within about three weeks of public disclosure — September 26, 2017 — along with the CIO and CSO, showing some leadership accountability once the story broke.
- Agreed to a $1 billion, five-year commitment to overhaul information security practices as part of the settlement, addressing the root technical cause.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The 40-day gap between discovery and disclosure is the central failure of this case — it is the single most-cited data point in every breach-disclosure training material since.
- Executives selling stock during the non-disclosure window created a second, separate scandal layered on top of the breach itself, and directly damaged the credibility of "we didn't know how bad it was yet" as an excuse for the delay.
- The original vulnerability had a patch available for two months before the attack; an internal order to patch it was given but never verified as completed — a basic process failure, not a sophisticated attack.
- Total settlement reached $700 million, one of the largest data breach settlements in U.S. corporate history, and the stock dropped 35% in the week following disclosure, wiping out roughly $5 billion in market cap.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Disclosure should have happened within days, not 40 days — even a preliminary "we are investigating a potential incident affecting customer data" statement, issued quickly, would have prevented both the appearance of concealment and the insider-trading scandal that compounded it. Any company holding sensitive personal data needs a hard internal rule: no executive stock transactions once a potential breach is known internally, regardless of public disclosure timing. And technical fixes ordered internally need a verification step, not just an instruction — "we told someone to patch it" is not the same as "it was patched."

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Up to $700 million in settlement costs ($425M consumer fund, $175M to states, $100M CFPB penalty), plus a separate $1 billion security-overhaul commitment; roughly $5 billion in market cap lost within a week of disclosure.
- Reputational: Became the reference case for "delayed disclosure" in every subsequent data-breach crisis playbook; CEO's abrupt exit and the executive stock-sale scandal kept the story in the news cycle for months beyond the initial breach.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms / ORM — the single most relevant case in this batch for data-breach and disclosure-timing advice. Directly applicable to any client facing a data incident: the lesson is disclosure speed and internal financial-conduct controls during the silent window, not just the eventual apology.
