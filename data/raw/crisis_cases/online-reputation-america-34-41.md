## Case 34

**Company:** Fashion Nova, LLC
**Industry:** Online fashion retail (e-commerce)
**Year:** 2022 (conduct alleged from late 2015 to November 2019; FTC complaint and settlement announced 25 January 2022, final order 21 March 2022)
**Crisis Type:** Review suppression / misrepresented customer reviews (regulatory action)

**Trigger Event:** On 25 January 2022 the US Federal Trade Commission alleged that Fashion Nova misrepresented that the product reviews on its website reflected the views of all purchasers who submitted reviews, when it had suppressed reviews rated below four stars out of five. The company used a third-party review-management interface that automatically posted four- and five-star reviews and held lower-rated reviews for company approval. From late 2015 until November 2019 it allegedly never approved or posted "hundreds of thousands" of lower-rated reviews.

**Response Type:** Negotiated settlement (the FTC release carries no public statement from the company)

**Response Speed Score:** 3/5 - The suppression allegedly ran from late 2015 to November 2019 and was resolved through a settlement announced together with the complaint on 25 January 2022, then finalised on 21 March 2022 (4-0 Commission vote after one public comment). The company settled rather than litigating, but only after regulator action.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 - Shoppers saw star ratings and reviews presented as representative while lower-rated reviews were held back; the practice was built into the review software and was invisible to customers.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** FTC Act (deceptive practices), via an FTC administrative complaint and consent order. The FTC called it its first case involving a company's efforts to conceal negative customer reviews and sent letters to 10 companies offering review-management services warning that avoiding the collection or publication of negative reviews violates the FTC Act. The FTC's later final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (announced 14 August 2024, approved 5-0) bars, among other things, review suppression through unfounded legal threats, physical threats, intimidation or false public accusations, and misrepresenting that suppressed reviews are all or most of the reviews, and lets the FTC seek civil penalties against knowing violators.

**Resolution Status:** Resolved - Fashion Nova agreed to pay $4.2 million and was barred from misrepresenting customer reviews or other endorsements. It must post all customer reviews of products currently being sold, with exceptions for obscene, sexually explicit, racist or unlawful content and reviews unrelated to the product or to services such as shipping or returns. On 28 January 2025 the FTC sent nearly $2.4 million in refunds to 148,351 customers who filed valid claims.

**What Went Right:**
- Settled with the regulator on a clear conduct remedy: publish all reviews, with narrow, stated exceptions
- Consumer redress was actually paid out (nearly $2.4 million to 148,351 claimants by January 2025)
- The case set a bright line the whole industry could follow: holding back low-star reviews is deceptive

**What Went Wrong:**
- A rating filter hid hundreds of thousands of lower-rated reviews for about four years, so the displayed ratings were not representative
- The rule "only publish 4 and 5 stars" lived inside review-management tooling, so it ran automatically with no human question asked about it
- The brand became the named example in the FTC's first review-concealment case, tying its name to "blocked negative reviews" in news coverage

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Decide review moderation by stated rule, not by rating: remove reviews only for defined reasons (abusive, unlawful, unrelated to the product or service) and apply the same rule to every review. Audit what default filters any review-management tool applies before turning it on, and never present a curated set of reviews as all of them. When a platform or regulator raises the issue, restoring the held-back reviews is the first fix to make.

**Estimated Impact:** $4.2 million settlement and a permanent publish-all-reviews obligation; FTC-led refund programme (claims programme in 2023, payments January 2025); named in the FTC's first review-concealment case, which also put every review-management vendor and retailer on notice.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Review Management (shows that filtering out negative reviews is itself a legal and reputational risk, which matters when advising clients on review "curation")

## Case 35

**Company:** Amazon (lawsuit against operators of Facebook groups brokering fake reviews)
**Industry:** E-commerce marketplace
**Year:** 2022 (lawsuit announced 19 July 2022)
**Crisis Type:** Paid fake-review brokerage on a marketplace (platform litigation)

**Trigger Event:** On 19 July 2022 Amazon announced a lawsuit, filed in a Washington state court, against the administrators of more than 11,000 Facebook groups that it said recruited people to write fake or misleading product reviews. The groups' fake reviews allegedly appeared on Amazon in seven countries (France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, the UK and the US), for products ranging from car stereos to camera tripods, and recruits were reportedly paid in cash or given free merchandise.

**Response Type:** Proactive (platform-initiated litigation plus reports to Meta)

**Response Speed Score:** 3/5 - Meta said that since 2020 it had removed more than 5,000 fake-review groups based on Amazon's reports, and one removed group had more than 43,000 members, so Amazon had been reporting for a while; the lawsuit in July 2022 escalated from takedown requests to identifying and suing the operators.

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 - Amazon publicly announced the suit, the scale and its aims (identify the administrators, shut the groups down, recover profits from brokering fake reviews), and Meta gave an on-record statement.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Civil suit in Washington state court seeking a cease-and-desist order, damages and attorneys' fees. The FTC's 2024 final rule on consumer reviews later made buying and selling fake reviews an enforceable violation with civil penalties for knowing violators.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved - the suit targeted unnamed administrators in order to identify them; the available coverage reports no final outcome. Meta said groups soliciting fake reviews violate its policies and are removed.

**What Went Right:**
- Went after the brokers (the organisers who sell reviews), not only individual reviewers or sellers
- Combined a legal action with platform enforcement: Meta removed groups reported by Amazon
- Stated the aim publicly: identify bad actors and remove fake reviews they commissioned

**What Went Wrong:**
- The scheme operated openly in tens of thousands of social groups across seven countries before the suit
- Group takedowns on their own targeted the groups, not the people running them, which is why Amazon went to court to identify the administrators
- Fake reviews had been posted across seven national Amazon sites, for ordinary consumer products, before the suit

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Fake-review supply chains are organised, so the response has to target the organisers and the places they recruit, not only the reviews themselves. Brands should document suspicious review patterns (timing bursts, incentive offers in outside groups) and report the recruiting groups to the host platform as well as the reviews to the marketplace, and should never buy reviews from a broker: buyers are part of the same problem the regulator is pursuing.

**Estimated Impact:** A suit against the administrators of more than 11,000 Facebook groups across seven countries; it signals that platforms will take brokers to court, not only delete reviews.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (useful when a client suspects a competitor of buying fake reviews, or when a client is tempted to buy them)

**Key Sources:**
- CNN Business via KVIA, "Amazon sues administrators of more than 11,000 Facebook groups that allegedly brokered fake online reviews" (19 July 2022) - https://kvia.com/news/business-technology/cnn-social-media-technology/2022/07/19/amazon-sues-administrators-of-more-than-11000-facebook-groups-that-allegedly-brokered-fake-online-reviews/
- FTC press release, "Federal Trade Commission Announces Final Rule Banning Fake Reviews and Testimonials" (14 August 2024)

## Case 36

**Company:** Google (lawsuit against a seller of fake Business Profiles and fake reviews)
**Industry:** Online search and maps platform
**Year:** 2023 (announced 16 June 2023)
**Crisis Type:** Fake business listings and purchased reviews (platform litigation)

**Trigger Event:** On 16 June 2023 Google announced a consumer-protection lawsuit against a bad actor it said had created more than 350 fraudulent Business Profiles on Google and used more than 14,000 fake reviews to bolster them. The defendant allegedly sold information about the consumers who had been lured in by the false claims.

**Response Type:** Proactive (detected and removed content, then sued)

**Response Speed Score:** 4/5 - Google said it detected and removed the content and then filed suit; the post cites a year of broader prevention (2022: more than 185,000 businesses protected from further abuse and 20 million attempts to create fake Business Profiles stopped).

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 - Google published the numbers and linked the court filing, but the post does not name the defendant or the specific relief sought, and gives no business-facing advice.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Consumer-protection lawsuit by Google. The post cites an FTC survey in which consumers reported wasting $125 on average each year because of inaccurate reviews.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved - the lawsuit was announced; the source gives no outcome.

**What Went Right:**
- Google removed the fake listings and reviews and then pursued the seller in court instead of only deleting content
- Published concrete scale numbers (350+ profiles, 14,000+ fake reviews)
- Said the suit aims to stop the bad actor from scamming other platforms and to protect users

**What Went Wrong:**
- More than 350 fake profiles propped up by 14,000+ fake reviews existed long enough to attract real consumers
- The defendant allegedly sold information about the consumers who were lured in by the false claims

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Treat fake listings as an ongoing operation, not a single removal: monitor the map and search results for your business name, categories and phone number, report impersonating listings through the platform's reporting tools with evidence, and keep records (screenshots, dates, numbers) so reports and any later legal steps are easy to support. A pattern of mass five-star reviews on a new profile is a signal of the same scheme.

**Estimated Impact:** Targeted 350+ fake profiles and 14,000+ fake reviews; part of a broader enforcement push (Google says it stopped 20 million fake-profile creation attempts in 2022).

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (listing impersonation and bought reviews are among the most common local-business reputation problems)

**Key Sources:**
- Google, "Google takes legal action to stop fake review scams" (16 June 2023) - https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/public-policy/legal-action-stop-fake-review-scams/

## Case 37

**Company:** Google (lawsuit against a network behind about 10,000 fake or hijacked Maps listings)
**Industry:** Online search and maps platform / local services (locksmith, towing)
**Year:** 2025 (announced 19 March 2025)
**Crisis Type:** Business Profile impersonation and hijacking (platform litigation)

**Trigger Event:** A Texas business alerted Google to an unlicensed locksmith impersonating it on Google Maps. Google's investigation then found and removed more than 10,000 illegitimate listings, including fake businesses and legitimate accounts that had been hacked or hijacked, many in "duress verticals" such as locksmiths and towing. Some customers who called a real company were intercepted and routed through a lead-generation service to the scammers, and the network allegedly sold fraudulent positive reviews to bury genuine negative ones.

**Response Type:** Proactive (investigated, removed listings, then sued)

**Response Speed Score:** 4/5 - Google reacted to a single business's alert by investigating similar listings with automated tools and human analysts, removing more than 10,000 listings, and filing suit; it reported about 12 million fake business profiles removed or blocked in 2023, about a million more than the year before.

**Transparency Score:** 3/5 - Google explained the scam mechanics and numbers to the press and said any damages would be donated to groups fighting scams, but the coverage does not state the court or the relief sought.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Civil lawsuit brought by Google against the network (the coverage does not state the court or the relief sought).

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved - lawsuit announced on 19 March 2025; no outcome in the source.

**What Went Right:**
- One business's report triggered a platform-wide sweep that removed thousands of listings
- Combined automated detection with human analysts
- Public explanation of the tactic helps businesses and customers recognise it

**What Went Wrong:**
- Legitimate businesses' customers were rerouted to scammers by intercepted calls and impersonating listings
- Scammers sold fake positive reviews to hide genuine complaints about them
- Customers searching for urgent services ended up with a fake listing and were then quoted far higher prices

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Check how your business appears on the map regularly (name, phone number, website, address, category) and report an impersonating or hijacked listing to the platform as soon as it is seen, with evidence. Tell customers the official phone number and website through your own channels, so a wrong number is easy to spot. In urgent-service categories (locksmith, towing, repair) impersonation is a known pattern, so monitoring should be more frequent.

**Estimated Impact:** More than 10,000 listings removed; scale context of about 12 million fake business profiles removed or blocked by Google in 2023.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (direct precedent for listing-hijack and impersonating-listing cases)

**Key Sources:**
- CBS News, "Google finds 10,000 fake listings on Google Maps, sues alleged network of scammers" (19 March 2025) - https://www.cbsnews.com/news/google-maps-fake-listings-lawsuit-scams/

## Case 38

**Company:** Memories Pizza (Walkerton, Indiana)
**Industry:** Local restaurant (family-owned)
**Year:** 2015 (31 March to early April 2015)
**Crisis Type:** Social-media pile-on and review-bombing of a small business

**Trigger Event:** On Tuesday 31 March 2015 the owners told a local TV station (ABC 57) that, under Indiana's Religious Freedom Restoration Act, they would not cater a same-sex wedding, although they said they would not deny service to same-sex couples in the restaurant. Within hours hundreds of people flooded the restaurant's Yelp page with one-star ratings and negative comments (some with irrelevant photos), thousands of negative reviews were posted in less than a day, and a fake website using the company's name was created. The family said it received death threats and police said they were investigating several threats.

**Response Type:** Reactive, then defended by supporters (owners withdrew from public view and closed the shop temporarily)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 - The owners answered a local TV station's question on a hot-button topic and the reaction was immediate; by Wednesday the shop was closed and a co-owner said the family was "in hiding", while a supporter-run crowdfunding drive moved faster than the business itself.

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 - The owners were open about their position when asked; the ensuing platform response was also public (Yelp explained that reviews must describe a firsthand consumer experience).

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Platform content guidelines (Yelp reviews must describe a firsthand experience; Yelp removed 2,870 posts by Wednesday per its spokeswoman); police investigation of threats, with at least one case forwarded to prosecutors; no formal regulator action in the sources.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved - Yelp removed posts and the threats were investigated; a GoFundMe campaign set up by a supporter raised more than $32,000 in four hours, passed $380,000 by Thursday and was stopped on Friday evening at $842,387. The sources describe the restaurant as closed on Wednesday; they do not say how the business fared long term.

**What Went Right:**
- Yelp responded quickly to the coordinated flood: its guidelines bar "non-germane, media-fueled" reviews, and 2,870 posts had been removed by Wednesday
- Reports of threats were taken seriously by police
- A supporter-driven fundraiser showed the same news cycle can drive support as well as attacks

**What Went Wrong:**
- An owner's answer to a local TV question on a contested political topic reached a national audience within a day
- Thousands of reviews from people who had never been customers distorted the listing within hours
- A fake website using the company's name appeared, adding to the confusion
- Threats against the family and the temporary closure followed

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Small businesses should decide in advance who speaks for the business on contested topics and that statements to media can move the business's online reputation within hours. When a pile-on hits a review page, save evidence (screenshots, timestamps), report the reviews as not based on a firsthand experience (the platform's own rule) and ask the platform to put the page under moderation, secure the brand's accounts and domain against impersonation, and report threats to the police. Do not argue in the reviews.

**Estimated Impact:** Thousands of negative reviews in under a day; 2,870 posts removed by Yelp within days; temporary closure; support campaign raised $842,387.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown (small-business review-bombing: platform reporting, evidence capture, impersonation sites and threats)

**Key Sources:**
- NBC News, "Online Reviewers Lambaste Indiana Pizzeria Supporting RFRA" (1 April 2015) - https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/online-reviewers-lambaste-indiana-pizzeria-supporting-rfra-n334301
- BuzzFeed News, "Indiana Pizzeria Closed, Owners 'In Hiding' After Saying They Won't Cater LGBT Weddings" (April 2015) - https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/maryanngeorgantopoulos/indiana-pizzeria-owners-say-theyd-deny-lgbt-people-service-a

## Case 39

**Company:** Amy's Baking Company (Scottsdale, Arizona)
**Industry:** Restaurant / bakery
**Year:** 2013 (episode aired 10 May 2013)
**Crisis Type:** Owner's hostile responses to online criticism (self-inflicted escalation)

**Trigger Event:** After the restaurant appeared on the TV show Kitchen Nightmares, viewers flooded its Facebook page, left almost 1,000 negative Yelp reviews and started Reddit threads. The owners posted angry replies on Facebook, reportedly calling commenters "crazy" and "nobodies", and one owner threatened legal action against critics. Within days the owners said their Facebook, Yelp, Twitter and website accounts had "been hacked"; the article notes few believed it.

**Response Type:** Reactive, hostile, followed by a denial that the posts were theirs

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 - The first replies were immediate but escalatory; within three days of the broadcast the situation had escalated, and the hacking claim a few days later came after the damage was done.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5 - The owners first attacked critics, then said the accounts were hacked, saying they were working with local authorities and the FBI; the sources report that few believed the claim.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** None identified in the sources beyond the owners' threat of legal action against critics and the claim of a hack reported to authorities.

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved - the sources describe the escalation but not a recovery.

**What Went Right:**
- Nothing in the owners' replies helped; the one positive was reach: before the episode the page had a few hundred Facebook likes and a few dozen mixed Yelp reviews, and by 16 May it had just under 75,000 fans, an attention spike a calm, constructive response could have used

**What Went Wrong:**
- The owners answered critics with insults and legal threats, which became the story
- Replies written in anger and in capital letters were screenshotted and shared on Reddit and elsewhere
- A later claim of hacking contradicted the earlier posts and damaged credibility further
- The restaurant had a thin review history, so a flood of one-star reviews dominated its profile

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Do not reply to hostile comments in anger; pause, and have a calm person write short factual replies or say nothing. The commentary on the case advises responding calmly and describing the specific steps being taken, admitting mistakes, avoiding repeated legal threats, and following basic social-media etiquette (complete sentences, no accusing critics of lying or crime). If an account really is compromised, say so at once and do not wait until posts have gone viral.

**Estimated Impact:** Almost 1,000 negative Yelp reviews; Facebook fans from a few hundred to just under 75,000 within about a week; sustained negative search and social visibility for the brand.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms (the classic "owner replies make it worse" case; useful when a small-business client wants to respond to critics personally)

**Key Sources:**
- NBC News, "How Amy's Baking Company ruined itself on social media" (May 2013) - https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna51911274

## Case 40

**Company:** Hassell Law Group (Hassell v. Bird)
**Industry:** Legal services; online review platform liability (Yelp)
**Year:** 2018 (reviews posted 2013; California Supreme Court decision 2 July 2018)
**Crisis Type:** Defamatory online review and the limits of court-ordered takedown

**Trigger Event:** In January 2013 a former client posted a one-star Yelp review claiming that a law firm had failed to contact her insurer and failed to communicate with her; a second one-star review under another name appeared in February 2013, which the firm suspected was written by the same person. The firm sued the reviewer for defamation but did not name Yelp, expecting it to invoke Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act. The reviewer did not appear, and on 14 January 2014 the trial court awarded the firm $557,918.75 and ordered the reviews removed, including by Yelp within 7 days.

**Response Type:** Legal (litigation against the reviewer, an order extended to the platform)

**Response Speed Score:** 2/5 - The reviews went up in early 2013; the trial judgment was in January 2014, a Court of Appeal ruling for the firm came in 2016, and the final answer from the California Supreme Court came on 2 July 2018, about five years after the first review.

**Transparency Score:** 4/5 - The proceedings were public court records, and the reasoning of each court is published.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Defamation law; Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (a platform is not treated as the publisher or speaker of users' content). The California Supreme Court held 4-3 that the order directed at Yelp was barred by Section 230. The US Supreme Court declined to hear the case on 22 January 2019.

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved - the order requiring the reviewer to remove the review and the monetary judgment against her were left in place, but Yelp could not be ordered to remove the review.

**What Went Right:**
- The firm obtained a judgment against the reviewer and an order requiring her to remove the reviews
- The Supreme Court left the order against the reviewer and the money judgment in place while protecting the platform

**What Went Wrong:**
- Five years of litigation for a pair of reviews that remained online during the process
- The attempt to reach the platform through an order against the author (a "procedural end-run" in the Chief Justice's words) failed
- The reviewer did not appear, so the case was won by default without a contested trial

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Defamation against the author is slow, and a court order against a reviewer does not automatically bind the platform. Before litigating, decide the goal: if it is to have the review removed from the platform, use the platform's own reporting and legal-removal process; if it is damages or an apology, litigate against the author. Lawyers should expect a platform that was not a party to resist, and should keep a record (screenshots, dates, any link to the reviewer) from day one.

**Estimated Impact:** $557,918.75 default judgment against the reviewer; precedent that a non-party platform cannot be ordered to take down user reviews on the strength of a judgment against the author.

**Onlyne Relevance:** Legal Takedown / ORM (sets expectations for clients who want a court to order a platform to remove a defamatory review)

**Key Sources:**
- Electronic Frontier Foundation, post on the California Supreme Court decision in Hassell v. Bird (24 July 2018) - https://www.eff.org/node/99170
- Wikipedia, "Hassell v. Bird" (summary of judgment dates, damages and vote) - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassell_v._Bird

## Case 41

**Company:** Barbra Streisand (the origin of the "Streisand effect")
**Industry:** Entertainment / personal reputation and privacy
**Year:** 2003 (lawsuit); term coined 5 January 2005
**Crisis Type:** Takedown attempt that amplified the content it targeted

**Trigger Event:** In 2003 Barbra Streisand sued a photographer and a website for $50 million on privacy grounds over an aerial photograph (Image 3850, "Streisand Estate, Malibu") in the California Coastal Records Project, an archive of about 12,000 California coastline photographs. Before the lawsuit the image had been downloaded six times, two of them by Streisand's attorneys. During the following month the site had more than 420,000 visits.

**Response Type:** Reactive (litigation to suppress the image)

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5 - The lawsuit was filed immediately but it was the wrong tool for the goal: it brought the image to public attention.

**Transparency Score:** 2/5 - The legal action was public by nature, but the goal (reducing exposure) was the opposite of its result.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:** Privacy lawsuit; the case was dismissed and Streisand was ordered to pay $177,000 in attorney fees (dismissal reported in December 2003, fee order dated 28 May 2004).

**Resolution Status:** Resolved against the plaintiff - the lawsuit was dismissed and she was ordered to pay the photographer's legal fees. The term "Streisand effect" was coined by Mike Masnick of Techdirt on 5 January 2005 for the phenomenon.

**What Went Right:**
- The case is now a widely understood warning, which makes it easy to explain to clients

**What Went Wrong:**
- A privacy lawsuit turned a six-download image into a 420,000-visit story within a month
- The case lost, and the fee order added cost on top of the publicity
- The coverage tied the person's name to the dispute for years

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:** Before demanding removal of content, estimate whether the demand itself will become the story, especially when the content is lawful, low-visibility and the person is well known. When removal is justified (privacy, defamation, impersonation), use the quiet routes first (a polite request to the site owner, the platform's reporting process) and keep legal letters for cases that can be won and where the attention is worth the risk; consider whether pushing the content down or out of search is a better answer than a public fight.

**Estimated Impact:** About six downloads before the lawsuit, more than 420,000 visits in a month after; $177,000 in fees; the incident gave the internet a name for the effect.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Search Results Management (the standard cautionary example when a client wants an aggressive takedown)

**Key Sources:**
- Wikipedia, "Streisand effect" - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streisand_effect
