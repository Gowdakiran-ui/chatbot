## CASE 13

**Company:** Videocon Group / Venugopal Dhoot (with ICICI Bank / Chanda Kochhar)
**Industry:** Consumer Electronics & Diversified Conglomerate (crisis is a Banking-Corporate Governance crossover)
**Year:** 2012 (loan sanctioned) → 2016 (whistleblower complaint) → 2018 (ICICI internal probe) → Dec 2022 (CBI arrests) → 2023 (chargesheet, bail)
**Crisis Type:** Quid pro quo loan fraud / conflict of interest / corporate governance failure

**Trigger Event:**
ICICI Bank sanctioned Rs 300 crore (part of a larger Rs 1,875–3,250 crore credit facility across six Videocon Group companies between 2009–2012) to Videocon, approved by a committee that included then-CEO Chanda Kochhar. Within roughly a day of the loan disbursal, Rs 64 crore was routed from a Dhoot-controlled entity into NuPower Renewables, a company linked to Kochhar's husband Deepak Kochhar — a transaction the CBI later called quid pro quo.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence (2016–2018), then Delayed-Reactive (ICICI, 2018), then Reactive (Videocon/Dhoot, 2018 onward)

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
Anonymous whistleblower complaints against Kochhar surfaced as early as 2016. ICICI Bank's board publicly backed Kochhar and dismissed concerns for nearly two years. It was only after fresh allegations in March–May 2018 (and mounting media and regulatory pressure) that ICICI announced an independent probe led by Justice B.N. Srikrishna. Videocon and Dhoot made no proactive public statement addressing the allegations at any point; their public position was reactive, largely confined to court filings and post-arrest bail submissions.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5
ICICI initially issued a strong statement of confidence in Kochhar and her "highest standards of governance and ethics" even as red flags were known internally. Full facts only emerged through investigative journalism (an anonymous whistleblower letter published via Indian media/an investor), not company disclosure. Videocon never issued a public accounting of the NuPower transaction; the group's version of events has come entirely through legal defense filings.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:**
- Banking Regulation Act violations (loan sanctioning process)
- RBI lending/credit policy norms
- SEBI show-cause notice to Kochhar (2018) for disclosure lapses
- CBI FIR (Jan 2019) under IPC Sections 420 (cheating), 120B (criminal conspiracy), 409 (criminal breach of trust by a public servant/agent), plus Prevention of Corruption Act provisions
- CBI chargesheet (April 2023) against Kochhar, Deepak Kochhar and Dhoot
- Enforcement Directorate money-laundering angle (PMLA)
- Bombay High Court subsequently granted bail to all three, ruling the December 2022 arrests were not compliant with law

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved
Criminal trial is ongoing as of the most recent public reporting; all three main accused are out on bail. Kochhar's reputation and ICICI's leadership were permanently altered — she resigned in October 2018 and ICICI later revoked her bonuses/stock options and reclassified her exit as "termination for cause." No final criminal conviction has been reported to date.

**What Went Right:**
- Once ICICI's board did act (2018), it commissioned an independent, retired-Supreme-Court-judge-led inquiry (Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee) rather than an internal-only review — this gave the eventual findings external credibility.
- ICICI Bank, after the Srikrishna report, took the rare step of converting Kochhar's resignation into "termination for cause" and clawed back bonuses/ESOPs — a concrete accountability action that most Indian corporates avoid.
- ICICI separated its own brand and going-concern reputation from the individual executive's alleged misconduct by acting decisively once findings were in — the bank's stock and depositor confidence recovered relatively quickly after the 2018 leadership transition.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Nearly two years of silence and public defense of Kochhar (2016–2018) despite specific, named whistleblower allegations — this is the single biggest reputational error in the case.
- No proactive disclosure control: the story was broken and sustained by financial journalists and an anonymous investor letter, not by any company statement.
- Videocon/Dhoot never engaged in public reputation management at all — total silence created a vacuum that was filled entirely by CBI/ED framing in media, meaning Dhoot's public narrative today is 100% prosecutorial.
- The related-party nature of the transaction (spouse of approving executive) was not flagged by any internal conflict-of-interest disclosure process at the time of sanctioning — a governance control failure, not just a comms failure.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
- Any conflict-of-interest linkage (spouse-controlled entity receiving funds proximate to a loan the executive approved) should trigger mandatory recusal and disclosure at the time of the transaction — not after a whistleblower forces the issue years later.
- On first credible whistleblower complaint (2016), ICICI should have commissioned an independent probe immediately rather than issuing a defensive statement of confidence — the two-year gap is what converted a containable governance question into a full-blown institutional credibility crisis.
- Videocon/Dhoot's counter-narrative vacuum shows why even an accused party under active investigation needs a minimal, lawyer-approved public position ("we deny wrongdoing, we are cooperating, the matter is sub judice") rather than total silence — silence is read by search engines, media and the public as an admission.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Loan exposure Rs 1,875–3,250 crore (loan classified NPA by 2017); ICICI clawed back bonus/stock compensation from Kochhar; ICICI's market cap saw significant short-term volatility around the 2018 resignation and the 2019 FIR news cycles.
- Reputational: Chanda Kochhar's public reputation — previously one of India's most celebrated woman CEOs — collapsed and has not recovered; ICICI Bank suffered a multi-year governance-credibility overhang used repeatedly by critics/competitors even after leadership change; Videocon Group's brand (already declining commercially) became permanently associated with the scandal in search results.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown angle (SERP dominated by CBI/ED reporting with no company-authored counter-content)

## CASE 14

**Company:** ABG Shipyard Ltd
**Industry:** Shipbuilding & Ship Repair
**Year:** 2013 (first NPA classification) → 2019 (SBI first complaint) → Feb 2022 (CBI FIR / arrests)
**Crisis Type:** Large-scale bank loan fraud (India's largest reported bank fraud at the time of the FIR)

**Trigger Event:**
ABG Shipyard, led by chairman Rishi Kamlesh Agarwal, defaulted on credit facilities extended by a 28-lender consortium (led by ICICI Bank and SBI). SBI filed its first fraud complaint with the CBI on 8 November 2019, alleging the company diverted and misused funds; the CBI registered a formal FIR only on 7 February 2022, more than two years later, covering Rs 22,842 crore — the largest bank fraud case in Indian history at the time.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence
ABG Shipyard and its directors did not issue any public statement or rebuttal when the CBI FIR was registered in February 2022. Media reports at the time explicitly noted "no response from ABG Shipyard Ltd."

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
No official company response was issued within days, weeks, or even months of the FIR. The only public-facing activity was regulatory/legal (CBI look-out circulars against directors, ED provisional attachment proceedings) — none of it company-originated communication.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5
The account was first classified NPA in November 2013 and again in July 2016 after a failed restructuring; a forensic audit by E&Y was commissioned only in 2018, and it took until 2022 for the fraud to be formally registered. There was no independent, company-commissioned investigation or public disclosure — all transparency came from bank/regulator/CBI processes, not from ABG itself.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:**
- CBI FIR under fraud/cheating provisions covering all 28 lender banks
- Enforcement Directorate money-laundering case (PMLA) registered 16 February 2022
- Look-out circulars issued against the accused (15 February 2022) to prevent flight risk, explicitly to avoid a repeat of the Nirav Modi/Mehul Choksi extradition situation
- Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code (IBC) — company referred to NCLT, Ahmedabad by ICICI Bank for Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)
- RBI fraud-classification and reporting norms for consortium lending

**Resolution Status:** Unresolved (criminal case ongoing; recovery for lenders remains partial)
The company itself is under insolvency/liquidation processes; the criminal case against Agarwal and other directors continues. Public commentary (including opposition political parties) has focused heavily on the multi-year delay between NPA classification (2013/2016) and FIR registration (2022) as a systemic failure, not just a corporate one.

**What Went Right:**
- From the banks' side: the finance ministry publicly stated that detection (52–54 months average vs. faster in this case) and forensic audit process (E&Y engaged in 2018) followed due process, and the case was pursued in parallel through both IBC (recovery) and CBI (criminal) tracks — a dual-track approach that is a genuine institutional best practice for lenders.
- SBI filed a second, more detailed complaint in August 2020 after the CBI sought clarifications on the first — showing lender-side persistence rather than dropping the matter.

**What Went Wrong:**
- Total company silence at every stage — no proactive statement, no rebuttal, no reputational defense whatsoever, even a purely legal "matter is sub judice" statement was absent from public record.
- A nearly decade-long gap between first signs of distress (2013 NPA) and criminal registration (2022) allowed the narrative to be shaped entirely by opposition politicians, financial journalists, and comparison pieces ("India's biggest bank fraud," explicitly benchmarked against Nirav Modi/PNB) — the company had zero voice in how its own story was told.
- No evidence of any crisis communication, stakeholder notification, or even a basic holding statement to employees, vendors, or the Gujarat shipbuilding ecosystem the company operated in.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
- Even a company under criminal investigation for fraud should issue a minimal factual holding statement distinguishing individual-director liability from company/employee/vendor standing, particularly for a business (shipbuilding) with long-tail contractual and employment relationships.
- For lenders/regulators: the multi-year gap between NPA classification and fraud registration is the actual root-cause lesson here — for any ORM/reputation advisory context, this shows why "the trigger event" as the public sees it (the 2022 FIR) is often years removed from the actual point of failure (2013–2016), and a client's reputation risk timeline should track both dates separately.
- A company facing this scale of allegation should get ahead of the "biggest fraud in Indian history" framing early — once that comparison anchor (vs. PNB/Nirav Modi, vs. Vijay Mallya) is set by media in the first 48 hours, it is extremely difficult to dislodge from search results.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: Rs 22,842 crore in alleged fraud across 28 banks (13 public sector banks, 3 international subsidiaries of PSU banks, 3 government-owned entities) — the largest such figure in Indian banking history at time of filing.
- Reputational: ABG Shipyard's name is now a permanent reference point/comparison benchmark in Indian financial media for "biggest bank fraud," appearing in virtually every subsequent major fraud story; zero company-controlled content exists to counterbalance this in search results.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (complete absence of company voice — a textbook "SERP owned entirely by adverse parties" case)

**Key Sources:**

## CASE 15

**Company:** Bhushan Steel Ltd (BSL) and Bhushan Power & Steel Ltd (BPSL) — related but legally separate companies, same promoter family (Singal/Singhal)
**Industry:** Steel Manufacturing
**Year:** 2014 (NPA/insolvency onset) → 2017 (NCLT admission, RBI's "first list of 12" defaulters) → 2018 (Tata Steel acquires BSL) → 2019 (JSW Steel wins BPSL bid) → 2019–2021 (SFIO arrest, ED attachment) → 2025 (Supreme Court cancels JSW's BPSL acquisition)
**Crisis Type:** Bank loan fraud / diversion of funds / insolvency (two parallel, related cases)

**Trigger Event:**
SFIO investigation found that promoters Brij Bhushan Singal and Neeraj Singal siphoned funds via inflated "stock-in-transit" declarations to avail excess drawing power against cash credit facilities, and misused letters of credit using fabricated documentation, to fraudulently draw down approximately Rs 45,800 crore in bank loans between 2013/14 and 2016/17 across BSL; BPSL separately owed over Rs 47,000 crore and was found to involve an alleged fraud of roughly $453 million uncovered by Punjab National Bank.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence (promoters), Reactive (through NCLT/legal process only)
Neither company issued proactive public disclosure of the fund diversion; the facts emerged through SFIO's forensic investigation and bank-initiated NCLT insolvency filings, not company disclosure.

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
No timely official response. The promoters' public engagement was limited to court filings challenging insolvency eligibility (e.g., Neeraj Singal contesting Tata Steel's eligibility under IBC Section 29A) rather than addressing the underlying fraud allegations.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5
Full financial diversion mechanics only became public via the SFIO's formal investigation report, not company disclosure. Bhushan Steel's stock collapse (from ₹400 in August 2014 to ₹43.70 by September 2015) reflected market-driven price discovery of undisclosed problems rather than any company-led transparency process.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:**
- Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 — both companies were among RBI's first list of 12 large NPA accounts referred for mandatory NCLT resolution
- Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) probe and arrest of Neeraj Singhal (first-ever individual arrest by SFIO), on charges of siphoning over Rs 2,000 crore
- CBI investigation revealing bribery (Rs 10 lakh bribe to a Syndicate Bank chairman to secure a credit extension despite default)
- Enforcement Directorate asset attachment (PMLA) — Rs 4,025 crore of BPSL assets attached in October 2019
- Section 29A of IBC (bars defaulting promoters from bidding to reacquire their own insolvent companies) — actively litigated in both cases
- Supreme Court of India ultimately overturned JSW Steel's completed 2019 acquisition of BPSL in 2025, ordering liquidation and citing failures by the resolution professional and committee of creditors to follow legal process

**Resolution Status:** Partially Resolved (BSL) / Unresolved-reopened (BPSL)
BSL: Resolved via Tata Steel's 2018 acquisition (₹35,200 crore), later merged into Tata Steel in 2019 — the first of RBI's 12 large defaulter accounts to be resolved under IBC.
BPSL: What appeared resolved in 2019 (JSW Steel's ₹19,700 crore NCLT-approved acquisition) was reopened in 2025 when the Supreme Court cancelled the acquisition and ordered liquidation — six years after the deal closed, creating fresh uncertainty for lenders, JSW Steel, and thousands of employees.

**What Went Right:**
- The IBC process itself worked as designed for BSL: a resolution professional (Deloitte's Vijaykumar Iyer) ran a competitive, multi-bidder process (Tata Steel vs. JSW Steel vs. Liberty House) that delivered one of the fastest and cleanest resolutions among RBI's first list of 12 defaulters.
- SFIO's arrest of Neeraj Singhal signaled that individual accountability (not just corporate/asset resolution) was being pursued — a meaningful departure from the more common pattern of promoters remaining insulated from personal consequences.
- Acquiring companies (Tata Steel, JSW Steel) each issued clear market disclosures at each bid stage via stock exchange filings, which — while a lender/acquirer practice rather than the defaulting promoter's — meant the market was not left entirely uninformed.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The 2025 Supreme Court reversal of the BPSL/JSW deal shows that "resolution" under IBC is not always final — the committee of creditors and resolution professional did not adhere to legal process, meaning a six-year-old, seemingly closed transaction was unwound, re-injecting uncertainty into a case many considered a settled success story.
- Promoters never publicly accounted for the fund diversion; their only public posture was procedural (challenging bidder eligibility), never substantive engagement with the fraud allegations.
- The bribery revelations (Rs 10 lakh to a bank chairman) surfaced only through CBI investigation, indicating no internal controls or self-reporting existed even after the company was already under financial stress.

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
- For lenders/resolution professionals: strict, auditable adherence to IBC process requirements is not optional even when a resolution appears commercially sound — the 2025 Supreme Court reversal is a direct lesson that procedural shortcuts in insolvency resolution create multi-year reputational and financial risk for acquirers, not just original promoters.
- For any company under SFIO/forensic audit scrutiny: proactive, documented cooperation and voluntary disclosure (rather than only contesting bidder eligibility in court) would have differentiated individual promoter conduct from the operating company's ongoing business reputation — critical for protecting employees, brand, and vendor relationships during a lengthy resolution process.
- For acquirers (a lesson directly relevant for M&A-stage reputational advisory): completed, NCLT-approved acquisitions should still be treated as reputationally "live" risk until all appellate/Supreme Court challenges are exhausted — six years is not necessarily enough to consider such deals final.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: BSL debt ~₹44,000 crore, resolved via Tata Steel's ₹35,200 crore acquisition; BPSL debt exceeded ₹47,000 crore, JSW Steel's ₹19,700 crore (later ₹19,350–19,700 crore reported) bid overturned by Supreme Court in 2025; SFIO estimated promoter fund siphoning at ₹45,800 crore (BSL) and roughly $453 million fraud (BPSL, PNB estimate).
- Reputational: The Singal/Singhal family lost an estimated ₹55,000 crore in wealth and both brands ceased to exist independently (absorbed into Tata Steel BSL and JSW BPSL); the 2025 Supreme Court reversal also created fresh reputational exposure for JSW Steel, whose stock fell nearly 7% on the news — demonstrating that reputational risk from a legacy fraud case can resurface years later and affect an unrelated acquiring company.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Legal Takedown / Crisis Comms (multi-party case with resurfacing risk — relevant for any client involved in distressed-asset M&A)

## CASE 16

**Company:** Ranbaxy Laboratories Ltd (later acquired by Daiichi Sankyo, Japan; assets eventually absorbed into Sun Pharma)
**Industry:** Pharmaceuticals (Generic Drug Manufacturing)
**Year:** 2004–2005 (internal fraud begins surfacing) → 2008 (FDA warning letters/import bans) → May 2013 (guilty plea, $500M settlement) → 2013–2014 (further plant bans) → ongoing Daiichi-Singal litigation
**Crisis Type:** Regulatory fraud / product safety / data fabrication (with M&A/shareholder-disclosure fallout)

**Trigger Event:**
Dinesh Thakur, then Director of Project and Information Management at Ranbaxy, and colleague Rajinder Kumar discovered in 2004–2005 that the company was systematically fabricating drug testing data and violating current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) and Good Laboratory Practices (cGLP) at its Paonta Sahib and Dewas plants, submitting falsified bioequivalence data to the FDA and other global regulators to secure drug approvals.

**Response Type:** Denial-Silence (2005–2008), then Delayed-Reactive (2008–2013), then formal Reactive resolution (2013 guilty plea)
When US federal officials conducted a surprise search of Ranbaxy's Princeton, NJ offices in February 2007, the company publicly stated it did not know why the search occurred and that it "came as a surprise" — despite the FDA having already received Thakur's internal report in 2005.

**Response Speed Score:** 1/5
Nearly eight years elapsed between the whistleblower's internal discovery (2004–2005) and the final guilty plea/settlement (May 2013). Even the narrower window — from the 2007 DOJ/FDA raid to eventual resolution — spans six years. There was no early, accurate acknowledgment at any point; the company's 2007 public statement claiming surprise was itself misleading given its prior knowledge of the internal report.

**Transparency Score:** 1/5
Ranbaxy's public statements throughout the 2005–2013 period consistently minimized or denied the scale of the problem. Even at settlement in 2013, the company's messaging framed the fine as "resolution of past issues," while continuing to state its products were safe — a claim regulators and subsequent inspections (2013–2014 discoveries of human hair in tablets, oil spots, non-functioning toilet facilities at plants) directly contradicted.

**Legal / Regulatory Framework Triggered:**
- US False Claims Act (qui tam whistleblower lawsuit, filed by Thakur in the District of Maryland, 2007)
- US FDA warning letters (2006, 2008) and import bans on Paonta Sahib and Dewas facilities
- US DOJ criminal prosecution — Ranbaxy pleaded guilty to 3 felony counts of violating federal drug safety law (cGMP) and 4 counts of making false statements to the FDA
- $500 million global settlement (May 2013): ~$231.8M federal share, ~$118.2M to US states, remainder criminal fine — at the time, the largest false-claims/drug-safety settlement ever reached with a generic drug manufacturer
- Subsequent FDA import alerts on the Mohali and Toansa plants (2013–2014) for continued cGMP violations
- Singapore arbitration tribunal ruling (2016, 2–1 majority) that Ranbaxy's former promoters (the Singh brothers) concealed material information from Daiichi Sankyo during the 2008 acquisition — enforced via Delhi High Court
- SEBI/shareholder-disclosure questions around whether the DOJ investigation was adequately disclosed to Daiichi Sankyo pre-acquisition

**Resolution Status:** Resolved (criminal/regulatory) but Fatal for the business as an independent entity
The $500 million settlement formally closed the US criminal and civil case. However, the brand did not survive as an independent company — continued plant bans, contamination discoveries (glass particles in Atorvastatin/generic Lipitor, November 2012), and eroding trust led Daiichi Sankyo to eventually exit by selling Ranbaxy to Sun Pharma in 2014–2015, at which point the Ranbaxy brand was retired.

**What Went Right:**
- Ranbaxy did eventually cooperate with the DOJ to reach a negotiated guilty plea rather than prolonging litigation indefinitely — this closed the primary US legal exposure in a single, defined settlement rather than years of piecemeal litigation.
- The company did execute functional recalls when contamination was identified (e.g., Atorvastatin glass-particle recall, November 2012; production resumed only after remediation in February) — an operationally correct, if reputationally damaging, action.
- Under Daiichi Sankyo's ownership, the company at least engaged with a formal consent decree (2011) laying out remediation steps for its manufacturing facilities, providing an external, auditable compliance framework going forward.

**What Went Wrong:**
- The core failure was an eight-year pattern of denial: from the 2005 internal whistleblower report to the 2013 guilty plea, every public company statement understated or denied the problem, including the explicitly misleading "came as a surprise" statement after the 2007 raid.
- Data fabrication was not a one-time lapse but systemic and multi-site (Paonta Sahib, Dewas, later Mohali, Toansa) and multi-year, indicating no functioning internal quality/compliance escalation path existed — or that escalations were actively suppressed.
- The acquirer (Daiichi Sankyo) itself was reputationally damaged by inadequate pre-acquisition disclosure — the 2016 arbitration finding that former promoters concealed the DOJ investigation from Daiichi is a direct lesson in M&A due-diligence failure, not just target-company misconduct.
- Post-settlement quality failures continued (human hair in tablets, oil spots, hygiene failures found in 2013 inspections) — undermining the credibility of the company's own claim that "the problems have now been resolved."

**Best Practice / What Should Have Happened:**
- Any credible internal whistleblower report alleging data fabrication in a regulated (FDA) product category should trigger an immediate, independent forensic audit and voluntary regulatory disclosure — not an eight-year adversarial posture that only ends when the government forces a guilty plea.
- For any client undergoing M&A in a regulated sector: a target company's regulatory history (open investigations, warning letters, internal whistleblower reports) must be assumed to be a live disclosure obligation — Daiichi Sankyo's experience is the clearest available Indian case study for why acquirers need independent regulatory-history diligence, not reliance on seller representations.
- Public statements during an active federal investigation should never claim ignorance ("came as a surprise") if the company has any internal record indicating otherwise — this is the single most reputation-destroying category of statement in a regulatory crisis, because it converts a compliance failure into a credibility/integrity failure once contradicted by later disclosure.

**Estimated Impact:**
- Financial: $500 million global settlement (2013) — at the time the largest drug-safety settlement with a generic manufacturer in US history; Daiichi Sankyo separately was awarded $525 million by an arbitration tribunal against former Ranbaxy promoters (the Singh brothers) for non-disclosure during the acquisition; Thakur received ~$48.6 million as whistleblower award.
- Reputational: Ranbaxy — once India's largest pharmaceutical exporter and a symbol of Indian generics' global reach — ceased to exist as an independent brand within roughly two years of the settlement, fully absorbed into Sun Pharma; the case remains the definitive global reference point for data-integrity failures in Indian pharmaceutical manufacturing, cited in virtually every subsequent India-pharma quality story.

**Onlyne Relevance:** ORM / Crisis Comms / Legal Takedown (a rare case where the crisis outlived and ultimately destroyed the corporate brand entirely — critical cautionary case for any pharma/regulated-industry client)

## Cross-Case Pattern Summary (for Onlyne internal use)

| Case | Response Speed | Transparency | Time from First Signal to Public Reckoning | Fatal for Brand? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 13. Videocon/ICICI | 1/5 | 1/5 | ~2 years (2016 whistleblower → 2018 probe) | Fatal for Kochhar personally; ICICI survived |
| 14. ABG Shipyard | 1/5 | 1/5 | ~9 years (2013 NPA → 2022 FIR) | Fatal (company under liquidation) |
| 15. Bhushan Steel/Power | 1/5 | 1/5 | ~3–4 years (2014 distress → 2018/19 SFIO arrest) | Fatal as independent brand; absorbed into Tata Steel/JSW |
| 16. Ranbaxy | 1/5 | 1/5 | ~8 years (2005 whistleblower → 2013 settlement) | Fatal (brand retired after Sun Pharma sale) |

**Recurring failure pattern across all four cases:** In every one of these, the company had specific, credible internal knowledge of the underlying problem years before any public reckoning, and in every case the public narrative was ultimately written entirely by regulators, investigative journalists, or opposition politicians — not by the company. None of the four produced a single proactive company-authored disclosure that got ahead of the story. This is the core, repeatable lesson for Onlyne's ORM practice: in large-scale Indian financial/regulatory fraud cases, the "silence is safer" instinct is empirically the opposite of true — silence simply cedes 100% of narrative control to adversarial sources for the entire multi-year duration of the case, and that adversarial framing becomes permanently embedded in search results.
